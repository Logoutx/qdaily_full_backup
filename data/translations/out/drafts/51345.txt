I often use an analogy: modernization is like a lone island. So far, only about 30 countries have swum ashore. More than 100 others are still swimming toward it.

More than 10 years ago, Tang Shiping told a friend that he would become one of the most important social scientists of this century. At the time, the friend thought he was joking. But to this day, Tang Shiping insists he has "never thought otherwise," and that this goal has not wavered in the slightest — if anything, he is closer to it. At the very least, he is now regarded by some as one of the most internationally influential Chinese social scientists of our time, and one of the finest social scientists in Asia today.

The 51-year-old Tang Shiping is a rare "madman" in Chinese academia. He calls himself "half a god," and says he "only compares himself to people like Karl Popper." His home field is international relations, but in recent years he has gradually "faded out" of the IR discipline; his present and future research will "focus essentially on: comparative politics (modernization, ethnic conflict, political transitions), institutional economics (the institutional foundations of economic growth), industrial policy, and the philosophy and methodology of the social sciences."

As for why, he explains in one of his books: "Because I believe the most fundamental grand theories and empirical questions in this field have already been solved by me (whether you agree or not). Once the questions that excite me most in a field have been solved by me, I resolutely choose to leave, and go search in another field for the questions that excite me most — even if that field may be one I was initially unfamiliar with. I believe I can master several different fields, and that is the real me."

And if you look back at Tang Shiping's life, you will find that this "madness" is not without its reasons.

Born in January 1967 in Woguoshui Village, Linwu County, Chenzhou, Hunan Province. Two months later, his father — a middle school teacher — was branded a "counter-revolutionary" and thrown into prison; one of the charges was that, on dialectical grounds, he had denied the existence of "absolute truth." Seven years later, the father's wrongful conviction was overturned, but two years after that he was imprisoned again and reinvestigated. It was not until 1978 that he was formally rehabilitated and released. Tang Shiping's childhood and adolescence were thus spent under the care of his mother and grandmother, in hardship and poverty. "Before I was ten I had seen what a real prison looked like, and what a letter written in blood looked like, because back then my father could only write to my mother in blood on a handkerchief," Tang Shiping recalled in his essay "My Father."

When he entered junior high at age 10, Tang Shiping became interested in the origin of life and resolved to win a Nobel Prize. In 1981, at 14, he was admitted to China University of Geosciences, majoring in stratigraphy and paleontology. But he later found that "studying dinosaurs couldn't solve the problem of the origin of life," so he conceived the idea of applying to the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC). After teaching himself 31 courses related to the biological sciences, he was admitted in 1985 to USTC's graduate program in molecular biology. After completing his master's, he went to Wayne State University in the United States for a PhD in molecular biology and genetics.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180321165020DGgzBQt4mfshCSin.jpg-w600)

In 1996, Tang Shiping, doing postdoctoral work at the Sidney Kimmel Cancer Center in the United States, ran experiments for eight or nine straight months without any progress, and found himself stuck. At the same time, the Taiwan Strait Crisis erupted, cross-strait tensions mounted, and the situation drew the attention of many Chinese students abroad. Tang Shiping was no exception. At the time, he felt that "China did not yet know how to deal with Western democratic governments," which was why the Taiwan Strait Crisis had occurred. After digging through materials in the library, he also discovered that at the time only one or two institutions in China studied the U.S. Congress — a huge gap.

So in 1997, Tang Shiping abandoned biology, a field he had studied for more than a decade, and went to the University of California, Berkeley, to pursue a master's degree in international relations. Because he was switching from the natural sciences to the social sciences with almost zero foundation, he had to read frantically and study hard. Back then, he read more than 1,000 pages of literature a week, and over 50 books a semester.

After finishing his master's in 1999, Tang Shiping returned to China and joined the Institute of Asia-Pacific Studies at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences (CASS). During his six years at CASS, he not only did a great deal of academic research but also spent a year in the middle of it as an assistant to the director at the Department of Foreign Trade of Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region. "As a scholar, you must study fundamental, big questions. The question I care most about is the rise and fall of nations. So I wanted to go to the poorest part of China and do solid, down-to-earth research," Tang Shiping said.

At the end of 2005, while teaching students at CASS, Tang Shiping made a grand pronouncement: he would write four English-language monographs in 10 years. From 2006 to 2009, he went to the School of International Studies at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore as a senior research fellow. In 2009 he returned to China, and has been a professor at the School of International Relations and Public Affairs at Fudan University ever since; in 2016 he was named a Changjiang Distinguished Professor by China's Ministry of Education.

Also in 2016, Tang Shiping completed all four of his English-language books. Among them, *The Social Evolution of International Politics*, published by Oxford University Press in 2013, groundbreakingly laid out the basic ideas of the social evolution approach. Drawing on evidence from historical documents and archaeological materials, and applying the "mutation—selection—inheritance" core mechanism of social evolution, the book traced three historic systemic transformations: how humanity's Eden-like paradise evolved into a Hobbesian world (offensive realism); how that Hobbesian world was transformed between 1648 and 1945 into a more peaceful, defensive-realist world; and how, after 1945, parts of the world became more rule-based and more peaceful.

Renowned international relations scholar Robert Jervis, a professor at Columbia University, appraised the book: "In this insightful and important study, Tang Shiping shows how international relations actually evolve. Through this research, he revises and reconciles many theories familiar to us, and brilliantly reinterprets the interrelations among war, states, and society. A truly magnificent interpretation."

In addition, *The Social Evolution of International Politics* won the International Studies Association (ISA) "Book of the Year" award. Tang Shiping was also the first Asian scholar to win this book prize. Founded in 1959, the ISA is the most respected and most populous scholarly association in the field of international studies, and one of the largest social science research associations in the world.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180321164427h3bkjY2ty1UwfzM9.jpg-w600)

In 2017, a Chinese edition of *The Social Evolution of International Politics* was published. In the preface, Tang Shiping made another grand promise: "In the next 10 years, I will write four more English-language monographs. The first will be *The Institutional Foundations of Economic Growth*. I remain hopeful that this time, my deadline will not slip too far."

Interestingly, Tang Shiping's Weibo handle is "The Swordsman of Xiaoxiang, Tang Shiping," and his bio is as haughty as ever, quoting a line from Tang dynasty poet Li Qiao's poem 《送骆奉礼从军》: "The sword stirs the spirit of three armies; his robe whips up dust for ten thousand li." Yet this haughty Tang Shiping is not one to look down on everyone. He once wrote a series of essays called "Scholarly Gratitude," thanking senior scholars such as Zhang Yunling, Yan Xuetong, and Wang Jisi — though, of course, there was also "a little bit of criticism."

Now, Tang Shiping works about 13 hours a day, takes no weekends off, and retains a burning passion for research. Together with like-minded scholars at Fudan University, Shanghai University of Finance and Economics, and elsewhere, he has formed a research group that they jokingly call the "Wujiaochang School."

On the afternoon of January 26, 2018, we met him at the Liberal Arts Building at Fudan University. Our interview began with the major topics of 2017, which had just passed. Professor Tang Shiping believes that, for China, the event in 2017 that will have an important and far-reaching impact on the future is the 19th National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party.

"After the 19th Party Congress, the question is how far we can actually go. Because for China to rejuvenate, to move toward high income, the country still faces enormous difficulties and challenges. Second, after 2018, of course, there's Trump and China. The United States has slowly begun to act, once again treating China and Russia as its main — whether threats or competitors. How big the impact will be remains to be assessed, but I think the trend will persist," Tang Shiping told QDaily.

As for his judgment of the situation, he said: "I'm neither optimistic nor pessimistic. China's problems still depend mainly on itself — that doesn't mean the external environment isn't important. At the very start of reform and opening up, China did need to join the Western world, or at least be accepted by it, before reform and opening could proceed. Because if you joined the Soviet camp, there was no market economy, and nothing you did could work. But now China's size and its underlying economic vitality have given it enough capacity to withstand a fair amount of external pressure. Whether it can withstand it successfully, though, probably still depends on how our own internal reforms advance."

This article covers part of QDaily's interview with Professor Tang Shiping; other topics will appear in future installments of this series.

### 1. Sustaining an economy's growth requires six basic dimensions — why are none of them dispensable?

**Q: In January this year, the National Bureau of Statistics released figures showing that China's GDP grew 6.9% in 2017, the first acceleration in seven years. How do you read this data? Why did it happen?**

**T:** I don't really work on short-term economic growth; I focus more on the institutional foundations of economic growth, so I can only answer your question briefly. I think it comes down to three things. First, some of China's internal reforms may be slowly taking effect. Second, of course, the external environment improved. The U.S. and EU economies are both recovering with fairly strong growth, and even Japan seems to be doing well. So overall it's relatively favorable for China. Third, there may be another positive factor: energy prices are actually at a relatively low level. For a big energy importer like China, that probably helps too.

**Q: I've noticed that for nearly 20 years you have been thinking about long-term economic growth. You are currently writing *The Institutional Foundations of Economic Growth* and have proposed a new theoretical framework. So in your view, for China to achieve faster growth in the future, where should it make improvements? If I remember correctly, you emphasize six basic dimensions? Could you go into detail?**

**T:** I've basically finished developing the theory, and the empirical work is largely done too, but because I'm still completing my fourth English-language book, the fifth — *The Institutional Foundations of Economic Growth* — is not fully written yet. My core theory is this: the institutional foundations capable of supporting long-term economic growth have six major dimensions. My analytical framework differs from much previous research that proceeds purely from an inductive angle; mine is relatively more deductive.

Overall, my starting question is: why does a person create wealth? Or, how does a country encourage its people, allow its people, protect its people to create wealth? Put plainly, understanding the institutional foundations of economic growth means answering exactly this question. And my answer is: the institutional foundations of economic growth basically have six major dimensions, and these dimensions interact with one another — they are not fully independent.

The first is order and stability. Because people need stability, and stability promotes investment. We call this Political Order — usually rendered as "political order." Order protects basic stability: no theft, no robbery, and so on.

The second is property rights. Property rights are definitely very fundamental, because they determine whether a person can expect to reap the returns of his own effort. This represents what we call incentive. But I am critical of North and others for over-emphasizing property rights. Property rights are just one dimension of the institutional foundations of economic growth — though certainly an important one.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180321165342m6o4vhjO5bg3la0M.jpg-w600)

The third dimension is incentive in the status market — or, a set of institutions about social mobility. Property rights support the incentive mechanism in the material market. But people also expect to advance in what we might call the status market. For example, a word commonly used in China today is "pin die" — "competing on fathers." "Pin die" is bad; it discourages people from competing for good positions and playing their own roles, and instead everything gets passed down through inheritance. In short, incentives in the status market are also a very important dimension.

The fourth dimension is democratic institutions. But we emphasize that democracy's impact on economic growth is indirect, channeled through specific pathways, and conditional — particularly on the level of economic development. Economic growth has certain stages. For a long time, many countries grow by learning from developed countries, so we call this "growth through imitation." China, for instance, may have been able to rely basically on imitation for quite a long stretch — the first 20 or 30 years after reform and opening up may have been enough to grow that way. Whatever others do, we copy, and do it more cheaply and more efficiently than they do, and you can grow.

But once economic growth reaches a certain point, and GDP per capita gets higher, it means there is less and less you can imitate. At that stage we call it "growth that requires innovation." And at that point you need to protect innovation more, and a relatively democratic system is very useful. So often when people say, compare China and India — as if to show why India's democratic system actually hinders economic growth — that's not a particularly rigorous scientific discussion. Many factors can interact with the political system and cause India's economic growth to lag China's. China may do many other things better than India, so it may achieve more growth.

The fifth dimension is enhancing people's capacity through redistribution. The goal of good redistribution is actually very simple: to give low-income people, or those born into poverty, a fair chance to compete for good jobs and receive a good education. I think on this point China did quite well in the early reform era, or even with land reform. But now there are quite a few problems; social classes have become highly rigidified.

The last dimension is equality of opportunity. For example: do you appoint people by favoritism, or by merit? China's lowering of the college entrance exam cutoff scores for some ethnic minority regions is an example of promoting equality of opportunity. To advance in the material market and the status market, you first need the opportunity to enter the market, and you need institutional guarantees of equal opportunity for all.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180321165710i4VvFO8HopdkGwce.jpg-w600)

### 2. Have we overstated the role of "stability" in this? Is violence inevitable in the modernization process?

**Q: You just mentioned that order and stability matter a lot. Many establishment figures believe that if controls were loosened, China would descend into chaos, so stability maintenance is crucial. What do you think?**

**T:** Even people who support spending enormous resources on stability maintenance understand that it is high-cost. So a good institutional system — I just said these six dimensions form a system — you cannot emphasize one and not the other, or only emphasize one and ignore everything else. That is an unscientific discussion. I keep saying these six dimensions are a system. At different stages, different configurations may all lead to economic growth. But at a certain point, all six dimensions probably need to be in decent shape for growth to continue.

So on the stability dimension, the best institutional system is one that does not maintain stability at too high a cost. If you maintain stability at too high a cost, you end up pouring money that should go into education, infrastructure, better medical care, and so on, into stability maintenance instead. Maintaining stability mainly through the machinery of violence is actually very expensive. A good stability-maintenance system keeps stability at low cost, which obviously benefits economic growth greatly.

Therefore, I believe that at least a certain degree of democratization has major positive significance for China's future. If done well, such democratization should not lead to large-scale political disorder. Many people either misunderstand democracy, or simply don't want it, and so at such moments they deliberately claim democracy inevitably brings civil unrest. That is not the case. In fact, many democratization processes have not taken the form of civil disorder.

**Q: Your research on the formation of the modern world actually proves this point — there are the experience and cases of many countries.**

**T:** Yes. I often use an analogy: modernization is like a lone island. So far, only about 30 countries have swum ashore. More than 100 others are still swimming toward it. And some countries are not just stuck in the middle of the ocean, clinging to a rock — some may already have drowned. Overall, the road to modernization is fairly tortuous. Most countries' experiences are fairly tortuous. A few countries are lucky — Britain, as we usually say, was relatively lucky.

Overall, judging from the process of modernization, modernization does not have to proceed through violence. But it is indeed very often accompanied by a great deal of violence. The reason modernization only seems to make progress through violence is that struggles over who controls the path of modernization produce violence. So China's 1911 Revolution, and the subsequent civil war between the Kuomintang and the Communist Party, were actually both born of disagreements over "how should China modernize?" — not because modernization itself requires violence.

Another point I especially want to stress: the reason violence so often arises in the modernization process is that political figures and political groups cannot form a democratic, or peaceful, system for transferring power. They are forced to resolve things through violence. So many people who believe modernization must involve violence have the causal relationship somewhat backwards — or at least gotten it wrong.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180321231640A1rSRUGdYicD5wta.jpg-w600)

### 3. What role does the middle class play in the modernization process?

**Q: Chinese society is also a society in transition. Traditional theory holds that in the long run, economic development will create the foundation for a modern democratic system. Because economic development brings the rise of the middle class, which in turn raises rights consciousness and drives the modernization transition. But applied to China, this theory doesn't seem to hold? Do you have any observations or views on China's middle class?**

**T:** I think this Lipset-style theory, or middle-class theory, assumes far too much that this transition will be very "natural," as if it flows smoothly once conditions are ripe. I think there are definitely some problems with that. Of course, China today, compared with the Cultural Revolution or the Anti-Rightist period, has made great progress. We call it liberalization — a certain loosening. It just hasn't yet reached a relatively complete enlightened polity.

But as for whether it suits China — I think that mainly depends on China's own development. Because China has really only been doing economic development for the past 40 years. Of course, we can say the first 30 years may have laid certain foundations, but overall China's economic development is still quite incomplete, and a large share of China's population has not actually entered what you would call middle-class status.

**Q: So what do you think will become the key driving forces of China's modernization transition in the future? Any thoughts?**

**T:** The driving forces are essentially those. One, of course, is the leadership. Is the Chinese Communist Party itself willing to push democratization? That is obviously very important, because top-down democratization tends to be relatively mild. Another force is the middle class, the intellectual elite, and the people who control or command a lot of economic and financial resources. And of course another force matters too. Here, we still need to agree with some of the theory of Marx and Engels — the working class, and even the peasants, are also important forces.

So I think China's democratization probably cannot be predetermined in some a priori way. But I suspect many people in our intellectual circles — and I would even say the broad public — hope for a relatively mild transformation, not an overly turbulent one.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180321165737AWVhN452nmTS6Qaf.jpg-w600)

### 4. Why is it that when the government talks about innovation, it cannot stop at technological innovation — that the real test for government lies in institutional innovation?

**Q: You just mentioned that different development stages require different institutional foundations, and that today's China most needs what you call innovative growth. Everyone knows Schumpeter's theory of creative destruction. But in your view, this kind of creative destruction never really happened in China in the past — there was only imitation. In the future, good economic growth can only be achieved through destructive innovation. What does the government need to do at this point? Such as protecting innovation.**

**T:** First, to emphasize: the innovative growth I speak of is broader than what Schumpeter described. Usually when economics talks about innovation it means technological innovation. But I believe institutional innovation is just as important as technology. Both types of innovation are necessary. So the innovation I refer to here includes both institutional and technological innovation.

What innovation needs most is an environment that protects innovation. China today is probably fairly adequate at protecting technological innovation. If you do technological innovation, you are usually protected — though also constrained. For example, we recently applied for a patent and were rejected. It wasn't because of any problem with our technology; it was a political problem. So when I see news like that, I don't know whether to laugh or cry. Because what we do is social science; it's not quite like making some electronic product.

But more critical — and something China will increasingly find constraining — are the constraints on institutional innovation. For example, as we often say, how easy it is to start a company is an important measure. On things like this, you can clearly see that under some of our existing institutional arrangements, certain innovations are not encouraged, or at least are difficult — institutional innovations in particular.

Very often it is easier for the government to push institutional innovation. But the government does not always have sufficiently good information. More importantly, if only the government is allowed to drive institutional innovation, bottom-up institutional innovation becomes very difficult. And Hayek and Karl Popper both discussed this — no one can know all the information. So relying entirely on top-down institutional innovation is problematic.

Therefore, we must acknowledge that top-down institutional innovation, or what is called top-level design, is certainly important. But we cannot emphasize only top-level design. We must also encourage ordinary people and grassroots organizations to have some innovations of their own. Such innovations may solve real problems better, and at relatively lower cost.

![Schumpeter, from: [blogspot](http://internationalpoliticaltheory.blogspot.com/2011/11/joseph-schumpeter-on-democracy.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180321170808BGCPjrwz8m2nHKbI.jpg-w600)

### 5. Industrial policy also requires a strong society, not just a strong government — why is top-level design alone not enough?

**Q: You said in a previous interview: "Only discussing industrial policy without discussing institutions is escaping political reality, because without good institutions, good policy is almost impossible. And only discussing institutions without considering industrial policy is ignoring history, because almost all countries' economic development requires industrial policy; the key is whether the industrial policy is good or bad. Similarly, some people like to discuss whether the government or society is strong. In fact, we need both a strong government and a strong society. The key is: where the government should intervene and where it should not, and how much it should intervene. And these are concrete questions." Could you elaborate on these two issues? Give some examples you consider important to the public interest?**

**T:** Because scholarship on industrial policy is still not sufficient, we are pushing this research forward. Overall, I think evaluating an industrial policy requires at least three basic dimensions.

The first is, of course, time. How long does an industrial policy take to work? Many people forget this. Some industrial policies may genuinely need five or ten years before you know whether they succeed.

The second is how you measure an industrial policy's success. We now generally use, for example, total factor productivity, or market share, or competitiveness. I think that is a very important approach.

The third, I think, is cost. If your returns are less than your inputs, it is of course a failure.

But even after measuring along these three dimensions, from an econometric standpoint there is still a problem: how can you be sure an industry's success or failure is related to the industrial policy? I think there are many technical issues here, and our current research is working through them.

Based on our research so far, some of China's industrial policies should be considered successful, but a considerable portion may not have had particularly large effects. As for the reasons, we are still researching and have not finished, so I dare not say too much.

Therefore, we should acknowledge that industrial policy is definitely useful in particular times and particular contexts. But I also don't endorse putting everything into industrial policy. Good industrial policy is not planned economy. In addition, I think a considerable number of Chinese industries lean too much toward state-owned enterprises, which may not be particularly efficient.

Also, discussing industrial policy means you must also discuss the institutional system, and discuss the improvement of macro policy. All three affect economic growth. So I think many people nowadays discuss industrial policy without discussing institutional change, and when they discuss institutional change, they assume industrial policy must be gone. I think this is biased. In many cases, debate should not lead one camp to completely reject whatever is correct or useful in the other.

### 6. Are globalization and liberal institutions entering a period of retreat?

**Q: In recent years, many people feel the whole world — including China, the United States, and Europe — is moving in a more extreme and closed direction. Many Western countries' liberal democratic systems have run into problems, and globalization is reversing. What are your thoughts? Why has it come to this?**

**T:** First, I want to say: we Chinese often have a saying, the road is tortuous. So I think we often look at history superficially and imagine modernization as a straight, broad highway. It is not like that; there are many setbacks. As I just said, many countries may have already been defeated — drowned, gone — or already sunk, and some may still be struggling downstream. So I don't think this kind of reversal is particularly surprising — by "not surprising" I mean we as observers of the world should not find it surprising.

But that does not mean we — citizens, politicians, companies alike — should not squarely face these problems. They need to be faced. There has already been a great deal of shallow discussion of these issues, and I may not have much new to add.

First, what everyone talks about most, especially for developed countries, is a paradox — a high-welfare society plus globalization, especially after low-end production flowed to developing countries, caused "re-impoverishment" inside developed countries. Many people lost their jobs, or can't find suitable jobs, because their work was taken by people at lower wage levels. These people fall back into — or back into the poverty they had already climbed out of. That outcome inevitably produces a great deal of discontent.

So I think we must acknowledge this. Especially for developed countries, this is a very clear challenge, and one that may persist for a while. The root of this challenge is that in the good times they built a very strong welfare state. Everyone knows that once people receive higher benefits, they are certainly unwilling to have them cut. And "re-impoverishment" is even more frightening. This, I think, is something developed countries must confront.

Second, the new inequality within developed countries is somewhat related to globalization, but not entirely caused by it. To a considerable degree, this inequality comes from the fact that a country's tax policies, financial policies, and so on all make it easier for the rich to make money. Suppose there are two people: one starts with $10,000, the other with $50,000. Ten years later, the $10,000 person has become $20,000 in income, but the $50,000 person has become $500,000. The gap grows larger and larger. This is the problem Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* reflects.

The third issue, I think, is indeed also related to the Western world too confidently asserting — or somewhat proudly believing — that it could change the world. Changing the world has never been that easy. For example, their foreign interventions produced many refugees and civilian casualties. Those refugees flooded into Europe and pushed Europe toward so-called conservatization. I think these three are fairly obvious reasons. We have criticized this kind of neo-imperialist practice in many places.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018032116592552ESeBFyh36zNVxL.jpg-w600)

### 7. When we're confident and proud, how do we stay clear-eyed about how the world judges us?

**T:** For developing countries, I'm not so sure. Perhaps according to China's data it is so, but whether other developing countries are the same, I'm not very clear. But what I want to say is: Chinese young people — some increasingly so (nationalistic). I think we can probably identify about three reasons.

The first reason is China's growing national power. Many young Chinese who can now travel abroad come from the coastal areas or from relatively high-income places. They don't really understand the hard, tortuous road China once walked, so it is easy for them to feel China is already a very rich country. And when others say China is not good — whether criticizing the economy, or even certain political defects — young Chinese get very upset. That is very easy to understand. When a person feels good about himself, he does not readily accept others' criticism.

The second reason: some foreign criticism is indeed not well-intentioned. It may genuinely come from being unable to stand watching China grow strong. On this point, we must also acknowledge it.

The third reason may be that Chinese media and foreign media have formed a kind of mutual dependence. We usually pay attention only to news that says China is either especially good or especially bad. In the smartphone era, most people will not carefully read lengthy studies, or even in-depth long-form reporting. What people consume is usually very brief — they read the headline and that's it. So I think a good part of certain appearances may not reflect the real state of things, but are the result of media amplification.

### 8. Why is it that no evaluation of Deng Xiaoping's reform and opening up could ever be excessive?

**Q: 2018 marks the 40th anniversary of China's reform and opening up. Looking back over these 40 years, what are your biggest takeaways? I remember you have written many related articles before, for example ["China Is a Not-Too-Successful 'Developmental State'"](http://www.ftchinese.com/story/001069171).**

**T:** That article of mine was not saying China is unsuccessful, but that it is not the most successful. It should be put this way: we have spent 40 years and have not yet reached OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) levels. South Korea, Japan, Singapore, and Taiwan spent 30 or 40 years to enter the ranks of OECD countries or regions. So we still have a long way to go.

I certainly hold reform and opening up in the highest regard. I have said many times that last century, in all of East Asia there were only three world-class leaders: Deng Xiaoping, Lee Kuan Yew, and Shigeru Yoshida. I believe Deng Xiaoping's reform and opening up means so much to China that no evaluation of it could be excessive.

But I think we now need to truly revisit and understand reform and opening up. We must be clear that Deng Xiaoping's reform and opening up was, put plainly and simply, a return to Adam Smith. Unfortunately, Adam Smith published his book in 1776, and Mr. Deng Xiaoping could only bring the market economy to China in 1978. But this return was necessary. Nowadays, too many people, too much of the time, want to blaze a completely unique path to modernization. I think there is a problem with that. I said modernization is a lone island; we see all around us a crowd of people who have already drowned, a crowd who are already done for. What does that mean? It means many paths lead nowhere: for example, without a market economy!

So should we still draw lessons and experience from others? Of course! The truly good reform and opening up is one that spares yourself from making mistakes — drawing lessons from others' failures and others' experience, so that our society's march toward modernization pays a smaller price and gains more in return. That way, it benefits both the country and the people.

**Q: But some people believe that right now no lessons are being absorbed — there is no reform and no opening up — and that this is very worrying.**

**T:** I would not dare say there is neither reform nor opening up. Reform is still proceeding, and opening up continues in some respects. But I think the questions we really need to care about are these: first, is the direction right? Second, is the progress sufficient? Third, do these policies have a certain continuity or stability?

Right now, I think the biggest concern for China, for its people, for foreign investors, and for Chinese domestic entrepreneurs is whether this continuity will hold. The government can make promises, but are your promises credible? If the law can be changed today and changed again tomorrow, that certainly affects many investors.

**Q: Do you have any particular warnings or worries about society right now?**

**T:** One of the most frightening problems is surely that China's wealthy class loses confidence in the whole system. The government can of course use many regulations to limit the outflow of wealth. But that still does not solve the incentive problem for investors. Because uninvested wealth is merely earning interest; it is not functioning as capital (wealth is not the same as capital).

So I am rather worried that China's wealthy class will no longer believe this system can bring them returns. To solve this, the government needs to make its commitments credible, and the direction of reform needs to be right.
