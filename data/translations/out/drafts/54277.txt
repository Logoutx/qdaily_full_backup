Beauty Devices Have Become Young Chinese Consumers' New Toys. How Did They Catch On?

Technical jargon, social networks and influencers, plus the fear of "early aging" — a new electronic toy is born.

Wet your face, apply cleanser, switch on the facial cleansing brush and glide it over your skin for 2–5 minutes.

After washing your face, use a facial steamer to open your pores. You can't apply skincare products yet — first spread on the gel, then move a radiofrequency (RF) beauty device in circles across your face. This, supposedly, lifts and firms.

Toner, serum, paired with an infusion device, and only then the face cream. Finally, massage your face with a microcurrent massager.

If you follow the beauty influencers on XiaoHongShu and Weibo, this is the basic routine.

Facial cleansing brushes, facial steamers, hair removers, face-mask devices, face-slimming devices... If you spot several of these products on a woman's vanity, don't be surprised — they are many female consumers' new toys.

All beauty devices make the same promise: a few minutes a day, sustained over time, will deliver results. Their common trait is a high price — among the top five best-sellers on Tmall, everything except domestic brands is expensive (entry-level models go for around 1,000 yuan, while mainstream high-end models can reach 3,000–4,000 yuan).

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614165751a2o4n6l803ispSGD.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614184219rlyN70T4DWPkOtGi.jpg-w600)

The popularity of beauty devices can't be explained by "consumption upgrade" alone — otherwise people would simply buy more expensive cosmetics. There's something else: the companies behind them have successfully conveyed a message to consumers — whether you're washing your face, applying moisturizer or putting on makeup, using your hands is no longer enough.

According to the manufacturers, you need technology. Only by deploying ultrasound, LED light, radiofrequency, microcurrent and the like can you slim your face, fight aging, help skin absorb products, and ultimately become more beautiful.

### Consumers are buying in — at least in China

According to market research firm Euromonitor International, facial cleansing brushes and personal-care appliances (the former plus shavers, hair removers and oral-care devices) grew at compound annual rates of 11% and 8% respectively in China over the past two years. Guotai Securities and Euromonitor estimate that facial cleansing brushes alone — a single functional niche — will grow from total sales of 2.24 billion yuan last year to an 8.26-billion-yuan market by 2020.

The category is too new and the product types too numerous; even the consulting firms haven't had time to systematically measure its size.

Euromonitor doesn't track newer categories such as facial steamers and skin-infusion devices. Executives at both beauty-device brands NEWA and Mushu — which QDaily interviewed — told us the industry is growing at multiples. Over the past two years, the beauty-device market has grown sevenfold, said Mushu founder Li Wang.

![Mushu products](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180615002236Cc6pmivq2BYzLfRe.jpeg-w600)

In August 2017, Tmall upgraded beauty devices from a second-level category to a first-level one, moving them out of small home appliances and under the beauty umbrella. Tmall even ran an online-offline "Super Brand Day" for beauty devices — a sign of how much the e-commerce platform values the category.

After smartphones and tablets, an electronics category this hot could only be beauty devices — or maybe air purifiers.

Israeli brand Tripollar passed 300 million yuan in sales in its second year in China. ReFa's parent company is reportedly preparing an IPO because the brand is so popular with Chinese consumers. Swedish silicone-focused personal-care brand Foreo saw its hit silicone cleansing brush Luna grow more than 500% globally in 2017.

The market was pioneered by startups like Foreo and Clarisonic. Big companies such as L'Oréal, or medical-aesthetics company Keyiren, entered by acquiring smaller players. That widened the sales reach and the pool of potential consumers.

Because beauty devices sit at the intersection of cosmetics and home appliances, all kinds of companies can plant a flag. Some are companies whose main business is medical-aesthetics services or equipment, such as Keyiren (a US company) and China's Ouhuan Meike. Domestic appliance brand POVOS has also launched a facial steamer, and Lexy — a vacuum-cleaner maker — has moved into personal-care small appliances.

Not to mention the big international cosmetics groups: beauty brands like Clinique, SK-II and Innisfree also have cleansing or infusion-type products. Celebrity personal brands such as Madonna's MDNA SKIN and Fan Bingbing's Fan Beauty have launched beauty devices too.

### The beauty-device concept is 30-plus years old, but social networks turned it into real demand

Beauty devices are not a brand-new market. Panasonic launched a facial steamer in Japan in the 1980s; the first Clarisonic cleansing brush was born in 2004; the same year, Japanese company Ya-Man introduced an ionic-light beauty device, entering the beauty small-appliance market.

![Ya-Man's product iterations](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614170326IxNw1p4TgC59Plsi.png-w600)

What turned beauty devices from a weak demand into a strong one was social networks.

If you drew a timeline for beauty devices, signs of their rise appear around 2011–2012, when a number of brands were founded — microcurrent-focused ReFa launched its first product in 2009, and brands founded even earlier were already trending: Clarisonic began selling well in the US after an endorsement from Oprah Winfrey; [at the end of 2011 it was acquired by L'Oréal Group.](https://www.loreal-finance.com/eng/news-release/loreal-acquires-pacific-bioscience-laboratories-the-market-leader-in-sonic-skin-care-technology-791.htm)

In China, 2013 was the first important milestone: Swedish brand Foreo and Clarisonic both officially entered the Chinese market that year. But even before that, consumers were already discussing beauty devices on social media, and products trickled in through daigou personal-shopper channels or overseas online shopping. "Miracle face-washing gadget" became a Baidu hot search term in 2014 — evidence that the cleansing-brush category had already gained recognition among consumers, with more people actively searching for it.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614165904aronf91B2JR05Gud.png-w600)

This period also coincided with the founding or flowering of a wave of social media. Instagram, focused on photo sharing, was founded in 2011; in China, WeChat was born (back when "micro-business" sellers and daigou probably hadn't yet adopted the platform); those were also Weibo's peak years. Two years later came XiaoHongShu, a community-plus-e-commerce platform for beauty sharing.

Because prices are higher than ordinary beauty products and the products carry some technical content, beauty devices are a high-involvement product category within the industry.

Beauty devices require a lot of consumer education before the sale. Today, the sonic technology used by Clarisonic and Foreo hardly counts as a breakthrough (it existed in oral care long ago), but brands still need to explain to consumers why it works and why it's safe.

![NEWA's e-commerce copy explaining the technology](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614170213QYwygTovc9h0LINm.png-w600)

Not to mention technologies closer to medical aesthetics, such as the multi-polar radiofrequency championed by NEWA and Tripollar. "What is RF, and why does it feel barely warm on the face?" — Gao Chen, general manager of the instrument division at Shanghai Yimei Beauty Co., NEWA's China operator, says this is the kind of content they constantly have to "educate" female consumers about.

"Can this thing touch water? Can I wear my earrings while using it? Can I use it with braces? Can I use it while pregnant?" Gao Chen says consumers raise all these questions. She believes the marketing cost of selling a beauty device is higher than for ordinary beauty products like lipstick.

The purchase decision also takes longer: consumers spend more time researching and comparing products. Indeed, the concepts behind beauty devices offer plenty to "research": ultrasound, LED light, radiofrequency, microcurrent — plus thrown-around terms like dermis and epidermis that leave people baffled (even if you know what the word means, you can't tell what it has to do with your face). But convincing people it works takes more than a single ad.

Mushu founder Li Wang told QDaily that conversion on the day an ad runs is low: "We found that they tend to view the product a second or third time before finally making the purchase decision."

The same goes offline. "Store beauty salespeople also tell us some customers may look once and not buy right away, then hesitate. That person may come back in — on the second or third visit she checks whether anything new has arrived. It doesn't close on the first visit," Li Wang says.

Jun, who works in IT in Beijing, told me she decided to buy a ReFa two months after first encountering it. Price was part of the reason.

What convinces consumers, over and over again, is no longer the advertising on department-store ground floors but the influencers on social networks.

NEWA's general manager Gao Chen says: "In fact all of our marketing is online," because the 20–35-year-old women in tier-one and tier-two cities that NEWA targets are active on social media such as Weibo and XiaoHongShu. They increasingly buy makeup online too. Half of NEWA's marketing budget goes to traffic acquisition, such as Taobao Zhitongche paid search.

If you search "beauty device" on these platforms, you'll find masses of review posts, recommendations, photos and videos. Word of mouth and advertising are tangled together, hard to tell apart.

Gao Chen believes consumers are easily swayed by "word-of-mouth recommendations" from people around them — a trait she considers common among women. On this point, beauty devices behave much like beauty overall. "What you're really capturing is the consumer's experience and emotional resonance," she says. And social marketing helps beauty products resonate with consumers.

Fully half of NEWA's marketing budget goes to beauty bloggers and key opinion leaders (KOLs). Fashion blogger Becky Li and beauty blogger "Junping the Demon King" have both worked with NEWA.

Whether selling lipstick or beauty devices, influencers are an unavoidable link; the beauty industry has been thoroughly transformed by the democratization of information channels. More and more consumers draw inspiration from the example set by influencers and KOLs. Unlike distant celebrities, influencers feel like ordinary people — "partners in solving problems."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614165946iPHpZILghD2dWJUt.png-w600)

Once the category matures, beauty devices still turn to celebrity endorsers. Celebrities who have shown off ReFa products include Madonna, Ruby Lin and Ni Ni; ReFa recently signed Fan Bingbing as its ambassador. Mushu signed Ady An; Clarisonic's ambassadors are Little S and Zhong Chuxi.

But the logic behind it is different. Economist Steven Landsburg remarked long ago that nobody really believes celebrities use what they endorse; endorsement works because consumers believe the company can afford a celebrity — which is no different in essence from a bank building itself a grand headquarters.

Having a fashion KOL use the device live on camera, by contrast, makes it easier for consumers to project themselves into it — "if I use it, I'll be as beautiful as them."

Buying a beauty device right after watching online reviews or recommendations, skipping the offline store experience entirely, is also some consumers' shopping habit.

Online channels contribute most of the sales for the category. Clarisonic's online sales account for 70% of its total; domestic brand Mushu is at roughly the same ratio; for NEWA, about 80% of its China sales come from online.

That is far above the online penetration of beauty products overall. According to Bain & Company, online sales account for only 25% of cosmetics as a whole — already much higher than daily chemicals and luxury goods.

Beauty-device brands also tend to have relatively few SKUs, which suits online sales. With most sales coming from online, they also save the cost of large-scale offline distribution.

### Fear of "early aging" has made young Chinese women the main users of beauty devices

In China, beauty-device consumers are mainly young women living and working in tier-one and tier-two cities.

They tend to be much younger than overseas markets. For massage-type or RF beauty devices, the same product is sold to mature women over 40 in overseas markets, while in China it targets an age bracket 10–15 years lower. You can see it from the product models or ambassadors.

![A model on NEWA's English-language website](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614191706urLNk72H6pMOcmfG.png-w600)

![NEWA's China site uses noticeably younger models](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614191526HdN6bAZGxEsrKPlB.jpg-w600)

Ever since the concept of "early aging" was coined, the marketing of many women's personal-care products has latched onto it. Whether brand marketing or beauty bloggers, they all tell you that women should start anti-aging at 25. Behind the beauty-device boom lies young women's anxiety about aging and their longing for beauty. "Chinese women of this age bracket love beauty too much — everyone's attention to the details of their own face is extremely high," Gao Chen says.

This selling point has indeed worked. Wang Zhen, chairman of Bijie Cleaning Technology — Ya-Man's general distributor in China — [said in an interview](http://www.xinhuanet.com/fashion/2017-10/25/c_1121842891_2.htm): in Japan, Ya-Man's consumers are mostly 35–40 and older, while in China, early adopters of beauty devices are young buyers aged 20–35, and the consumer base even includes college and high school students.

Beauty-device consumers keep getting younger. Sun Ling, a NEWA marketing specialist, told me the average consumer age was 26 in the past couple of years, but it is now trending downward.

Kitty, who studies in Guangzhou, is one example. She is 19 this year and decided to buy one after trying a friend's Foreo. She also uses a NuFace — for face slimming, she says.

Kiko, 26, does design work in Hefei. She bought a Luna cleansing brush, a ReFa microcurrent roller massager and a Tripollar RF beauty device — using all three together. "I just don't want to get old; I want my skin to stay good," she told QDaily.

Another marketing line resembles that of thousand-yuan skincare products: "Buying a beauty device (or a high-end beauty product) is an investment in your looks."

This is tied to the fact that high-end beauty's audience in China skews younger overall.

Kantar's Beauty Panel research in 2016 showed that 20–29-year-olds have become the main consumer force in the cosmetics category, and their love for high-end brands far exceeds other age groups. Market moves in high-end beauty bear this out: SK-II signed Leah Dou as ambassador; Guerlain chose Yang Yang; Estée Lauder picked Yang Mi and Wang Kai — all aimed at young consumers.

The consumption outlook of the post-80s and post-90s generations is completely different from their predecessors'. They grew up in an era of rapid wealth growth, are more willing to spend, save less, and have richer parents. Chinese luxury consumers are also younger than luxury buyers in Europe and the US.

Digital marketing also lets beauty-device brands target this group precisely. After all, besides being willing to spend, their other defining trait is being "digital natives."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614170505VFMdg2J4Q0sRtXxq.png-w600)

Unlike other beauty products, beauty devices require more consumer-relationship maintenance after the sale. Most beauty-device brands have launched companion skincare products: some stipulate that the device must be used with the brand's own products (such as Tripollar and NEWA), others recommend using them with the brand's skincare (such as Foreo and Ya-Man).

Therefore, urging consumers to keep using the device matters. It is also a source of continuing revenue for beauty-device companies.

"Because a beauty device easily gathers dust, you need someone to nag you like a personal trainer," Gao Chen says.

### Now that beauty devices are hot, pricier and cheaper options will multiply

Behind the growth of the beauty-device market lies a consumption upgrade from consumables and status goods to durable goods.

Over the past two years, consumption upgrade has entered the durable-goods phase. [In 2016–2017, China's GDP per capita was roughly at the level of the US around 1975 and Japan around 1980](http://www.cgws.com/cczq/ggdt/ccyj/201709/P020170918599289792955.pdf). At this stage, growth in non-durable goods slows while consumption upgrade in durables (including cars and home appliances) becomes more pronounced, and the share of services consumption is climbing too.

"Treating yourself well" no longer stops at eating well, buying more clothes, or status consumption like luxury bags — it has extended into home care and personal care.

Among personal-care and home-care products, robot vacuums, smart toilet seats, water flossers and washing machines with drying functions — these non-essential durable goods, once well marketed and precisely targeted, have all met with good market response.

![The top-selling beauty devices on Tmall keep getting cheaper](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614185957nrGKEX6mwRsidOLv.png-w600)

When China's beauty-device market was just taking off in 2013, product prices were relatively high — even entry-level products cost around 800–1,000 yuan. That was also because the new category was still in its introduction phase and consumers were not yet so sensitive to price.

As the market grows, available price points will come down. Guotai Securities believes hundred-yuan mid-range beauty devices may have potential in the future.

Gao Chen says: "I think in two years, beauty-device prices will stretch out, gradually spreading from both ends." She believes home beauty devices will multiply at both extremes — as low as tens or a hundred yuan, and as high as over 10,000 yuan.

Shanghai Yimei is considering introducing a new brand priced lower than NEWA. Foreo has already launched an entry-level product costing just over 200 yuan. And at the other end — Mushu, whose main price range is 200–500 yuan, plans to launch a premium line costing over 1,000 yuan.

Could beauty devices become a product everyone owns several of, like lipstick? Hard to say. Many people in the industry describe this consumption as "novelty-seeking." How to keep the heat up and extend the life cycle of a brand or product remains an open question.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180614170110mw82ePHxlD3RBJUA.png-w600)

The earliest hit, Clarisonic, is already showing signs of decline. From 2014 onward, Clarisonic's global online search volume slid steadily — exactly ten years after the brand launched its first product. In 2016 Clarisonic began [laying off staff at its US headquarters](https://uk.reuters.com/article/uk-loreal-results-idUKKCN1082FZ), and L'Oréal noted in its earnings that the brand's overall value had been impaired. Earlier this year, its Google search heat was overtaken by rival Foreo.

But how far Foreo can go is also a question, given that its technological moat is not deep (the same is true of most beauty devices). Cleansing brushes focused on silicone and vibration are proliferating, with products from Korean brand Innisfree and domestic brands Kingdom and Mushu among those launching similar devices.

Still, many industry insiders believe there is potential to draw in more consumers.

### "Perception outweighs fact"

Although beauty devices involve plenty of technology, the effectiveness of most of them remains controversial (the comments alone show it). Skin condition has always been shaped by many factors: genetics, lifestyle including diet and exercise, skincare products... You could even say beauty and skincare are a kind of metaphysics.

Last year, a post [tearing down a ReFa unit piece by piece](https://weibo.com/ttarticle/p/show?id=2309404154006472630771&mod=zwenzhang) made Weibo's hot search; the blogger's conclusion was that the product costs little to make, uses simple technology, and isn't worth its thousand-yuan-plus price. Interestingly, the replies suggest many consumers didn't accept this: "Maybe the one torn down was a fake," "It works with long-term use," "So what if there's brand premium — I'm happy using it" — these were their rebuttals.

"Perception outweighs fact," says Mushu founder Li Wang. That could sum up the entire beauty-device — even beauty — industry.

Following the vague concepts, the category keeps getting livelier.

Small-appliance brand Midea, for instance, recently put names like "soft-water beauty device" and "skin-cleansing instrument" on products that [appear to be the kitchen water purifiers it previously sold](https://item.jd.com/5190194.html). A.O. Smith has launched similar products too.

Useful or not, you will see more and more products appear under the beauty-device name.
