During the National Day holiday, we invited eight young writers to recommend books they consider worth reading. Each has made considerable achievements in their own field and enjoys sharing new knowledge and ideas. This is the third writer: Zhao Yimin.

**It's book recommendation season again.**

**At this time last year, QDaily invited some interesting people from the big accounts on Weibo, WeChat, and Zhihu to recommend a batch of books for us. These people included [Zhihu founder Zhou Yuan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15357.html), [Guokr founder Ji Shisan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15272.html), [Zhang Wei, founder of the WeChat official account "Shixiang"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15320.html), [Wu Miao, founder of the WeChat official account "Leviathan"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15303.html), and [screenwriter Shi Hang](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15617.html). The pitch for those recommendations was: recommend a book you think is good that you read within the past year — it might help your career, or it might simply be good. Chinese and English titles both welcome.**

**This year, we've decided to change the format slightly.**

**Professional readers are a scarce resource in any era. Their judgments about book recommendations and reviews can become an important reference for how we choose books — the reviews themselves can even be worth reading as books, like Tony Judt's *Reappraisals*.**

**Of course, our invited writers don't all reflect on twentieth-century history as weightily as Tony Judt does in his book. These people are younger, more interesting, sharper-eyed, and not professional writers; they have a certain influence in their respective fields and enjoy sharing new knowledge and ideas.**

**We hope to introduce them to you through the format of "book recommendations + long-form book reviews," offering a bit of reference for the small matter of reading.**

**Since everyone's experience differs, the books each person recommends and the style of each review naturally vary — and puzzling over those differences is itself rather fun. From October 1 to October 8, we will publish one writer's reading list and book review each day; we hope you find them useful. If you spot other good professional readers around you, leave us a comment and tell us — you might see their article here before long.**

**This is the third: Zhao Yimin.**

Zhao Yimin — known as "Duanchang" on Douban — is now a PhD candidate in the Department of Geography at the London School of Economics, working mainly on space and power, urban political economy, and the politics of land. What follows is a large number of books about cities and space; even the novels have to do with the city.

Some of these recommendations read rather abstrusely, but they should help you systematically understand where you find yourself. The pity is that some of them have not yet been published in Chinese translation.

### **Book Recommendations**

**By Yimin Zhao**

***The Urban Revolution***

Author: Henri Lefebvre

Publisher:

Univ Of Minnesota Press

Publication date: February 2003

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Urban-Revolution-Henri-Lefebvre/dp/0816641609/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475248139&sr=8-1&keywords=The+Urban+Revolution)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930231449uy3eSvgCjVXKn6lH.jpg-w600)

The rapid process of urbanization has made urban space a term we can no longer escape in everyday life — but before we take it for granted and choose and confirm our own position vis-à-vis the city, do we already understand concepts like the city, space, and urban society well enough? This little book is a representative work by the French philosopher Henri Lefebvre, and it gives us a fitting entry point for rethinking the condition of the city and reflecting on where we stand when facing the urban process. The French edition was published in 1970 — after the red-hot sixties, yet before *The Production of Space* (the latter, published in 1974, is one of Lefebvre's most important theoretical works). True to his consistent historicist and left-wing stance, Lefebvre makes a metaphorical yet apt distinction between the city and urban society. On the one hand, he reveals how our everyday space is eroded by abstract space dominated by capital and the state; on the other, he treats "urban society," this new form, as a "concrete abstraction," hoping to build a new centrality at the spatial level and, on that basis, reshape politics and everyday life. Incidentally, the concept of "planetary urbanisation," first proposed in this book, borrowed Trantor, the city Isaac Asimov created in his science-fiction *Foundation* series, and in recent years it has become a new frontier in urban studies.

***For Space***

Author: Doreen Massey

Publisher:

Sage Publications Ltd

Publication date: March 2005

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Space-Doreen-B-Massey/dp/1412903629/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475248229&sr=8-1&keywords=For+Space)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930231749mRCbzsVY73yEltcv.jpg-w600)

Space is the keyword of the entire discipline of geography, and different scholars and schools read it in wildly divergent directions. The author's academic background can be placed at the intersection of Marxism and feminism (and was to some extent influenced by poststructuralist thought); this dual concern powerfully propelled her reflection on core concepts such as space. In this book, published in 2005, she hoped to construct a concept of space as method on relational foundations, and then use that concept to reactivate politics. The activation unfolds mainly in two directions: first, breaking the various hidden spatial fetishisms — for instance, the structuralists' veneration of space in fact led to the cancellation of space, while the postmodernists' rebellion against them merely produced another, equally shallow view of time-space; second, criticizing the essentialist dichotomy of place/space (the targets here include, but are not limited to, Heidegger, Yi-Fu Tuan, and the early-to-mid work of David Harvey), so as to reconstruct, on the basis of rejecting a "poetics of place," the "grounded connections" and "liveliness" inherent in the concept of space. This book isn't thick either, and likewise deserves reading and rereading. My most recent reread was in 2015, and it once again spanned a very broad time (and space): from Manchester in March to Chicago in April, then New York in May, and finally, completion in London in June. One more thing: the Chinese translation of this book is titled 《保卫空间》 ("In Defense of Space"), which is without a doubt a mistranslation — Massey mounted a deep critique of Althusser's structuralist framework and would hardly borrow his "experience" when it came to naming books. If Althusser's *For Marx* can still be translated with a word like "defend," Massey's feelings toward space go far beyond what "defense" can capture.

***The Great Urban Transformation***

Author: You-Tien Hsing

Publisher: Oxford University Press

Publication date: March 2010

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Great-Urban-Transformation-Politics-Property/dp/0199644594/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475248420&sr=8-1&keywords=The+Great+Urban+Transformation)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930231934ETSdOwAJPy4tBLeK.jpg-w600)

Lefebvre's and Massey's readings of concepts like the city and space happen mainly at the theoretical level; You-Tien Hsing's book forms a link from theory to the reality of China's urban transformation. Grounding herself in geography's concern with urban space and its transformation, the author draws on the methodology and theoretical material of sociology and anthropology, tells many urban stories, and offers many insights. The book's central argument is about how China's local governments themselves have been urbanized (the urbanisation of the local state): rather than saying the government drives urbanization to happen, it is better to say they use this spatial mechanism to realize their own ambitions in accumulation and territorialisation. Yet this process is not monopolized by local governments; other interested parties are interwoven in it, together forming a picture of reality too tangled to cut or sort. To keep the narrative logic smooth, the author first borrows Deleuze's account of territorialisation/deterritorialisation/reterritorialisation at the theoretical level, then relies in the empirical part on a spatial typology of China's urban transformation (a typology composed of urban cores, peripheral spaces, and township enclaves). Although the presupposed theoretical and spatial frameworks slightly compromise the depth of the narrative, on the whole this book is already enough to count as the most perfect introductory reading for understanding contemporary China's land politics and the transformation of its urban political economy.

***The Politics of the Encounter***

Author: Andy Merrifield

Publisher: University of Georgia Press

Publication date: April 2013

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Politics-Encounter-Urbanization-Geographies-Transformation/dp/082034530X/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475248499&sr=8-1&keywords=The+Politics+of+the+Encounter)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930232036OXpvfI7PmjTLFeNR.jpg-w600)

The book's core concern is whether the currently fashionable "right to the city" still works — and to what extent it remains effective. The context of this reflection is the wave of urban space-occupation movements, increasingly prevalent since 2010 and deeply fused with social media — from Tunisia to Egypt, from St Paul's Cathedral (London) to Zuccotti Park (New York). Faced with the disconnect between theory and practice, the author returns to the "dusty stacks" in search of a response. The main theoretical tool he finds is the aforementioned Lefebvrian distinction between the city and urban society: if the city is a historically formed spatial entity, then urban society should be regarded as a pure form — a form that has no shape of its own and can only be co-shaped by the human practices within it. In this sense, calling for the "right to the city" cannot bring the possibility of remaking political action; it is more likely to remain trapped within the "rights" myth constructed by capital and the state. By contrast, the "Philosophy of the Encounter" advocated by the late Althusser inherits several lines of thought from Marx's *Grundrisse* (Outlines of the Critique of Political Economy) as well as Deleuze's account in *The Logic of Sense*, offering a new possible way to build political action amid changing urban conditions — for example, this framework proves more effective than the "right to the city" framework in analyzing the Occupy Wall Street movement.

***Global Gentrifications***

Editors: Loretta Lees, Hyun Bang Shin & Ernesto López-Morales

Publisher: Policy Press

Publication date: January 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Global-Gentrifications-Uneven-Development-Displacement/dp/1447313488/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475248565&sr=8-1&keywords=Global+Gentrifications)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930232150AuJxgpmFnDsWPRTC.jpg-w600)

Gentrification is commonly rendered in Chinese as "gentrification," "gentryfication," or "middle-class-ification" (绅士化、士绅化、中产阶级化). Uninformed observers often assume that the spatial transformation the word denotes is a legitimate, "modern," commendable phenomenon, but in academic discussion it is an out-and-out pejorative. The term was first coined by the British geographer Ruth Glass in the 1960s to describe the process by which working-class neighborhoods in inner London were gradually taken over by the middle class. In current usage the concept keeps expanding — according to this book's authors, whenever urban space is transformed and that transformation is accompanied by the forced demolition and displacement of original residents, "gentrification" can be considered to have occurred; its core meaning is the worsening of social injustice in the process of spatial transformation. By this definition, the so-called "urban renewal," "spatial upgrading," "old-city renovation," "shantytown redevelopment," and "key village renovation" on urban-rural fringes now widely taking place in Chinese cities can mostly be brought into this framework for critical analysis.

This book expands the horizons of gentrification research at both the theoretical and the empirical levels, and it also reflects methodologically on Western urban theory's claims to universalism. "Comparative urbanism" has been clamoring for years in urban studies, but how exactly to do it remains a matter of dispute. Although gentrification is the book's analytical focus, its core purpose is actually to liberate urban theory from its comfort zone through careful case studies and dialogue among them, striving to break the hegemonic position of colonialist Western urban theory discourse. In this book the chapters are ordered alphabetically by the authors' surnames — an ingenious arrangement. Recommended reading order: Introduction – Conclusion – the empirical chapters on the regions that interest you.

***Cosmopolitanism and the Geographies of Freedom***

Author: David Harvey

Translators: Wang Zhihong & Xu Tailing

Publisher: Socio Publishing

Publication date: February 2014

![From: [books](http://digital.books.com.tw/sample/epub_reader/panel/item/0010624285/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930232413gZWdzpnOwmvC1AiN.jpg-w600)

If you were to pick the number one figure in geography today, David Harvey would surely win without controversy — the spectacle of his recent lecture tour in China speaks for itself. Harvey's transformation from standard-bearer of the quantitative revolution to titan of Marxist geography remains an unavoidable topic in the discipline's intellectual history, and the evolution of his thought since he began publishing critical geography works in 1973 forms another important narrative thread. Although *Capital* has always been Harvey's object of admiration and forms the undertone of nearly all his works, his thinking in concrete analysis and writing was never constrained by Marx's classic texts. He is very good at drawing nourishment from his critics, then transforming and internalizing it into his own argumentative structure. For example, in this book, guided by his political ambition — searching for the conditions of possibility for a revolutionary politics of transformation — Harvey fiercely attacks Kant's cosmopolitan vision and its geographical foundations, ultimately grounding his new cosmopolitan framework on Whitehead's process-based philosophy of nature, and highlighting the significance of relational theories of time-space (Lefebvre can also be counted in this theoretical orientation) for geographical discussion. His argument unfolds around neoliberalism, the geographical banality of evil, and basic geographical concepts such as space, place, and territory — all of which are very helpful for re-understanding and re-knowing geography; the concluding section's reconstruction of the concept of the state from a relational time-space angle is also valuable. The translation has a few minor flaws but is overall of high quality.

***Atlas: The Archaeology of an Imaginary City***

Author: Dung Kai-cheung (text) / Yang Zhiheng (illustrations)

Publisher: Linking Publishing

Publication date: June 2011

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_95708382052331338/?asin=9570838205&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=9570838205&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930232737Ng0ORZzVkUhT6H14.jpg-w600)

Spatializing time is probably the most attractive feature of this novel, which takes the Hong Kong map as its main body. The map is not the territory, yet changes in the map correspond to the stories within the territory — sometimes confirmation after the fact, sometimes prophecy. It is in this sense that the author can claim: "Fiction is the essence of every city, and a city's map is necessarily a novel that revises and disguises itself." But the novel's ambition doesn't stop there: its slices of time are set at indeterminate future moments, reconstructing the present time-space and society of Hong Kong through an "archaeology of the future." In this writing scheme, the map (or cartography), a "truth-seeking" symbolic system, becomes the vehicle carrying the "falsifying" intent of literary writing. The tension between seeking truth and faking it is further intensified through the use of geographical theory. In the novel's first part, if you only looked at the table of contents, you could hardly believe it is actually a novel: geographical concepts like counterplace, misplace, nonplace, extraterritoriality, utopia, and omnitopia fill the pages, leaving one utterly baffled. Only after reading the whole book and looking back does one realize the author's careful design: lurking between true and false, future and past, theory and reality is unease; what is uneasy is identity; and what is identified is the drifting "place." If Chan Koonchung's *Hong Kong Trilogy* tells the story of his generation of Hong Kong people, then the "V City Tetralogy," including this *Atlas*, portrays the shifting inner journey of Dung Kai-cheung's generation of Hong Kong intellectuals.

***Medieval Cities***

Author: Henri Pirenne

Translator: Chen Guoliang

Publisher: The Commercial Press

Publication date: July 2006

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E6%B1%89%E8%AF%91%E4%B8%96%E7%95%8C%E5%AD%A6%E6%9C%AF%E5%90%8D%E8%91%97%E4%B8%9B%E4%B9%A6-%E4%B8%AD%E4%B8%96%E7%BA%AA%E7%9A%84%E5%9F%8E%E5%B8%82-%E4%BA%A8%E5%88%A9%C2%B7%E7%9A%AE%E9%9B%B7%E7%BA%B3/dp/B00H4UIX3U/ref=sr_1_1?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1475248992&sr=1-1&keywords=%E4%B8%AD%E4%B8%96%E7%BA%AA%E7%9A%84%E5%9F%8E%E5%B8%82)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930232938jw2VavfU9eultP5X.jpg-w600)

The book's main aim is to narrate the rise of European cities, yet its core doesn't seem to be the city itself. In Pirenne's telling, the city is not the protagonist of social change; what really matters is commerce and the economy, and the city, as a by-product, is merely the natural outcome that flows forth under certain conditions. When the Muslims closed the Mediterranean in the 8th century, the Carolingian dynasty happened to step onto the historical stage — the two were no coincidence. The replacement of the Merovingian dynasty by the Carolingians marked the Franks' turn from a maritime people to an inland one: isolated, closed, without outlets; the true Middle Ages in fact began right then. But the economically darkest years of medieval Europe lasted barely more than a century before a new dawn arrived. From the 10th century, Western Europe entered a period of relative peace and stability; the birth rate rose sharply, and commerce and trade began to revive. The First Crusade in 1096 marked the final retreat of Islam; maritime trade gradually developed, further stimulating the urban economy. And so the "new Europe" gradually recovered the basic character of ancient Europe as an urban region: the burgher class kept growing while the nobility kept retreating; in the end, "the air of the city makes one free."

Had there been no attacks by the Islamic states and no blocking of Mediterranean traffic, Western Europe's economy would not have been so wretched in the early medieval period, and its cities would not have been so dilapidated. Correspondingly, without the reopening of the trade routes, the urban revival, the later Renaissance, and even the Industrial Revolution would all have been impossible. The medieval and the modern are in fact separated only by a thin line. History's course has never been interrupted; the undercurrents hidden beneath the apparent great gulfs are the true logic of social development. It's just that, without careful prospecting, almost no one notices those thin streams.

**《望春风》 (*Gazing at the Spring Wind*)**

Author: Ge Fei

Publisher: Yilin Press

Publication date: June 2016

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E6%9C%9B%E6%98%A5%E9%A3%8E-%E6%A0%BC%E9%9D%9E/dp/B01H3GF242/ref=sr_1_1?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1475249104&sr=1-1&keywords=%E6%9C%9B%E6%98%A5%E9%A3%8E)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930233039B6MdY3xheHcUER4v.jpg-w600)

This is a book about "hometown," but its climax comes at the moment when the hometown, amid raging urbanization, disappears. According to Ge Fei himself, the idea for the book grew out of the overflow of his *Jiangnan Trilogy*. Perhaps the story's frame still rests within the time-space system of that trilogy, but when the story ends, the hometown as a spatial entity also ends; the only thing left is this book. In the book, the first-person narrator — Zhao Boyu, a retired factory librarian — was born after the founding of New China and, from childhood, lived through the various shadows the changing times cast over the village: swept up in them were his mother's leaving home and his father's unnatural death. The encounters of his half lifetime are organized around characters and narrated by himself. The story flows slowly, on and on — through his leaving home, entering the factory, the marketization wave, the opening act of urbanization — all the way until he meets a hometown he can no longer recognize. Finally, it is Biantong Nunnery, a relic of old times renovated into his dwelling, that becomes the place where he writes the memories of his half life. Such a spatial metaphor lends itself to many readings: it is both the projection of hometown and memory onto present reality, and it can be seen as an on-the-ground utopia, representing the disappearance and inaccessibility of "hometown" at both the material and the conceptual levels. But whatever the reading, this realist story actually prompts us to reflect: in the process of urbanization, what have we gained, and what have we lost? At the moment of the hometown's disappearance, what are we nostalgic for, and what can we do? Personally, on the same theme of "nostalgia" amid urbanization, Ge Fei's book has far more chew and tenderness than Yan Lianke's "mystical realism" writing (for example, *The Explosion Chronicles*).

### **Book Reviews**

***Arrival City***

Author: Doug Saunders

Translator: Chen Xinhong

Publisher: Shanghai Translation Publishing House

Publication date: February 2012

![From: [doubanio](https://img3.doubanio.com/lpic/s7049011.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930233400VAO5LxvWaKMPejmu.jpg-w600)

**We Created a World That Constrains Us**

**By Yimin Zhao**

The development of the arrival city is a critical link in the process of urbanization: get it right and everything goes smoothly; get it wrong and conflicts multiply. This is the central idea Doug Saunders puts forward in *Arrival City: How the Largest Migration in History Is Reshaping Our World*. What is the arrival city? It is an enclave built in the city by rural migrants. It sits at the edge of urban space, yet it is the core zone of social change. The arrival city has its own ambition: it never sees itself as a slum and resigns itself to that fate. On the contrary, it provides new arrivals with a sea of work, opportunities for self-employment, a relational network of neighbors on all sides, and it maintains the balance between country and city — it is a converter, not a slum.

True, the poverty rate in these zones has never fallen to the level people hope for. But the poverty of arrival cities is precisely the mark of their success. As converters, they do not necessarily move toward the middle class and gentrification; their greatest effect is to provide people with the conditions that help residents climb the social ladder faster. Each time an earlier cohort of residents moves to a more upmarket neighborhood as their economic and social situation improves, the vacancies are inevitably filled by a new cohort of poor newcomers — the poverty rate stays the same, or even rises, but who can deny that this arrival city is no longer the one it was before?

The reason arrival cities become conspicuous phenomena — or "problems" (from the orthodox point of view) — is first of all inseparable from the people living in them. The "arrival" refers to the landing place of new migrants after they enter the city. No doubt, the city's new migrants must come from the countryside. So the arrival city is essentially still a spatial container in which a kind of identity transformation happens: villagers become urbanites. This is a multidimensional process covering economics, politics, and social relations. But in the end, the focus of our attention should be people.

As a journalist, Saunders explores this crucial link of migration with very delicate observation. He finds that in many countries the road home in fact constitutes an important part of the population's migration into cities. The return trip becoming a segment of the journey away from home sounds a bit incredible. The reason for this can be analyzed with the "chain migration" strategy Charles Tilly once proposed. When villagers who have left their native place decide to enter the city, what they must give up is not just the once unchanging seasonal routines, but a whole set of social customs and relational networks they have grown used to. They dare not sever themselves completely from the countryside; they dare not abandon the original network before establishing a solid urban social safety net. So they need to use the arrival city as a base to push the collective relocation and reconstruction of their social network. To maintain the old social network, they must preserve the most basic connection to their hometown — and this is accomplished through the road home, taken once or several times a year.

Besides population, the most important topic in the arrival city is land. Why did they choose here rather than elsewhere? What kind of force helped the new migrants seize this piece of land? According to Saunders's observation, land in arrival cities is basically state-owned wasteland and derelict land; the private seizure of state land to provide housing is an international phenomenon. But this phenomenon has become rarer in recent years — one reason is that property rights are increasingly clearly divided, the other is that available idle urban land is increasingly scarce.

When facing the influx of migrants, Chinese cities were not caught off guard the way other developing countries (India, Brazil, Mexico) were. At our urban edges there are plenty of villages waiting for urbanization to arrive, and the influx of outside migrants is precisely the first step of spatial transformation. Every village shelters a considerable number of migrants; they are willing to endure deficient infrastructure (of course, compared with home the facilities are already quite good) and willing to crowd into tiny houses to live — not because they love such a way of life, but because the future prospects are simply too wonderful, wonderful enough to persuade them to endure present hardship.

But it is here that China's urbanization parts ways further with other countries'. In almost every country where state-owned land is privately seized, matters end with the land being granted to the occupiers. Because city governments eventually realize: the guarantee of ownership is a core issue bearing on the future of the arrival city, and places lacking guarantees correspondingly lack the motivation to improve infrastructure and social structure. Such a grant means the new migrants become property owners, are swept into the economic system, and possess the seed capital for starting their own businesses. But in our urban villages, migrants own next to nothing. They cannot own the houses and land they live on, nor can they in the short term save enough money to give themselves a sufficiently solid livelihood security; thus their bond with the countryside will remain stiff and unbreakable for a long time.

Of course, not all cities choose to grant land ownership to residents; strong city governments take the initiative, pushing all kinds of good-looking so-called "slum redevelopment plans." These plans make spatial renewal their mission, yet they hide the social connotations of space without a trace. In the blocks after renovation, the most common sight is low-density middle-class housing dotted among squares and broad avenues. But what about the residents? Do the original inhabitants still have a chance to move back into these areas? Do they still have sufficient spaces for social interaction? Can they still maintain their once-tight social networks? Focusing only on the built environment while ignoring the meaning of social relations, such renovation destroys rather than enhances the vitality of the arrival city.

Arrival cities shape not only the present; they also leave thick traces in history. Saunders even declares outright: the Revolution in Paris was in essence the revolution of arrival-city residents. In *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte*, Marx criticized the smallholders for lacking class consciousness, arguing that they understood politics less than urban workers and thus caused the failure of the revolution. In fact, Marx too was obscured. He neglected careful observation of the revolutionary subject, failed to notice that arrival-city residents made up the absolute majority in the revolutionary process, and paid no attention to the demands these residents were making through revolution.

Nothing under the sun is ever new. The Iranian Islamic Revolution of the 1970s was in essence the same: with arrival-city residents as the vanguard and main force, it successfully overthrew one dynasty and built another extremist regime in its place. The roots of these revolutions lie in a crisis of interests: social and economic progress is often achieved at the cost of crushing the interests of vulnerable groups, and arrival-city residents are usually the first to bear the brunt. When the Shah of Iran launched the White Revolution, trying to use economic growth to resist the danger of going red, he surely never imagined that what would defeat him in the end was not some leftist, but merely a group of new migrants living at the city's edge.

The White Revolution did achieve notable results: cities including Tehran took on a fresh look, which in turn attracted large rural populations to flood in. But these migrants quickly fell into disappointment. They found that the city's beauties had nothing to do with them. No fine apartments, no high-paying jobs — what they faced was only a wasteland called the Shrine of Isa. When they reluctantly settled in it, the king began to panic. He feared the migrants, worried about social instability — and so the arrival cities were bulldozed, and the migrants finally had nowhere to go. And so in the end, a cleric named Khomeini wrote a weighty page in history.

The connection between arrival cities and politics is a thousand and one threads, by no means limited to 19th-century Paris or 1970s Tehran. In Turkey, arrival cities are called "gecekondu," meaning places settled at night. The land of Istanbul's largest gecekondu once belonged to a prince of the last dynasty; though nationalized after the revolution, it ultimately could not withstand the pace of private encroachment. In such areas, the power structure is no longer formal but is dominated by militarized strategies and underworld connections. This kind of power structure kept evolving and eventually formed a brand-new political force, triggering a riot of enormous scale. The riot did not succeed, but neither was it in vain. After it ended, the city government began to face up to the existence of the gecekondu and its problems, granting residents ownership of houses and land, thereby drawing them into the economic system to form a community of interests. The city government also began to pay attention to infrastructure in arrival cities and invested substantial resources in it.

The gecekondu did not then develop tamely. Their political interest remained strong, and small political parties took root one after another. When the secular government could no longer keep the economic and social situation under control, these opposition parties shot up. Among them was a small party called the Virtue Party, from which later split an organization called the Justice and Development Party. Still later, the leader of that organization — Erdoğan, the descendant of migrants born and raised in a gecekondu — was elected Prime Minister and then President of Turkey, and has been re-elected to this day.

Paris's "arrival city" neighborhoods are likewise profoundly changing the city's landscape and social relations. Under the ghost of Le Corbusier, these migrant settlements in Paris's northern suburbs are all Cartesian communities: low building density, public spaces reduced to zones of crime, residents unable to build communities of communication and mutual aid — drift thus becomes the core theme running through migrant life. Because of "zip-code discrimination" and the resulting lack of a sense of belonging and of fair opportunity, migrants struggle to find social networks connecting them to French society, and at the same time lose their connection to home. The end result was the Paris riots of 2005. The youths who took part in the riots said a line: "We are neither Africans nor Europeans" — another footnote to the spatiality and sociality of the arrival city.

The time and space of arrival cities keep developing and evolving. Faced with such an existence, evaluations of arrival cities diverge enormously: some see them as the source of all evils, but others believe this place is the very root of the city's vitality. Judged by expectations, the existence of arrival cities is a necessary condition for the upward mobility of the floating population, so their significance is immense — this is the thesis the author has consistently held; but judged by present conditions, the infrastructure and living environment of arrival cities are deplorable, so they must be cleaned out — this, presumably, is what our city governments firmly believe. Measures of every stripe — "urban-rural fringe renovation," "clearing out low-end industries," "reducing the floating population" — are currently in full swing in certain megacities. The authorities rarely stop to think: of the wretched state of these arrival places, how much is the responsibility of space and the migrants, and how much is their own?

Arrival cities are never the terminus. In the carefully planned journey of social mobility for rural migrants, entering the arrival city is only the first step. In the author's view, "They have staked their whole lives, sacrificing the income and peace of an entire generation — certainly not merely to exchange one form of poverty for another. Arrival-city residents do not consider themselves 'the poor,' but successful urbanites who must first pass through this stage of poverty, which may last as long as a generation." (p. 278) So what resources do arrival cities and the migrants within them need for their development? The author proposes four essentials: land ownership, business opportunities, an autonomous economy, and mutual-aid social networks. Whatever way you summarize them, the most important goal is to provide new migrants with "access" — that is, the tools and opportunities Amartya Sen says are needed to become true citizens. Whether these recommendations work can only be verified in the future through more empirical investigation.

Although the book has more storytelling than theoretical exploration, the journalist's sharp perspective and the richness of the material are already enough to open a new window for observing urban change on a global scale. Here, whatever your position and whatever observation angle you choose, the discussion of spatial issues — especially the issue of arrival cities — should follow the method R. E. Park reminded us of: spatial structure and social relations are inseparably interconnected — a method this book practices with great clarity.
