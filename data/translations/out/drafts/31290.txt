**In 2011, Jeff Hammerbacher, a former Facebook data scientist, told Bloomberg Businessweek: "The best minds of my generation are thinking about how to make people click ads." Now the publishing industry is the same—the best minds are all thinking about how to make their books ride the internet's waves.**

**The trends come in many forms, and their ways of chasing them differ just as much. We have put together the stories of six book editors to see how these smart minds keep this old-fashioned industry thriving.**

The enduring popularity of *The Willpower Instinct* is something of a miracle.

Since its release in 2012, *The Willpower Instinct* has sold 2 million copies. It still ranked fourth in Amazon's 2015 annual book chart, marking its fourth consecutive year in the top ten. Quick-hit, bandwagon inspirational books keep flooding the market, yet this four-year-old title still holds the top spot in Amazon's "Emotional Intelligence and Emotions" category.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160822201341N0PZKUzkOBQV8vmC.jpg-w600)

*The Willpower Instinct* was written by Stanford psychology professor Kelly McGonigal, based on a course on "the science of willpower" she taught at the university. It explains how this emotion called "willpower" works and how to "harness it to improve your health, happiness, and productivity"; "people who have taken the course call it 'life-changing.'"

Books that promise a better life and a better future through psychological self-improvement are hardly rare. Inspirational psychology books are not only airport bookstores' favorites—they hold a prominent place in the book market as a whole. No organization has compiled specific market figures for this category, but according to [research](https://www.academia.edu/27751132/Chinas%5C_Self-Help%5C_Industry%5C_American%5C_ized%5C_Life%5C_Advice%5C_in%5C_China) by Dutch sociology postdoc Eric Hendriks on China's "self-help" book industry, the bulk of these are "supplementary education books," accounting for 34% of China's print book market.

Self-help books translated from the English-speaking world began pouring into the Chinese market more than a decade ago. In 2001, the phenomenal bestseller *Who Moved My Cheese?* topped that year's charts. The United States has always been a major exporter of self-help books and is China's largest source of imports after domestic inspirational titles (37% in 2011). By contrast, the genre is marginal in Europe—for example, [self-help books account for only 5.7% of the market in Germany](https://www.academia.edu/27751132/Chinas%5C_Self-Help%5C_Industry%5C_American%5C_ized%5C_Life%5C_Advice%5C_in%5C_China).

That self-help books are similarly popular in China and the US is no coincidence. With limited social welfare and a widening wealth gap, in fierce—even brutal—social competition, workers who are not smart enough, not hardworking enough, or not strong enough risk being eliminated quickly, and the hope of changing one's life falls on the "individual."

This is also the skill readers of *The Willpower Instinct* hope to acquire from it. The tagline promises "in just 10 weeks, successfully take control of your time and your life"—copy that may well have struck a chord with young people who dream of a life "always in perfect order, where he is the big winner of life."

### The Bestseller That Was a Category C Book

When she first came across *The Willpower Instinct*, Wang Zeyang thought her life was a mess.

In 2011, Wang Zeyang, an editor at Motie Book, sprained her ankle—she was wearing high heels—on her way to meet an author, and spent three months at home. A colleague suggested a book project to her: a popular Stanford course, "The Science of Willpower," and the companion book psychologist McGonigal had written for it.

Five or six years into her career, Wang Zeyang happened to be at a low point. She felt "so bored, one book after another," and even considered switching professions. In 2012, a small team she led "super-failed" and was dissolved. Reading the line "when you fall short, forgive yourself," she felt liberated from her habitual self-blame and decided to devote herself to this book.

Still, she and the publisher had little expectation for the book at first. The starting point of the project—prestigious university open courses—was far removed from the reasons it would later sell so well. "At the time I also felt the book wasn't particularly eye-catching; I just thought that since 2009 these university open courses had become really popular, and if we played up the university open course angle it should be OK, so we just treated it like a regular book, using the usual framework and approach," Wang Zeyang told QDaily.

Also, unlike Michael Sandel's then hugely popular Harvard *Justice* lectures, McGonigal's course had not yet been introduced to China and had not racked up 10 million online views, so the odds of riding the open-course wave to a bestseller were slim.

Based on sales expectations, Motie internally classifies books into categories A, B, and C before publication, assigning different levels of promotion and marketing. According to Wang Zeyang, A-category books are expected to sell 60,000 to 80,000 copies within six months of publication, B-category books 30,000 to 50,000, while *The Willpower Instinct* actually landed in the least-favored Category C—an advance royalty of only five or six thousand US dollars and a first printing of 11,000 copies, whereas publishers will typically print up to 50,000 copies of a book they are confident in.

Not to mention, *The Willpower Instinct* also faced a very similar competitor.

In 2012, CITIC Press released *Willpower*, by an American psychology professor at Florida State University, about how to "train your willpower." Its theme and content overlapped heavily with *The Willpower Instinct*—in fact, judging by the original English title of *The Willpower Instinct*, the key word in the main title is "willpower," not "self-control."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160822153438JjaAnsM8fzIGYeQ0.jpg-w600)

"That book really was one lots of people had been waiting for, because that expert is really impressive—a big name in American psychology. Theirs hit the market slightly before ours, about a week earlier. That actually worked in our favor: we figured, since there's a similar book out, we'll just model ourselves on it—whatever that book did, we did," Wang Zeyang said. *The Willpower Instinct* also sold well in its home market, ranking 12th among self-help books in Amazon's "Time Management" category.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160822153449p509TOBuYSNRrqez.jpg-w600)

The free-riding, same-category competitor marketing strategy went very smoothly. "When neither of the two books had cracked Amazon's top 100, they would get some promotional push and we would follow. Later they got into the 70s and we got into the 80s; they reached the 50s and we reached the 60s," Wang Zeyang said.

*The Willpower Instinct* soon left *Willpower* behind, its popularity far exceeding the editor's expectations. It hit No. 1 on Amazon's chart and stayed there for nearly seven or eight months. "At the time sales were roughly one to two thousand copies a day... we couldn't reprint fast enough. That's how hot it was."

Wang Zeyang believes luck played a part in *The Willpower Instinct*'s success. "Sometimes when you sum up a success it may not even be fair," she says—she still can't quite say what the fundamental reason for the bestseller was.

*The Willpower Instinct*'s edge may simply be that it fit the mold of a bestseller better—simple, easy to read, lively. "We found a translator and tried to make the translation as easy to read as possible," Wang Zeyang said.

Judging from reader feedback, compared with *Willpower*'s more theoretical, academic style, *The Willpower Instinct* seemed to more promptly satisfy readers' urgent desire for change. Each chapter of *The Willpower Instinct* comes with two kinds of assignments, short "deep dives" and "willpower experiments," promising that "these strategies, based on scientific research or theory, can genuinely improve your willpower so you can meet the challenges of real life."

On Douban, the review with the most "helpful" votes came from Douban celebrity "Shuimei Wuyu," and it is fairly representative—"What I care about most is that it has an actual practical manual; no matter how good the theory is, if it can't change my current situation it's useless."

The author's identity and looks as a woman also lent endorsement and bonus points. Motie had a TVO interview with the author translated into Chinese, put it on Amazon's purchase page, and reposted it on Weibo. Although Wang Zeyang says they never thought of using a female author as a selling point—which might seem "not authoritative enough"—what people wanted was never an authoritative "old professor." "A great beauty just looks like she has a lot of willpower," one reader commented.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160822153611IR2KaTpkqvscwyWH.JPG-w600)

### Once It Sells, the Resources Follow Quickly

This past March, Motie published the third book in the series, *The Willpower Instinct: Making Friends with Stress*. The book was reclassified as a Category A title, meaning Motie would pour substantial planning and marketing resources into it.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160822153637q7OfjYEG1JdgcmVD.jpg-w600)

Although *The Willpower Instinct*'s success came as a surprise, once Motie saw the early sparks of it catching fire, its marketing kept pace quickly—the publisher monitors data daily, which helps book planners adjust marketing strategy in time.

Wang Zeyang and her team began targeting students preparing for graduate school entrance exams. *The Willpower Instinct* came out in late July 2012, and "by early September its 'No. 1' spot would slowly be squeezed out by all the exam-prep and review books," so Wang Zeyang devised a move—combine exam prep with willpower. Using the tone and perspective of a graduate school applicant, they posted on Renren, which was still hot at the time: "My attention is scattered, I can't control myself—what to do? Then I found a good book, and its ideas really helped." The post resonated with many test-takers and was widely shared.

Targeting students and white-collar workers as readers, online stores were *The Willpower Instinct*'s main sales channel. Wang Zeyang said many people probably just went straight to an online store to buy it after reading the post.

Another example: writing reading notes themselves. For this kind of classic American bestseller packed with cases, the main thread is actually simple—a few slides of PPT can sketch out the key ideas—so a colleague of Wang Zeyang's at Motie posted a long note on Douban to help readers read it efficiently.

After six months of sales, Motie wanted to press the advantage while the first book's momentum lasted, but they couldn't wait for the hen to lay eggs.

Wang Zeyang emailed the author McGonigal, hoping to sign her second book. McGonigal replied that she was still working on ideas. So Wang Zeyang searched Amazon "to see if there was anything usable in the meantime" and discovered that McGonigal had published an old book on how to do yoga back in 2009.

Beyond sharing the same author, this yoga guide had little to do with *The Willpower Instinct*, and the editors felt "awkward" about it, but if the second book was to keep selling, Wang Zeyang believed it still needed to be tied to the author McGonigal—and to "willpower." How to get from willpower to yoga? Wang Zeyang considered that *The Willpower Instinct* has a dedicated chapter on yoga, exercise, and meditation—"it's basically an extension of that content." In the end Motie forcibly strung the two books together with the line: "When her willpower runs low, she does three things—exercise, meditation, and yoga."

In May 2013, *The Willpower Instinct 2: The Yoga Workbook* (《自控力 2：瑜伽实操篇》) was published, and this time they rolled up their sleeves and classified it as a Category A book.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160822153719cUDuXdge1qLWv9TP.jpg-w600)

Two years later, in May 2015, the author's new book *The Upside of Stress* was published in the US. Since the first book came out, Motie had kept up an email correspondence with McGonigal, and in early 2015 they signed the rights contract for the new book.

Wang Zeyang worried a bit that sticking with the words "The Willpower Instinct" might not boost sales. *The Willpower Instinct 2: The Yoga Workbook* had just sold past 100,000 copies, and too many books of the same type had left people weary. For example, *Emotional Self-Control* and *Super Self-Control* came out in 2013, and by 2014 there were more: *Emotional Self-Control: The Power to Turn the Negative Into the Positive* and *Learn a Little Super Self-Control Every Day*.

Still, after "seven or eight months of brainstorming titles," Wang Zeyang and her team couldn't come up with a better selling point. In the end, this book about how to turn stress into a good thing was reluctantly listed once again as a *The Willpower Instinct* sequel, published this past March under the title *The Willpower Instinct: Making Friends with Stress*.

Sales of *The Willpower Instinct: Making Friends with Stress* passed 170,000 by this August. While 170,000 is a solid result for an ordinary book, it falls far short of the first book in the series.

### Every Book Can Be Developed Into a Course

*The Willpower Instinct*'s continued bestseller status still hasn't freed "mass-market book" editor Wang Zeyang from professional burnout.

Since 2007 she has worked on mass-market books—the "very, very commercial" kind that "make money off the masses." In 2009 she joined Motie and produced the first million-copy bestseller of her career, *Knock Down the Wall in Your Mind* (《拆掉思维里的墙》).

But now Wang Zeyang believes "there has never been a market called 'the masses.'" On one hand, she feels readers' interests come and go like the wind—"someone around you reads a book, and then everyone reads that book." This is also borne out by the fact that the latter two *Willpower Instinct* books could not reproduce the first book's success. Perhaps you can do research or spot new trends, but truly understanding what readers are thinking is almost impossible. On the other hand, Wang Zeyang says she has noticed far fewer blockbuster bestsellers appearing in recent years. "Before, books that could easily sell a million copies would come out several a year, but these past two years you might get one a year. Books that sell over a million are few and far between."

Wang Zeyang's claim is not entirely accurate. Since 2014, the market has produced million-copy and even multi-million-copy sellers such as *The Miracles of the Namiya General Store*, *Be Good, Pat on the Head* (《乖，摸摸头》), *Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future*, *The Storied Life of A.J. Fikry*, *Everything I Never Told You*, and *Yu Zui* (《余罪》)—blockbusters are hardly fading. Still, the several editors QDaily interviewed did all mention that "the book market has become more diverse and segmented."

Some book editors believe the better approach now is to make books for "a certain group of people" rather than "the masses." They have higher loyalty, tastes that are easier to capture, and their internal word-of-mouth spreads more effectively—more helpful for a book editor in keeping a career "in perfect order." Wang Zeyang holds the same view: "It's hard to say there's one big trend that sums up how the whole market is doing... it's all small markets, small trends."

Continuing the *Willpower Instinct* line, Wang Zeyang went on to plan practical, skills-oriented books including *This Strange Life* (《奇特的一生》) and *Two Awesome Hours*. In her view, these books target people "with procrastination, who always stay up late, who need time management, who seek self-improvement."

In this field she has also found a new professional positioning: targeting "growth addicts" as users, she has transformed from a mere book editor into a "product manager." "I really hope to try next—maybe not just books, but also courses, communities, or other derivative products. Distilling many aspects into a complete value chain, being the person who runs the whole operation," Wang Zeyang said.

This shift has already begun. Wang Zeyang added a QR code to the back cover of *The Willpower Instinct*; by scanning it, roughly seven or eight hundred readers initially joined the "Willpower" WeChat group. She had planned to use this for more genuine reader research—"sometimes the readers we see, or the research we do, turns up exactly the results you wanted, which is really a form of self-deceiving research." The right approach, she believes, is "to go out of your way to truly be friends with readers."

The WeChat group is one channel for promoting new books to loyal readers. When *The Willpower Instinct: Making Friends with Stress* came out, Wang Zeyang sent gifts to group members using the book—"everyone's reading it."

But these days the community business isn't just about selling books. In 2015 she helped build a WeChat official account called "Willpower Lab," attracting readers to "start with willpower, and through reading-and-writing, meditation, and exercise boot camps, grow together into better versions of ourselves." The account now has 13,000 followers.

"Willpower Lab" comes in two forms, a "core group" and a "public-welfare group." Put simply, the difference is that the core group charges fees, with "teachers lecturing and giving guidance, and group leaders monitoring." Currently the core group offers three courses—meditation, exercise, and reading-and-writing—each running 10 weeks and priced at 399 yuan per session. The Willpower Lab's WeChat store also offers a "premium" reading-and-writing course at 999 yuan; if a student completes all assignments in the reading group, 200 yuan of the tuition is refunded.

The "teachers" in the core group are mainly Wang Zeyang and her friends; the "group leaders" are students who "stand out," and students check in regularly and turn in assignments. "For example, did you exercise today? If not, send out a red envelope," Wang Zeyang said.

In the "public-welfare group," though there are no teachers or group leaders monitoring, members share their plans for the day and a brief summary every morning.

A user named "Hand Me That Deuterium" posted today's plan—"K3 fat-burning," "Getting started with Lai Shixiong's English," "drafting a personal study plan," and so on—and has kept it up for 50 days. He found the Willpower Lab group through an official account about "growth," feels this format is pretty good, and says it somewhat eases his procrastination.

As the inverse of turning open courses into books, Wang Zeyang is bullish on the prospect of turning books into community reading. "A product manager on my team told me he thinks every book can become a Willpower Lab, or a course. Actually every book can be developed into a course—the editors just haven't realized it," she said.

Obviously, though, internet entrepreneurs including Luogic Show have realized it.
