Backed by indie studios and streaming services, it has once again taken on meaning beyond the genre — exactly suiting audiences in crisis

> No live organism can continue for long to exist sanely under conditions of absolute reality; even larks and katydids are supposed, by some, to dream. Hill House, not sane, stood by itself against its hills, holding darkness within; it had stood for eighty years and might stand for eighty more. Within, walls continued upright, bricks met neatly, floors were firm, doors were sensibly shut; silence lay steadily against the wood and stone of Hill House, and whatever walked there, walked alone.
>
>
> *The Haunting of Hill House*, Shirley Jackson

Two things happened this October.

On October 12, Netflix released a horror series of the same name adapted from Shirley Jackson's 1959 novel *The Haunting of Hill House*, scoring 9 on IMDb and 93% on Rotten Tomatoes. Though it looks like genre fare about a haunted house, the show plays more like *This Is Us* — a family drama set against a gothic-horror backdrop. With no cheap scares, it builds from character, telling a story of trauma, PTSD, and family bonds; ingeniously plotted, stunningly shot, and with a lingering emotional aftertaste, nearly every outlet has praised it as "possibly Netflix's best original series."

A week later, *Halloween* opened. It is a direct sequel to the 1978 original *Halloween* — the film that, forty years ago, launched the slasher subgenre that remains popular to this day. Made for just $10 million, it took $77.5 million at the North American box office in its opening weekend, second only to Sony's big-budget *Venom*, which broke the record for an October opening. Amid the mainstream critical praise, the most attention went to "scream queen" Jamie Lee Curtis's return forty years on, and the female force in a new era of horror became a hot topic.

Before that, you had already heard the awards-season buzz around Luca Guadagnino's remake of *Suspiria*, perhaps had your appetite whetted early by *The Nun*, binge-watched *Castle Rock* — that grab-bag of Stephen King fiction — over the summer, sought out A24's *Hereditary* and the smash hit *A Quiet Place* on reputation, gone back to the source material to prepare for *It*, joined the plot discussions around *Get Out*, which earned an Oscar nomination for Best Picture, and possibly been watching since 2013 as James Wan's *The Conjuring* and *Insidious* series spun off their own universes amid mainstream acclaim…

Looking back over the past five years, horror film and television — once the preserve of a niche audience — has drawn increasing attention from the mainstream media. Varied in subject matter, it no longer aims solely at making viewers' adrenaline spike; compared with comic-book adaptations that take half the box office, it hardly comes off second best, sneaking into the year's top 10 grosses at remarkably low cost and attracting a growing audience. This is no illusion: by Box Office Mojo's count, at least at the box office, powered by *It* and *Get Out*, [2017 became the strongest year in horror history](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/10/26/movies/top-horror-movies-box-office-it-get-out.html).

Horror has never lost its audience; there is always some film that breaks through in one way or another and opens up a category, or a golden age — Universal's monster universe half a century ago, George Romero's invention of the zombie genre in the 1960s, the slasher films of the 1980s represented by *A Nightmare on Elm Street* and *Friday the 13th*, the found-footage wave inspired by *The Blair Witch Project* in the 1990s, and the ingenious trap-and-torture variety led by *Saw* after the turn of the century… And yet, the last time a pure horror film won mainstream recognition and huge box-office success dates back to 1973: *The Exorcist* alone carried that year's horror grosses and became, the following year, the first horror film to take the Oscar for Best Picture. For decades afterward, most horror retreated to B-movie videotape territory, embraced only by a "hardcore gore" audience.

![*[*The Exorcist*](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/1495286695/)* (1973)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201810301118302gGelKwNVfHT4q1I.jpg-w600)

The treatment horror film and TV receives today hardly looks like just another subgenre enjoying brief, superficial popularity; it resembles a horror renaissance, paying homage to the sober 1970s *The Exorcist*, Roman Polanski's layered *Rosemary's Baby* from fifty years ago, the aesthetically extreme *Suspiria*, and *Alien*, a masterclass in the psychological thriller. At the same time, against today's turbulent backdrop, it has donned the garb of real-world issues.

That this situation has come about is no surprise; it even feels inevitable.

### A high return on investment keeps the horror category evergreen

Horror movies are the best way for "the poor" of the film world to make quick money. Your core audience wants only one thing — to be scared. They don't mind watching unknown actors, so you needn't blow your budget on stars; the more realistic the setting, the more it resonates, so you shoot in basically one or a handful of locations, or entirely indoors, with no need to scout locations; and you don't need fancy special effects — nearly everything can be done with makeup, models, and buckets of fake blood. You only have to handle the cleanup afterward, mopping up the ketchup or the honey dyed red.

[A survey of the profitability of US theatrical releases from 1996–2016](https://stephenfollows.com/profitable-horror-movies/) found that 53% of horror films turned a profit — the highest of all genres (adventure, romance, musical, action, comedy, suspense, drama, dark comedy, western) and far above the industry average of 37%. [Data cited by The New York Times](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/45185.html) shows that in pure return-on-investment (ROI) terms, horror ranks second only to documentaries among all genre films, averaging a 6x ROI at the North American box office and as much as 12x worldwide.

The all-time ROI record is held by a horror film — the box-office myth of *The Blair Witch Project* lifts everyone's spirits. Posed as footage shot by missing students, it cost just $35,000, yet viral marketing with a "based on true events" tag helped it gross $250 million, a budget-to-revenue ratio of 1:7094. Its mock-documentary form also launched the found-footage category — meaning grainy image quality, shaky cameras, hyper-everyday settings, amateur actors, and precious few scares all suddenly had a legitimate reason to exist. Follow-ups like *[REC]* and *Paranormal Activity* used the same tricks to keep earning astonishing returns.

![*[*Night of the Living Dead*](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/453911314/)* (1968)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030112340miRzKhPtJ2ZIoVuL.jpg-w600)

For independent filmmakers, this is the most realistic path: do everything yourself, build a following among audiences, and eventually win over potential investors. George Romero, who shot *Night of the Living Dead* on a budget raised from friends (it grossed $30 million worldwide), and Peter Jackson, who made his debut *Bad Taste* as a weekend hobby with friends over dozens of weekends (the film sold distribution rights to buyers from 12 countries at the 1987 Cannes Film Festival), no doubt know the feeling.

Nobody dislikes a profitable business. Apart from a handful of pioneering innovators, the studio system mass-produced genre-film consumer goods; even when the plots were hackneyed, formulaic, and cheap, they turned a profit thanks to rock-bottom costs. In the 1980s and 1990s, when slashers ruled, large numbers of horror films skipped theaters entirely and went straight to the home-video market — freed from cinema revenue shares and strict rating constraints, loyal horror fans and mainstream viewers looking for an occasional thrill provided steady income.

In the internet age, streaming took the home-video slot, and the rise of the Netflixes resembles something of a revival of B-movie studios. Relatively low budgets, skipping theaters and censorship, and experimental exploration within films happen to fit the established model of streaming original content.

For traditional studios, meanwhile, investing in horror is always a safe fallback. [As The New York Times has analyzed](https://www.nytimes.com/2017/05/11/magazine/the-scarily-profitable-hits-of-jason-blum.html?mtrref=www.google.com), a standard commercial tentpole today costs around $200 million, typically plus a $100 million marketing budget; that limits how many films a studio can make in a year and leaves it unable to afford failure — one reason they keep leaning on sequels and reboots to stay safe. Horror films barely have such worries: keep the budget under control, draw people with the hooks of each subgenre, target a fixed niche audience, even settle for a small release on a few hundred screens — and once good word of mouth carries it to a broader mainstream audience, the returns are even richer. Even a well-crafted horror film needn't have a scary budget: *The Conjuring* cost $20 million and grossed over $300 million worldwide — and then you can make a sequel.

![*[*The Conjuring*](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/2016102311/)* (2013)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030112615c8SpZCN3gOIr9Qsi.png-w600)

Either way, it is a business that cannot lose. And with quantity assured, it is almost inevitable that some works will stray from the genre, probe its boundaries, and pursue quality.

It is precisely this kind of business that created today's horror king, production company Blumhouse.

### Exploring the horror category with an indie-development mindset

The 48-year-old Jason Blum is one of Hollywood's hottest figures. CEO of production company Blumhouse (you probably remember their title card: a little girl in a white dress walking past dark-green walls, giving timid viewers their first scare before the film even starts), he is the maker of several box-office myths. Behind nearly every hit horror film of the past decade that people remember is Blumhouse money — *Paranormal Activity*, *The Purge*, *Insidious*, *Sinister*, *Happy Death Day*, *Get Out*, and most recently *Halloween*…

A strategy of low cost, high output, and genre focus has kept Blumhouse profitable and made Blum willing to give filmmakers more room to experiment. Blum, who once worked for the Weinstein brothers and served as an indie producer for Warner Bros., was not a horror fan when he entered the business, but he holds an unusual attitude distinct from the "quick money" mindset.

[Asked by THR why he is drawn to horror](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/features/jason-blum-rebooting-halloween-working-weinstein-1152748), Blum replied, "What attracts me to horror movies is that I genuinely love independent film. Truly independent film is extremely hard to develop, and indie work has now basically merged with television. I discovered the main reason I love the horror category while producing *Sinister* — the challenges it poses are the same as making a great independent film. You ask the director: if you cut out all the scary sequences, would this film still get into Sundance? Would it still achieve the same effect? Would the storytelling still work?"

"The key to a good horror film is not horror, not scares, not gore — it is story. The same key as any other good film: story and dramatic conflict… I don't ask, 'Will this make a lot of money?' I ask, 'Does it feel like a good independent film?'"

He cites *Sinister* as an example: the film is really about a man whose career has hit a ceiling, a protagonist who puts career ahead of family and therefore does not hesitate to place his loved ones in danger. "I love using this genre to deliver some remarkable, distinctive stories to a wider audience."

![*[*Sinister*](https://img1.doubanio.com/view/photo/raw/public/p1898409907.jpg)* (2012)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030112925m2grAncfzHZTaCLb.jpg-w600)

With the same attitude, Netflix let Mike Flanagan create the *The Haunting of Hill House* that opens this article. Strip away the horror elements and it is a superb family drama telling the most moving character stories; the extended ensemble long takes and flashback transitions in episode six show the creators' hard work toward the best possible effect. This is exactly the same motivation as James Wan's camera "showboating" in confined spaces in his films — breaking free of the mass-production horror mold and committing to "making a good film."

Blum's eye for investment decides the fate of those independently minded horror films getting made, while his professional-producer thinking weighs the strategies that help profitability: every film's budget must stay under $5 million, while sequels derived from existing IP can be generously funded to $15 million. He wants to see what happens when the Blumhouse system is applied to IP development — "to see whether we can impose an innovation system on a franchise."

The result is that virtually every Blumhouse work you can name has a sequel. It is safe enough, follows the same indie-development thinking, costs far less than a tentpole, and Blumhouse has long since secured a healthy cash flow thanks to breakout hits with astonishing ROI.

Blumhouse's indie sensibility has set the tone of today's horror mainstream. "Beyond scares, it must say something else" has become a near-mandatory element for any horror film seeking mainstream recognition, and they are also looking for chances to innovate — subversive, anti-genre attempts like *The Cabin in the Woods*, or the mining of cleverer ideas like *Don't Breathe* and *Lights Out*. Scouting independent filmmakers has proceeded in parallel.

![*[*Lights Out*](https://movie.douban.com/subject/26684564/)* (2016), adapted from David F. Sandberg's horror short. After directing this feature, Sandberg entered the mainstream horror market, earned the chance to direct *Annabelle: Creation*, and jumped up again to shoot Warner Bros.' superhero film *Shazam*!](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030113409zZUgTiNQOVnGRSwr.jpg-w600)

Another driver of this approach is that audiences have grown increasingly desensitized to the traditional horror recipe. Each subgenre has matured too far, its patterns fixed; some franchises have numbers past five after their titles, and within the traditional frame there is little fresh to excite people. Some also point out that people are now surrounded by real-world evil — "We live in an age where real violence and death are playing out on Facebook and YouTube. Isn't a desensitized audience the more worrying thing?"

Fleeting, superficial scares no longer satisfy; people crave genuine fear — they need to be given something that stimulates at a deeper level.

### What scares you now?

Creators and audiences have reached a consensus on deeper stimulation — perhaps without ever comparing notes, but sharing a turbulent, unsettling era, they have at least arrived at a similar vantage point on fear. After all, the job of a top-tier horror writer is to touch the dread buried in the subconscious and probe the questions society refuses to discuss, and this is mostly built on shared trauma.

Stephen King is a writer who hands out trauma.

A new generation of creators grew up in the shadow of Stephen King's work and quickly found that it has not gone out of date. The insular small towns cut off from modernity, and the childhoods in which kids learn the world's evil too early, apply just as well to contemporary issues — religion, addiction, abuse, bullying, the evil of children; a forgotten, closed-off town left behind by America meets the worst that can happen, things take a sudden turn for the worse, and the protagonist is left without any hope…

Once creators realized they had a platform for expression, these shared traumatic experiences were fused into their work, producing films with genuine feeling. Andrés Muschietti, director of *It*, and Sam Shaw, co-creator of *Castle Rock*, have been King readers since childhood and were deeply influenced by him. Their interpretations of King's work easily resonate with readers, and have let people witness a second Stephen King renaissance, after the 1970s wave of *The Shining*, *Carrie*, and *Children of the Corn*.

![*[*It*](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/2498062412/)* (2017)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030114936fFi1LZWdIQmhKrRk.png-w600)

In the Cold War years when Stephen King's work first became popular, Americans feared communist infiltration and nuclear annihilation; what they fear now has changed, but seems to share similar qualities. [Shaw said](https://www.polygon.com/tv/2018/9/12/17847144/castle-rock-stephen-king-movies-tv-books-adaptations), "I think the world genuinely feels like an unstable, worrying place, so it doesn't surprise me at all that so many storytellers gravitate toward a genre that contains uncertainty, fear, and suspense."

[Film studies professor Stephen Prince of Virginia Tech commented](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2017/sep/14/horrorwood-will-the-new-golden-age-of-scary-movies-save-cinema), "Whether the world is actually more dangerous than before remains an open question, but people today feel themselves under greater threat than ever. Horror films give voice to the problems that keep haunting us — the fragility of the human body, our brittle sense of control over our own lives, and that murky question: what is a person? Why can't you trust other people to remain dependable?"

This doubt and anxiety over social problems has seeped into filmmakers' expression and, merged with the studios' indie-development approach, achieved maximum effect. When a racially themed *Get Out* earns an Oscar nomination; when *American Horror Story*, after six years of supernatural oddities, titles its seventh season *Cult* and in effect uses the election to depict political chaos; when *The Purge* evolves from the home-invasion format of the first film into political satire across the later three; when *Unfriended* unfolds entirely on a computer screen, stressing the theme of social-media bullying; and [when *Halloween* begins to discuss the "intergenerational trauma" women experience](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/10/26/movies/jamie-lee-curtis-halloween.html) — you can clearly perceive how the understanding of horror has changed. The reality you inhabit is the true source of horror, and artworks have begun, almost involuntarily, to explore race, immigration, gender, and nation; the exaggerated fear elements in fictional works have become an escape from reality — compared with true events, fake blood and ghosts are not worth mentioning, and can even bring joy.

![*[*American Horror Story: Cult*](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/2496915865/)* (2017)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030115251BpvDb20ZfWXdEVu3.jpg-w600)

A broadening of subject matter naturally draws mainstream audiences' attention. The horror audience is no longer limited to oddly-tasted "freaks." In fact, female attendees at horror festivals are on the rise, [nearly reaching a 4:6 ratio with men](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2017/sep/14/horrorwood-will-the-new-golden-age-of-scary-movies-save-cinema), thanks largely to horror's female characters becoming more diverse: no longer the "flirty victim" or the "virgin who survives to the end," they can be the mastermind or the avenging hero, shedding the scream-queen label entirely. [Media outlets have begun urging Hollywood to hire more female directors for horror films](https://www.theatlantic.com/entertainment/archive/2018/10/jason-blum-female-directors-horror-blumhouse/573571/); one should not simply assume "women don't want to direct this genre."

This is a horror market you have never seen before — carrying meaning beyond what the original genre could express, today's horror film and television caters to audiences in crisis, and creators' serious self-expression makes this once non-mainstream category increasingly worthy of being taken seriously. More and more people are seeing the things that truly frighten them.

Don't be afraid.
