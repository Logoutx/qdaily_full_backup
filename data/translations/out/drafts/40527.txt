The most elite, yet the most open

On the afternoon of April 27, Alphabet (Google) reported earnings. Neither of its two founders — CEO Larry Page and president Sergey Brin — was at the Mountain View headquarters.

Both were at the Vancouver Convention Centre for the annual TED conference.

Brin chatted with people at a round table on the waterfront side of the convention centre, politely deflecting the gaze of those who came up to strike a conversation. Not far away, Joe Belfiore, the Microsoft corporate vice president in charge of Windows design and software, strode briskly into the auditorium.

The last session of the afternoon was about to begin, and most attendees were already seated. A day earlier, Spielberg had also arrived at about the same time and only found an empty seat in the last row. Those who arrived earlier and sat in the front row included Chris Sacca, an early investor in Twitter and Uber, and Mark Bezos, the younger brother of Amazon's founder (this volunteer firefighter once gave a [hilarious TED talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/mark_bezos_a_life_lesson_from_a_volunteer_firefighter)).

All of them are regulars at the TED conference, going back more than a decade. No wonder World Bank president Jim Yong Kim, at the end of his talk introducing an investment plan for poor regions, waved his arm at the audience and said: "You sitting on trillions of dollars in cash — come find us!"

This year's TED conference was the largest ever, with 1,800 attendees. Setting aside sponsorships from big names like Google, Microsoft, Lululemon and BMW (which cannot be embedded in the talks), ticket sales alone exceeded $20 million: the lowest tier cost $8,500 apiece; 487 people paid $17,000 for Donor tickets that let them queue up earlier; and 65 people bought the $250,000 five-year Patron tickets.

And the ticket isn't even easy to buy. To purchase one, you have to answer five questions online, such as "How would your friends describe your accomplishments?" and "What are you fanatical about?", and at the end leave contact details for two referees for TED to run background checks.

Starting next year, the base ticket rises to $10,000 apiece, and the second tier to $25,000.

What do people come here to do, having paid this much?

Like every high-barrier conference, TED is a good place to network. Attendees' badges clearly state their name, title and company. The biggest stars in the room are the speakers; many of them, previously known only within their professional field, are approached nonstop the moment they step off the stage.

The organizers arranged a series of ice-breaking activities. For instance, Monday's lunch was a six-person picnic basket that could only be claimed by groups of six. Or, between sessions, two people at a time could take BMW-sponsored electric cars for a spin around Vancouver, chatting as they went.

There were themed dinners every day — some open to booking by everyone, some by invitation only, and others organized by companies or individuals. One small circle was divided into many smaller circles.

TED even prepared a dedicated app, TEDConnect, where you can see this year's speakers and attendees and send them messages — essentially LinkedIn, restricted to TED attendees.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170507093818KbOrusWqoS9CnpDL.png-w600)

Unlike other high-barrier conferences, TED attendees spend most of their daytime not socializing — they actually listen to the talks. More than 1,400 people attend nearly every session.

Like the hundreds of millions of TED viewers around the world, they watch TED talks ranging from 5 to 18 minutes — Elon Musk was the exception this year, taking part in a [40-minute conversation](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/40345.html).

The difference is density. These 1,800 people watch 111 TED talks over four and a half days, totaling 28 hours including the transition segments.

"You need to prepare for TED — you have to exercise every day and eat a lot." Silicon Valley angel investor Chris Sacca told QDaily at a party the night before the conference that TED was the most intense of all the conferences he attends. He hasn't missed a single one since 2005.

For the following four and a half days, you could see Sacca in a baseball cap sitting in the front row with his wife Crystal Sacca at almost every session.

TED made many preparations for this marathon. Every morning, attendees could choose one of yoga, running or tai chi, led by coaches to lift their spirits — one morning even featured a 4:30 a.m. hike up a mountain to watch the sunrise.

If you feel constrained sitting in the main auditorium, the lounge areas outside the theater are filled with big screens playing the live feed.

![Audience members watching a talk on a screen outside the main auditorium](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170507100855R63mMLCU45O2xyIV.jpg-w600)

The snacks at the venue are also exceptionally abundant and healthy: cricket protein bars, salmon jerky, kale chips, seaweed sheets labeled non-GMO... even the desserts are marked gluten-free. The two coffee shops are independent local brands from Vancouver and Seattle respectively. Everything is free.

Throughout the venue, only the occasional red bottle of Coca-Cola reminds you that you haven't wandered into some hipster fantasy world.

But none of this is enough to ease the pressure of attending TED. A law firm partner who has come for eight or nine consecutive years said that by the fourth day he could no longer remember what had been said in the first two days. More than one attendee said they realized, while exchanging contact details with someone, that they had already chatted in the first two days.

It isn't hard to understand why. The information density is too high, the range of fields too wide, and nearly every talk tries to stir the audience's emotions.

Every year, the TED conference has one big theme, and each session has a smaller theme, composed of six or seven talks. But between individual talks, the topics and fields can have nothing whatsoever to do with each other.

Chess master [Garry Kasparov recalled how he lost to IBM's Deep Blue as the "last line of defense of the human brain"](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/40121.html). The members of OK Go came on stage together to [explain how their hit music videos were conceived](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/40136.html).

Computer science professor Stuart Russell [followed the logic of artificial intelligence through all the ways things could go wrong](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/40183.html) — the worst-case scenario being a household robot boiling a cat while preparing dinner.

Best-selling author Tim Ferriss, writer of *The 4-Hour Workweek*, introduced his methodology for overcoming fear and emerging from the shadow of suicide. Nobel laureate in medicine Elizabeth Blackburn talked about how different behaviors affect telomerase and, in turn, human lifespan. The executive director of the civil rights organization ACLU spent more than ten minutes on stage riffing on Trump with Renaissance paintings.

This year may be the one with the fewest animals ever on the TED stage. No one [released a jar of mosquitoes](https://www.ted.com/talks/bill_gates_unplugged), no one [led out an ox and asked the audience to guess its weight](http://blog.ted.com/of-oxes-and-the-wisdom-of-crowds-lior-zoref-at-ted2012/), and no one [brought out a real camel while introducing an archaeological discovery](https://www.ted.com/talks/latif_nasser_you_have_no_idea_where_camels_really_come_from). But someone strapped jet engines to his arms and flew like Iron Man — the test footage he showed on stage could be dropped straight into the next *Iron Man* movie. And someone else [had a robot dog deliver a can of soda](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/40183.html).

So why sit through so many utterly unrelated talks in a row?

Chris Anderson addressed this question at the first TED conference after he took over, in 2002. "For the first three days," he said, "you keep asking yourself, 'Why am I listening to all this unrelated stuff?' By the end of the fourth day, your brain is buzzing and you're exhilarated. That's because all the pieces of information have clicked together. It's entirely an experience of the brain — like a deep massage for the mind."

Before Anderson, TED was a smaller, cheaper invitation-only event, and its speakers came from the fields represented by TED's three letters (Technology, Entertainment, Design). Bezos, Gates and others were all regulars at this event.

During the dot-com boom, Anderson made billions of dollars from technology and business magazines. After the bubble burst, he left the magazine company he had founded, bought TED in 2001, and turned to running the non-profit.

Fifteen years on, the TED conference has stretched from three and a half days to four and a half, and the talks have grown from fewer than 40 to more than 100.

The so-called TED talk — a carefully prepared short speech of no more than 18 minutes — also took shape after Anderson took over.

In 2006, Google acquired YouTube. That same year, TED began uploading videos online.

Free TED videos then replaced the conference as what most people picture when they think of TED. So far, TED videos have been watched 6.1 billion times online, 2.5 billion of those in the last two years alone — not counting a large volume of unauthorized, untraceable plays.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170507164132Ii0MSs9UvCOhgFpW.png-w600)

Megan Smith, the U.S. Chief Technology Officer and a former Google executive, [criticized years ago](https://www.ft.com/content/56caf5b6-0896-11e2-b57f-00144feabdc0) that "TED is now over-prepared, which is a good thing for saving everyone time, but there's not that much raw information."

She has a point: a TED talk usually carries less information than a one- or two-hour conversation at an industry conference. But that is also precisely why videos of industry conferences never go viral.

As [last week's report on TED talk preparation mentioned](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/40317.html), the theme of every TED conference talk must be settled in advance, with at least two rounds of rehearsal, reviewed by Anderson himself.

The talks have also acquired a fixed rhythm. Even this year's video message from Pope Francis, sent from the Vatican, started with a joke about jet lag and, midway through, taught the audience with a vivid metaphor: "Power is like drinking gin on an empty stomach. You feel dizzy, drunk, off balance." — The Pope doesn't normally give speeches like that.

Controlling quality, focusing the information and saving the audience's time are exactly why TED draws people back to buy tickets again and again. Even if a topic isn't that interesting, there are at least jokes — and it doesn't go on too long.

When your audience has paid high ticket prices and sits in the theater for five days, the worst possible outcome is boredom.

TED's team prepares the conference the way a media company prepares a magazine — except its publishing cadence is not measured in days or hours; it plans content on a yearly cycle.

The end result is like a meticulously choreographed TV show. Everyone who takes the stage — whether entrepreneur, civil rights activist, scientist, writer or star — shares their ideas through a performance-like talk.

Even CEOs accustomed to standing on stage to promote their companies talk about different things at TED. In 2003, Jeff Bezos — who still had hair back then — went on stage to discuss [the parallels between the dot-com bubble and the gold rush](https://www.ted.com/talks/jeff_bezos_on_the_next_web_innovation). Last year Travis Kalanick was indeed promoting Uber's world-changing mission, but he too [started from the story of the jitney carpool service, strangled by regulation a century ago](https://www.ted.com/talks/travis_kalanick_uber_s_plan_to_get_more_people_into_fewer_cars).

Behind all this lies lengthy preparation. For the Pope's single video, TED spent more than a year communicating with the Vatican, explaining TED's style and audience.

The live audience and the reach of the videos are both draws for speakers. Anna Rosling Rönnlund, who used data this year to talk about living standards across global income levels, sold her company to Google in 2007 — because Larry Page had watched, live in the auditorium, a talk by her father-in-law, global health expert Hans Rosling.

Jerry Lin, who spent years at Johns Hopkins working on cancer detection research, serves as chief scientist at genetic testing company Natera. At noon on the first day of the conference, he went on stage to [present his breakthrough in DNA-based cancer detection](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/40112.html), after which Natera's stock began to rise. On the third day, the paper behind the talk was published in *Nature*. By the end of that day, Natera's share price had climbed 20% above its pre-talk level.

While every talk is underway, 12 cameras around the auditorium capture the speaker's every move. The footage is edited by a theater director and relayed to a conference room outside the theater labeled TED Management, where members of the TED editorial board watch the live feed.

![A spider cam glides silently overhead in the TED auditorium. TED spares no expense on filming equipment: several floor cameras are fitted with Fujinon XA99 lenses, the kind normally used to shoot sports events, each costing upwards of a million.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170507102735KxeDvf5rV6UH3kjl.jpg-w600)

A 12-member editorial board including Chris Anderson decides, based on the speaker's performance, audience reaction and topical relevance, exactly which day to post each talk on TED's website.

TED's website publishes only five videos a week. Judging by the fact that around 90% of last year's talks made it onto the site, the just-concluded TED conference will supply the website with material for months to come.

Viewers won't truly come to understand a field from a ten-plus-minute talk. But they will learn about something they had never paid any attention to — and that can be a beginning.

![An audience member takes notes in a notebook provided by TED during World Bank president Jim Yong Kim's talk. This is not uncommon.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170507101300fIjMENxZrLQ7h9Xp.jpg-w600)

Data scientist Andreas Weigend, former chief scientist at Amazon, is also a longtime TED attendee. When this year's conference wrapped up, he told QDaily that he felt nothing in particular about the conference's discussions of data, but the Pope's video intrigued him.

It's a fairly common sentiment. The conference's attendees relish being exposed to dense information outside their familiar territory. After an exhausting yet exhilarating week, many booked tickets for the following year.

One person who has attended for seven or eight years in a row said coming here is like a vacation.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170507092824Bv5DNxPSZfWonLF2.png-w600)

But this is not a vacation everyone can take. During the conference, one speaker joked, "Kids still have to choose their families these days — you can't be born into a poor one."

In the media centre, a reporter watching the live feed on a screen remarked, "Everyone here chose pretty well."

Inside the TED venue, apart from staff, the overwhelming majority look to be at least 40. Most are corporate and non-profit executives, family fund managers, or high earners such as investors and lawyers.

Apart from the speakers, who get in free, you won't see many academics — even though their TED videos are the most popular.

That is the inevitable result of an $8,500 ticket.

The conference is TED's main source of revenue — far larger than donations or publishing, accounting for nearly two-thirds of total income.

Besides the annual TED conference, there is TEDGlobal, held mainly in Africa, and TEDWomen, focused on women's issues — each also charging several thousand dollars a ticket.

As TED expands, video production and bandwidth costs, the annual million-dollar prize, and the TED Fellows program for cultivating young speakers all require more investment. Most of that money comes from the conference.

This is TED's contradiction. The content it produces is completely open, offered without reservation to a global audience — a considerable portion of whom are still students.

Yet its revenue comes mainly from an elite group that is no longer young and predominantly white.

This group was young when TED first got going — the founders and executives of Amazon and Google were only in their twenties and thirties when they first attended. Twenty years ago, Bezos's company had an uncertain future, and the two Google guys celebrated funding rounds at fast-food joints. Now they all rank near the top of the global wealth lists and travel by private jet.

That may also explain why the tone of the entire TED conference has stayed so consistent. Even when the topics are refugees, terminal illness, dictatorship, massacre or technological bottlenecks, every talk ultimately lands on the note that effort can make the world better — if I can do it, so can you.

TED's audience may not interfere with the programming, but you really won't feel any emotion in the venue other than optimism.

It's like the ending of a Spielberg movie — but there is far more than one kind of film worth watching.

Tom Reilly, TED's director of community, doesn't believe the audience shapes speakers' topics. He said most speakers are also first-timers and don't really know exactly who is in the room.

At the same time, TED is trying to change the makeup of its live audience.

Its fellows program (TED Fellows) is bringing on 20 to 30 people a year and, through more intensive coaching, enabling young people who have done concrete projects but lack speaking experience to share their work on the main stage. Most of these speakers are not white.

This year marked the first time the TED conference featured a non-English session, with seven speakers giving talks in Spanish. Bollywood superstar Shah Rukh Khan also came to speak; he will collaborate with TED later this year to bring TED to India in the form of a TV program.

Starting in 2018, TED will offer a small number of $5,000 tickets to young attendees who haven't been to the conference before.

Whether these measures will work remains to be seen. But TED does provide a platform for voices that don't easily get heard. Anjan Sundaram, a journalist who had recently returned from a war zone, is one of them.

Sundaram doesn't focus on today's hottest topics — the Middle East, Afghanistan or North Korea. He documents obscure subjects — [the injustices taking place in Congo and the Central African Republic](https://www.amazon.com/Anjan-Sundaram/e/B00E5MOIUC/ref=dp_byline_cont_ebooks_1), two African dictatorships — and is working on his third book. Some of the photographers whose images he used in his talk have already been shot dead.

At the celebration on the final night of the conference, there was — as always — exceptionally healthy food, a band and beer. Sundaram was there too.

Asked what he thought of this conference full of businesspeople, Sundaram said he knows most people don't truly care about the massacres in Africa. But he is still glad — at least the TED organizers consider the topic important enough, and a talk here always gets his record known by more people. "Whatever else there is, consider it a bonus — like winning the lottery."
