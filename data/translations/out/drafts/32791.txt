So what exactly is true love? First, let's enjoy a few answers:

- The Wang Xiaobo version: "Can you and I manage to have no regrets about everything that has happened, and no demands about everything that hasn't? I'm willing to try."
- The Zhihu version: "True love is when two people are both so unattractive, yet each still worries the other will get snatched away."
- The Natsume Soseki version: "The moon is beautiful tonight."

—— The definition of true love is like an open-ended essay prompt: everyone has their own answer. But the ways true love manifests, the ways to judge it, and the ways to sustain it still share some common ground.

To make "true love" a bit more concrete, the QDaily Research Lab launched a series of surveys: "Under what circumstances do you feel someone likes you?", "What do you pretend to do in the early stages of a relationship?", "Under what circumstances do you feel someone has stopped loving you?", "What does your partner do that makes you feel disillusioned about the relationship?", and more. Based on the results, we put together a soulmate-screening scale (how to choose), an intimacy-level comparison chart (how to get along), and a true-love-vs.-not-love comparison table (how to tell).

These scales are not a romance template telling you "true love must look like this." They're meant to share some universal patterns, data, and standards about romance, and to teach you to look at "true love" from a relatively scientific angle.

### **The Soulmate-Screening Scale**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929154758uLSvy5FoxgTc2WiX.jpg-w600)

People have a set of selection criteria for those close to them. Take choosing friends: according to the theory of British anthropologist Robin Dunbar, in a typical social group, humans can maintain stable relationships with about 150 people. In other words, you meet tens of thousands of people in a lifetime, but you only choose roughly 150 of them to be close to.

Extending this to the more cautious business of choosing a soulmate, people's criteria become even more refined. Of course, the first step in finding true love doesn't mean finding a replica of yourself, but rather finding someone who overlaps with you on certain dimensions and whose qualities you can affirm.

[Our Lab once ran a survey titled "How do you screen candidates for a 'soulmate'?"](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/1022.html) It drew 4,666 responses. Combining two metrics—votes received and frequency of mention—we summarized 12 keywords people most commonly bring up when screening a soulmate:

**1. First impression:** A first impression is a mode of thinking based on one's profession, experience, knowledge, and instinct. It isn't always right, but it is vivid and durable, and it determines how the two people interact afterward. Two people whose first impressions of each other are off may never get anywhere.

**2. Looks:** Judging by face is a mate-screening standard rooted in human genes. In our Lab's survey on partner-selection criteria, the option "first things first, check out the face and sort them into tiers" received 491 votes, ranking third—behind the top option, "what matters is whether we can talk to each other; if there's no conversation, good looks are useless" (771 votes).

**3. Sense of humor:** Jokes run hot and cold; comedic taste runs high and low. Two people who will spend the long haul together don't both need to be masters of comedy, but their senses of humor should at least roughly match—you fire off a meme, I fire one back; you toss out a reference, I catch it instantly. Finding each other funny is the starting point of love.

**4. Hobbies:** The number of shared hobbies determines the amount of common ground. Setting aside the parts where you "agree to differ," it's best to have some shared likes. If you truly have no hobbies in common, you should at least manage not to dislike their hobbies—and not force them to like yours.

**5. Eating preferences:** Do you eat spicy? Durian? Cilantro? Do you prefer Chinese or Western food? Soup before the meal or after? Tofu pudding / zongzi / mooncakes—salty or sweet? ... Conflicts over survival basics are fundamental conflicts, and to preserve world peace, people tend to pick someone whose diet is fairly compatible.

**6. Aesthetics:** "You love cool tones, I prefer warm ones; you can't stand Zhao Liying, I can't stand Lin Chi-ling." When two people live together, rather than perfectly identical taste, the better trend is for the less aesthetically gifted to defer to the more gifted—and make the world a more beautiful place.

**7. Personality:** The excuse "our personalities clash"—seemingly impeccable but utterly unoriginal—has become a Double 11 bestseller among breakup excuses. The word most tightly bound to "personality clash" is usually "hard to communicate." One partner loving bustle and the other loving quiet isn't scary; what's scary is the divergence that comes from not communicating—or from communicating passively.

**8. Tacit understanding:** The tacit understanding people most want is "knowing each other's thoughts without a word being said"—the kind where, the moment I raise my hand after a meal, you can instantly tell whether I want a napkin or a toothpick.

**9. Daily habits:** When they say details defeat love, the details they mean are: do you smack your lips when you eat / jiggle your leg / keep long fingernails? Toilet seat up or down? Does your curly brace go on a new line? ... Small stuff like this. Remember: any detail repeated ten thousand times is no longer a small thing.

**10. Worldview:** A worldview means "looking out at the world from above"; it is hard to change in the short term. So for a couple, if you're still quarreling constantly over questions like "after graduation, should I go to a big city to do what I want, or take the arrangement my family has made?"—then break up. Next question.

**11. Spending habits:** Can you accept a roadside food stall? Is a concert ticket at 1,280 yuan expensive or cheap? How much do you spend on travel each year? People will make temporary concessions on spending for love—for instance, accommodating their partner's spending habits by buying cheaper things. But such concessions are limited and never last.

**12. Future plans:** When it comes to future plans, people care about two things: first, "am I in your plan for the future?" and second, "are your plan and my plan the same plan?"

### **Intimacy-Level Comparison**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929154814ZKtlvBPCWUsYNweh.jpg-w600)

After you enter the dating stage, the details of daily life reveal your degree of intimacy. For example, someone who "insists on washing their hair and spraying perfume for a date, and agonizes back and forth among 20 outfits" is generally in the early stage of romance, while someone who "bundles up in front of you in a red down jacket over long johns, wrapped into a chrysanthemum" is generally in the old-married-couple stage.

[A Jezebel report shows](http://jezebel.com/levels-of-intimacy-when-to-burp-fart-poop-in-front-1449422446) that, on average, women wait at least 4 weeks before letting a partner see them without makeup (a rather conservative figure)—and that's just the tip of the intimacy iceberg.

To explain intimacy, Jezebel broke the relationship down into five stages from a woman's perspective, summarizing the new territory unlocked for the other person at each stage. Broadly speaking, how much of your girlfriend's real self you get to see says how intimate you are:

1. When you don't know each other well, you are both perfect specimens of humanity. In your eyes, your girlfriend basically looks like this: makeup done just right; dressed sweetly but casually, without trying too hard; freshly washed hair; eating at an elegant frequency; sneezing while tilting her head and covering her mouth; using foundation to hide pimples; showing only feet with manicured toenails.

2. When you know each other a little, this stage marks the start of the perfect-specimen image falling apart. As your meetups grow more frequent, she begins to admit that this image is maintained and managed—not the face she rolls out of bed with—and that she, too, needs to eat and breathe to stay alive. You gradually start to see your girlfriend's: feet without carefully trimmed toenails; tanned patches of skin; the way she gets hangry; the way she suffers through rhinitis; the unwashed-hair look; and, of course, the makeup-free look.

3. When you know each other fairly well, your relationship has reached the honeymoon stage where neither of you will be scared off, and you even tease each other about daily habits. She won't mind letting you see: the box of sanitary pads; the most comfortable but possibly slightly weird loungewear; period attire (wrapped up like a chrysanthemum); browser history; and she'll fart in front of you without a care—or even deliberately come do it right in your face.

4. When old-married-couple mode switches on, you should already be so familiar with the following that you can look right past them: the silly essays she wrote as a kid; odd moles on her body; the dopey sight of her trimming her toenails or digging out her ears; or the strange gases produced by constipation / diarrhea / other sources.

5. At the stage of confirming the other person as the love of your life, it boils down to one sentence: she basically won't mind letting you witness all manner of terrible excretory activity. Please use your imagination.

### **True Love vs. Not-Love: A Comparison Table**

The "true" in true love lies mainly in "sincerity" and "authenticity." True love, you could say, pursues a way of living together—enjoyed by both sides and friendly over the long run. But "love" is a stage-based emotion that can go off. In fact, the "true love" that many people see as unshakable may well be "fake true love."

On the question of "what is love, and what is not love," the QDaily Research Lab has run two surveys. One asked "Under what circumstances do you feel someone likes you?" and drew 6,998 responses; the other asked "Under what circumstances do you feel someone has stopped loving you?" and drew 12,240 responses (turns out there are more heartbroken people out there).

The realization that someone "likes you" mostly happens in the early stages of romance. In this state, people approach the other person very proactively and put on a bit of an act to leave the most perfect impression. If someone treats you like this, they probably like you:

They contact you very proactively, caring about where you are and what you're doing, making conversation out of nothing, talking in circles, always asking "what are you up to," always asking "have you eaten," always asking "you there, junior?";

They reply with great enthusiasm. No matter how boring or trivial your topic, if you say three words to them, you get five or more back; they'll pick up even the dullest thread;

They come with a special set of chat etiquette: the last line of every conversation is always theirs, and they say goodnight like clockwork every day;

They create chances to meet, always saying "I have a basketball game / a movie ticket today—want to come watch?";

Their attitude toward food is all-embracing. What's constantly on their lips is "I eat anything, you order," and they especially love footing the bill;

They pretend to be connected to every beautiful word in the world. Men are all gentlemen, all stylish, pretending their lives are high-quality and colorful—not at all a neckbeard coder who orders takeout every day. Women are typically associated with "ladylike / refined / reserved / domestic / elegant / sweet"—and if the other person likes the innocent type, they can even pretend not to understand what "dating" means;

They pretend to stay up late, pretend work isn't busy, always seem full of energy, are never sleepy, and act for 24 hours a day as if everything you say is hilarious;

They pretend to have a good temper, pretend it doesn't matter how long they wait, pretend they never swear—they won't even complain about being tired while shopping.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929154836i2v4LYBgax0EmAUb.jpg-w600)

And the process of shifting from "love" to "not love" usually shows up as daily details getting sloppier and less time and energy being spent on the other person. If someone treats you like this, they've probably stopped loving you:

They almost never contact you first; your chat history goes from 1,000 messages to 500 to 200 to "two days ago"; phone calls go from 2 hours to 30 minutes to 10 minutes to "two days ago";

Even when they reply, it's cold, and their replies get shorter and shorter: "Oh." "Heh." "Fine." "Whatever." You ask: "What's wrong?" They reply: "Nothing.";

The chat etiquette has changed—they no longer reply instantly, and the last line of every conversation is always yours;

On phone calls there are long awkward silences, or they say "nothing much, I'll hang up now"; or they ask: "What did you just say?";

They no longer clamor to see you every day; they never come to you first unless it's business; they're always busy; even when they do see you, they're on their phone;

They used to love photos of the two of you; now they only want selfies. You used to be pinned to the top; now you belong on the blocklist.

### **Questions About True Love**

As a research outfit good at discovering and summarizing patterns, the QDaily Research Lab, building on the data above, has also compiled some "little data on true love" for you. Ordered by the sequence of meeting, falling in love, and getting married, it tries to answer the inevitable questions at each stage of a romance:

**1. What should you watch out for in online dating?**

Research from Cornell University and Michigan State University shows that 90% of people fudge their personal information a little. Men tend to cheat on height, while women on average subtract seven or eight jin (roughly 3–4 kilograms) from their weight.

Data from the American online dating site OkCupid shows that people tell a few typical lies when dating online: actual height is 5 cm shorter than claimed; actual income is 20% lower than claimed; the more attractive the photos look, the more likely they are old ones; and 80% of people who claim to be bisexual are actually single-orientation.

**2. How many "true loves" built on long-term relationships does a person have?**

A YouGov survey shows that, within a limited lifetime, more than 85% of people have had three or fewer long-term relationships—and 10% of people have never properly been in one at all.

**3. Is long-distance love more likely to be true love?**

On long-distance relationships, scholar Merolla of Baldwin Wallace University in the US points out in a study that if you stick with a long-distance relationship, it actually becomes stronger—like the plot of *Only Lovers Left Alive*: the two people tend to share common passions, don't depend on constant companionship, and this kind of bond transcends ordinary love that requires being together. It's very hard to shake.

**4. What should you watch out for when a long-distance relationship ends?**

The problems are most likely to arise when a long-distance relationship ends. In a survey of 335 college long-distance couples, Merolla found that 180 of them broke up once the distance ended and they could finally be together. The reason may be that idealized behavior stops—at which point each discovers the other's many flaws. Statistics show that one-third of reunited long-distance couples break up within 3 months of reuniting.

**5. What happens after you move in together?**

A research report from University College London shows that if one person makes a positive change in life, their spouse may follow suit. Take quitting smoking: if a person tries to quit on their own, the success rate is 7.7% for men and 8.2% for women; but if a couple quits together, the success rate rises dramatically—to 48.4% for men and 50% for women.

**6. Does true love have arguments?**

True love isn't entirely free of quarrels and friction—there are simply too many things worth getting angry about. UC Berkeley compiled a ranking of breakup causes (respondents could choose multiple): no toilet paper in the bathroom (45%), dirty dishes or cups in the room (40%), shower gel running out and not being refilled (35%), dirty dishes in the sink (19%), an unmade bed (13%), and a mountain of dirty laundry going ignored (12%).

**7. What kind of person is right for marriage?**

The QDaily Research Lab once ran a survey titled "What kind of person would you least want to marry?" It drew 9,385 responses. The results show that what people find most unbearable in a partner is "stinginess" (17.5%), followed by "boring" (14.5%) and "unemployed" (12.5%).

**8. Is marriage a once-and-for-all fix?**

To be precise, the share of perpetual conflict in marriage is 69%. A follow-up study lasting 4 years found that couples tend to argue endlessly over the very same things—things like: putting on new clothes, changing a hairstyle, gaining or losing a few jin, gaining or losing a few wrinkles.

In the book *After the Honeymoon*, psychologist Dan Wile writes: "When you choose a long-term life partner, you inevitably choose a particular set of unsolvable problems. You may be fighting these problems for 10 years, 20 years, even 50 years."

**9. What are the methodologies for maintaining true love?**

You need to know the "precise coordinates" of some of your partner's mental wiring—for instance: you can name the person who annoys your spouse most; you can list your spouse's least-favorite relatives; you know the three most special moments in your spouse's life; and if your spouse won the lottery, you know what they'd do with it, and so on.

In conversation, use a lot of "we" instead of "I" and "my." When arguing about something, couples with a high true-love index hit a good ratio: five positive remarks before every negative one.
