The festival is built around *Down and Out in Paris and London*.

[According to *The Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/apr/28/george-orwell-down-out-london-paris-live-performance?CMP=twt_gu), Libby Brodie Productions recently announced a partnership with [the Orwell Foundation](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/) and [UCL's Festival of Culture](http://www.ucl.ac.uk/festival-of-culture/events/festival-of-culture-wednesday-6-june-2018) to stage *ORWELL'S DOWN AND OUT LIVE*, a theater festival this summer honoring George Orwell's *Down and Out in Paris and London* — a tribute to a great writer who understood the homeless and the poor like few others.

Specifically, the event spans two days, first in London and then in Paris. The London event is set for June 16, from 2 to 6 p.m., inside Senate House, the University of London's landmark building. It will be livestreamed, directed by Edwin Mingard. The Paris event is scheduled for September; further details have not yet been announced.

In 1927, Orwell resolved to become a writer and began observing the lives of tramps. Two years later, gravely ill and robbed of his last money by a thief, he was driven by a stretch of hardship to write *Down and Out in Paris and London*. First published in 1933, it was the debut work of 30-year-old Eric Arthur Blair under the pen name George Orwell. The book explores poverty in the modern city in two parts: the first follows an English man of letters scraping by on odd jobs in Paris, mired at the very bottom of society; the second describes his drifting from Paris back to London, where his situation worsens further until he becomes an unemployed vagrant wandering from place to place.

[The Guardian reports](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/apr/28/george-orwell-down-out-london-paris-live-performance?CMP=twt_gu) that the forthcoming *ORWELL'S DOWN AND OUT LIVE* is an immersive theatrical production and series of events adapted from Orwell's *Down and Out in Paris and London*, his first essay *The Spike*, and the novel *A Clergyman's Daughter*. Actors, artists, politicians, and activists will weave together Orwell's writing and the voices of today's homeless, immersing audiences in the poverty of the present through theater, music, and literature.

[*The British Theatre Guide* commented](http://newdiorama.com/whats-on/down-out-in-paris-and-london): "Wandering from the streets of twentieth-century Paris to those of twenty-first-century London, *ORWELL'S DOWN AND OUT LIVE* uses humor, tragedy, and the genuine sorrow of shattered souls to tell us that in fact nothing has changed — and that human beings can endure anything." The festival's organizers hope the event will respond to the increasingly severe problems of poverty and homelessness facing both countries.

"Today low incomes, precarious work, high housing costs, debt, and a lack of welfare are pushing people into the abyss. Through drama adapted from Orwell's fiction, people can pay more attention to, better understand, and help drive the resolution of modern poverty," [said Campbell Robb, CEO of the Joseph Rowntree Foundation](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/apr/28/george-orwell-down-out-london-paris-live-performance?CMP=twt_gu). [The Joseph Rowntree Foundation](https://www.jrf.org.uk/), an independent civil society organization dedicated to tackling poverty in the UK, is one of the event's participants. At the end of 2017 it released a [report](https://www.jrf.org.uk/report/uk-poverty-2017) examining changes in UK poverty over the previous 20 years — the first progress report on the UK's poverty-reduction efforts since the foundation launched its ["Solve UK Poverty"](https://www.jrf.org.uk/solve-uk-poverty) program in 2016.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805080520513lIOt1povC0hVPk7.jpg-w600)

*ORWELL'S DOWN AND OUT LIVE* is not the only significant event in recent years paying tribute to or engaging with Orwell. In November 2017, for instance, [the BBC, Orwell's former employer, unveiled a statue of him outside its newly built headquarters](http://www.bbc.com/zhongwen/simp/41922096). On the wall behind the statue is a quote from *Animal Farm*: "If liberty means anything at all, it means the right to tell people what they do not want to hear."

Robert Seatter, head of BBC History, said: "This sets a high bar for the BBC, challenging it to live up to Orwell's standard of narrative clarity and truth-telling." In June 2017, the Orwell Foundation and Libby Brodie Productions collaborated on [*1984 LIVE*](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/events/1984-live/), an immersive theatrical adaptation of Orwell's representative novel *Nineteen Eighty-Four*; in June 2017, [a stage version of *Nineteen Eighty-Four* ran on Broadway](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/42366.html), where audiences vomited, fainted, and screamed; in January 2017, after a remark about "alternative facts" by Kellyanne Conway, an advisor to U.S. President Trump, [*Nineteen Eighty-Four* suddenly shot to the top of Amazon's U.S. bestseller list within days](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/37268.html), prompting the publisher to print an additional 75,000 copies, and so on.

The reasons for these events are simple. As Penguin Books wrote on the paperback of *Nineteen Eighty-Four* in 1979: "First published in 1949, it remains as relevant today as it was then."

The same can be said of the man and his other works. [The BBC reported](http://www.bbc.com/zhongwen/simp/41922096) that, after careful deliberation, one of the main considerations in the BBC's decision to erect a bronze statue of Orwell outside Broadcasting House was that he stands as journalism's finest exemplar — a point made all the more relevant amid today's deluge of fake news. And in the "alternative facts" affair, [Karen Tumulty, a reporter for *The Washington Post*, remarked](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2017/jan/24/george-orwell-1984-sales-surge-kellyanne-conway-alternative-facts): "'Alternative facts' is a George Orwell phrase."

Moreover, *Nineteen Eighty-Four* has once again become a bestseller in many places. Orwell's coinages — "Newspeak," "doublethink," "Big Brother" — have entered English dictionaries, a measure of his influence. Even his name itself has become a proper noun in constant circulation. "Orwellian" and "Orwellism" are everywhere.

"Orwellian" is an adjective, usually used to describe the horrors of totalitarianism and the falsification of history; "Orwellism" is a noun, usually used to describe his left-wing admirers and self-conscious followers. "Few writers have become both an adjective and a noun. Offhand I can only think of Marx, Freud, Darwin, Dickens, Tolstoy, Joyce, and James," writes Timothy Garton Ash, professor of European studies at Oxford, in his essay *Orwell for Our Time*.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180508052204tLBAi6JjV9l23OFd.jpg-w600)

In that essay, Garton Ash discusses the question of "why Orwell is great" in detail. In his view, it owes a great deal to the peculiar fascination of Orwell's life and work and to their enduring importance.

Orwell was English, with "a peculiar, stubborn, rebellious, thoroughly English character." Garton Ash even claimed that "nobody has portrayed the English character better than Orwell, and he himself was a living anthology of Englishness" — his humor, his clumsiness in dealing with women, his love of the countryside, animals, and gardening were all thoroughly English.

Garton Ash even sketched his life in brief: "The scholarship boy from Eton who served as a colonial policeman in Burma, washed dishes in Paris, and tramped the streets of London; who kept a shop in the countryside, fought in the Spanish Civil War, abandoned London's left-wing literary set, and moved to a remote farm on a Scottish island, dying of tuberculosis at the very moment of literary triumph, aged only 46. Tall and thin, in a shabby tweed jacket, loose corduroy trousers, and a black shirt, with a strange pencil-line moustache, a high, shrill voice, smoking working men's cigarettes — in his lifetime these were anecdotes; after his death they became legend."

As for importance, Orwell is without doubt "the most influential political writer of the 20th century," who hoped to turn "political writing into an art." The Orwell Prize, today the UK's most important award for political writing, likewise aims to encourage "outstanding political writing." "Long before Solzhenitsyn — a span stretching from 1945 to 1990, the entire Cold War — what we then called 'the West' regarded Orwell as the supreme portrayer of totalitarianism in general, and of Soviet totalitarianism in particular," Garton Ash wrote.

According to the *Oxford English Dictionary*, Orwell was the first to use the phrase "cold war" in English. He was also a left-wing Cold War intellectual, a socialist who opposed communism. According to files declassified by the UK National Archives on September 4, 2007, Orwell was suspected of being a communist, and from 1929 until his death in 1950 he was kept under surveillance by MI5 and the Metropolitan Police's Special Branch. More important, he was honest enough in his own writing — "daring to be a Daniel, to fight alone, to hold fast to his purpose, to lay it before the public" — believing that "only writers who belong to no party can be honest."

[According to the BBC](http://www.bbc.com/zhongwen/simp/41922096), Orwell died of tuberculosis in January 1950 at University College Hospital in London. His hope of seeing a "republic" rise from the postwar ruins came to nothing; the "true socialism" he spoke of never even took shape; yet the concepts he coined in his newspaper columns — "cold war" and "the United States of Europe" — proved remarkably prescient. The Cold War has ended; European political and economic integration has gone back and forth, and now stands at a major turning point because of Brexit. Many writers and columnists after him have tried to imitate him, but few have succeeded — mainly because behind Orwell's prose lay an entire set of unique and extraordinary experiences that few people today can or are willing to undergo. Yet Orwell's influence has only grown over the years.

"That is the great Orwell. We still need him, because Orwell's works will live forever," Garton Ash writes in *Facts Are Subversive*.

Finally, to help you get to know this great writer better, we have picked 7 books for your reference.

### Novels: *Nineteen Eighty-Four* and *Animal Farm*

Orwell wrote only six novels in his lifetime (*Burmese Days*, *A Clergyman's Daughter*, *Keep the Aspidistra Flying*, *Coming Up for Air*, *Animal Farm*, *Nineteen Eighty-Four*). *Animal Farm* and *Nineteen Eighty-Four* are without doubt his most famous and most important works. As that much-quoted line goes: "Every additional person who reads Orwell adds a safeguard for freedom."

### Non-fiction: *Homage to Catalonia*

Many people wonder: Orwell was thoroughly English, never went anywhere near Russia or Eastern Europe — so why did his novels depict life under totalitarianism so accurately? How did he understand it? How did he know? The answers are all in this book. And it is worth noting that this is probably Orwell's finest work of political writing.

### Essays, miscellanies, and reviews: *Inside the Whale* and *The Lion and the Unicorn*

Beyond fiction and non-fiction, Orwell's essays, miscellanies, and reviews are also distinctive, spanning politics and literature. In these two books he discusses Greene, Gandhi, Dickens, Kipling, Tolstoy, Shakespeare, Zamyatin, Mark Twain, nationalism, totalitarianism, socialism, anti-Semitism, and many other subjects. To be clear: *Inside the Whale* is a collection of literary criticism, while *The Lion and the Unicorn* is a collection of social commentary.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180508052656ZEP7nqGNzL6dFjeH.jpg-w600)

### Biography: *Orwell: Wintry Conscience of a Generation*

This is a biography of Orwell by Jeffrey Meyers, a leading Orwell scholar. *The Atlantic* praised it: "Jeffrey Meyers has drawn a portrait of Orwell that inspires admiration, yet he does not hide the writer's distinctly unsaintly qualities... His biography is the most authoritative one to date."

"Orwell had human weaknesses too. He longed for wealth, for good looks, for effortless success with women. Women were always important to him... The Orwell presented in this book does not measure up to the towering legendary figure. He was of noble character, but also capable of violence and cruelty, tormented by guilt, so given to self-punishment that it amounted to a kind of masochism, and at times self-destructive," Meyers writes in the book.

### Related: *Finding George Orwell in Burma*

In the 1920s, Orwell spent five years in Burma as a policeman of the British Empire. Five years later, with no warning, he suddenly returned to Britain to hand in his resignation and began his writing career. Part of the reason for quitting was that he could no longer stand the arrogance, narrow-mindedness, and indifference of the British in Burma. These experiences and feelings are plainly visible in *Burmese Days*, the novel he published in 1935. Burma would thereafter appear in Orwell's books in various forms: *Burmese Days*, *Animal Farm*, *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, and his unfinished work *The Smoking-Room Story*, among others.

American journalist Emma Larkin revisited the places in Burma where Orwell had lived and worked — Mandalay, Myaungmya, Rangoon, Moulmein, Katha... She sought answers to questions such as: what experiences drove Orwell to abandon colonial life and turn to writing? And what has made Burma so poor and wretched? Larkin recovered this stretch of Orwell's life almost entirely overlooked by researchers, wrote about his spiritual influence on Burma today, and portrayed the country's present, entangled between the ghost of colonialism and the grip of dictatorship.

"In Burma people joke that Orwell didn't write just one novel about this country — he wrote three: a trilogy made up of *Burmese Days*, *Animal Farm*, and *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. Outsiders like me can hardly see through the illusion created by the generals, and can hardly imagine the daily fear and insecurity of living in such a country. As I struggled to understand this side of Burmese life, I became captivated by Orwell. All his novels attempt to explore how individuals are trapped by their surroundings — controlled by their families, by the society around them, and by an all-powerful government," Larkin writes in the book.
