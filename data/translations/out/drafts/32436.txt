The Trendy Goods That Replaced "Affordable Luxury" Never Actually Talked About "Affordable Luxury"
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
Value for money is a good thing, but sameness is not; easy availability is a good thing, but making consumers look worthless is a big mistake.
@@QD_BODY@@
Value for money is a good thing, but sameness is not; easy availability is a good thing, but making consumers look worthless is a big mistake.

When you have three or four thousand yuan to spend on a new bag, you'll find there are far more options now than there were five years ago.

Around 2012, the concept of "light luxury" began to catch on in China. Its English term is affordable luxury — that is, "luxury that can be afforded." Although the word "luxury" is right there in the name, it is really more of a fashion concept: initially, consumers bought affordable-luxury brands to enjoy name-brand products — and the satisfaction that comes with them — within, or just slightly above, their own spending power.

The standout of the genre needs no introduction: Coach spotted this demand earlier than anyone. [Back in the 1990s, Coach opened a flagship store on Madison Avenue in New York](http://www.haberdasherstandard.com/2013/11/brand-story-all-about-coach-leatherware.html), selling products that working women took to immediately: practical, unpretentious, and far cheaper than the big-name houses.

Brands serving working women kept appearing after that, and their representatives now have stores all over the world: Michael Kors, Tory Burch and Kate Spade. Their expansion spread from Europe and America into Asia, and today their decline has likewise begun at the source and is spreading into Asian markets (China above all). Given the breakneck expansion of these brands between 2012 and 2015, their store footprint and efficiency-driven distributor networks have become a burden instead.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609212028356dofqTyDscOQleU1.jpg-w600)

Neither the brands themselves nor Wall Street could stomach this decline. Michael Kors's share price today is only about half of its peak, while Kate Spade now trades at just 40% of its all-time high. Coach's net profit from the North American market fell from 64% of the total in 2011 to 58.9% in 2015. This past August, [Coach announced it would pull out of roughly 250 department stores](http://www.cnbc.com/2016/08/09/coach-draws-the-line-with-department-stores.html), and [Michael Kors chose to cut back on promotions at its department-store locations](http://www.cnbc.com/2016/08/10/michael-kors-is-fed-up-with-department-stores-damaging-its-brand.html). Department stores became the first stop on their escape route.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921194745M069iHU57A4bqYhl.jpg-w600)

These conditions have persisted for at least six months. Marc-Olivier Arnold, director of the Intuition & Solutions division at RTG Consulting, which specializes in fashion and luxury, told QDaily that while discounting can make a brand popular fast, it has become a challenge the brand must now face: "When every woman on the street is carrying the same deeply discounted Michael Kors or Coach product, how do you get people to want your current season's high-end pieces?"

[Citi analyst Paul Lejuez offered this assessment of Michael Kors's remedy](http://www.cnbc.com/2016/08/10/michael-kors-is-fed-up-with-department-stores-damaging-its-brand.html): "Reducing exposure in the wholesale channel is indeed the right thing to do, but we believe the damage has been done."

What amplified the damage, in fact, lies not only with the affordable-luxury brands themselves. Seen from one angle, apart from certain designs that hew too closely to luxury products, they don't have major product problems. As for discounting — it does create the impression that "what you buy today will be worth nothing tomorrow," but it is not the root cause of the rout. If that counted, outlet stores would also have to be counted among the culprits — these days, the places that clear inventory for the big brands are choosing locations closer and closer to city centers in order to reach consumers, putting them in conflict with brand stores selling the latest collections. According to estimates by analyst Paul Lejuez cited by The Business of Fashion, [outlet sales accounted for as much as 70% of Coach's retail revenue in 2013](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/intelligence/do-accessible-luxury-brands-have-an-inherently-limited-lifespan) — which gives you a sense of how tangled the problems Coach needs to sort out really are.

But none of these is the most fundamental problem. The most fundamental problem is that the "affordable luxury" of yesterday, by lowering the barrier to purchase without limit, has overdrawn its brand value. And at the same time, consumers have so many similar alternatives that they can abandon these brands easily and pick what they want from a kaleidoscope of niche labels — and when those niches band together, they become a formidable competitor.

All of the consumers we interviewed felt that some affordable-luxury styles had already been "played out." Karen, who has bought Coach products, said, "Before Coach changed designers, it was a huge favorite with middle-aged women out grocery shopping." May, a graduate student, said that during her internship at an advertising agency she noticed all her colleagues used name-brand bags, but "there's no way I could ask my family for that much money to buy a really expensive bag — these were honestly the only options." And Sophia, a fashion blogger who works in the fashion industry, said: "Because I want to look a little different, if a certain affordable-luxury style becomes a viral hit, I'd probably give up on that style."

Just as they can easily find out what's in fashion now, consumers learn what's gone out of fashion almost as quickly — perhaps even faster, since nobody wants to be thought tacky.

As every law of commerce shows, choice cultivates consumers, and consumers in turn spawn more market choice.

In China, a "viral hit" appears more and more frequently. Furla's "jelly bag" (the Furla Candy) and Longchamp's "dumpling bag" (Le Pliage) have been hot items for a long time; in 2014 the Mansur Gavriel bucket bag was impossible to get your hands on; in 2015, Issey Miyake's BAO BAO became the undisputed street staple.

![Source: [3oneseven.com](http://3oneseven.com/issey-miyake/bao-bao-issey-miyake/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921195126zeOSvD50IR4JWhNG.jpg-w600)

Their common trait is that sales of a single item drive the presence of the entire brand, and if the brand rides the wave, it gains even more. In the first quarter of this year, [Furla's sales in China rose 45%](http://www.scmp.com/lifestyle/fashion-luxury/article/1966875/italian-brand-furla-pioneering-affordable-luxe-sector) — and that figure counts only sales at Chinese stores, not overseas shopping. Longchamp's global revenue rose 10% year over year in 2015, with [China becoming their most important market after France, where sales rose 30%](http://www.reuters.com/article/longchamp-results-idUSL8N15N37J).

No brand, it seems, would proactively talk about a positioning like "affordable luxury." What they talk about is the consumer mindset behind it: ironically, it is no different from the one that flocked to Coach back in the day — the consumer demand for value for money is eternal.

Take a quick look at the hot viral hits and you'll find their prices cluster around US$500, in the same range affordable luxury occupied back then. And the heat around the early viral hits has faded too; the styles still in fashion either keep releasing limited editions or lack large-scale purchase channels and can only be bought through scattered daigou agents.

Some brands deliberately control shipment volumes while emphasizing different selling points through different channels.

The most obvious examples are Mansur Gavriel and Manu Atelier. The Mansur Gavriel bucket bag was out of stock for a long stretch; [on December 9, 2014, they told their Instagram followers that the new season's products were finally going up on the website, and within the following hour 95% of the products sold out](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/intelligence/mansur-gavriel-created-first-post-recession-bag) — and that was just one piece of evidence of the brand's popularity.

This brand, founded in 2013 by then-unknown designers, first released products that June; by July they had sold out completely and left behind a long waiting list. The credit goes to social media: fashion blogger Garance Doré, who had 250,000 Twitter followers at the time, recommended Mansur Gavriel's black bucket bag, Flamma, in June, and by September, during New York Fashion Week, many editors and celebrities were photographed carrying bucket bags; once those photos circulated on social networks, the products sold out even further.

The story of Manu Atelier from Turkey is a similar one. Merve Manastir and Beste Manastir founded the brand in February 2014; after Eva Chen, the former editor-in-chief of the fashion magazine Lucky, posted a photo of a Manu Atelier bag on Instagram in June and praised it, it quickly caught consumers' attention.

![Source: [The Business of Fashion](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/intelligence/mansur-gavriel-created-first-post-recession-bag)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921195927hSGbBAgoxJ45rwNK.png-w600)

Summing up Mansur Gavriel's marketing rules looks quite simple:

1. Limit the number of products; sell only a certain quantity in a handful of high-end boutiques and department stores. Products sold on the official website are also limited;

2. Tease new releases on social networks beforehand, so that consumers hoping to grab the products will pay extra attention to their social accounts;

3. The Instagram account conveys "the story of the product" to consumers: the photos are basically of the products and little-known models, with minimal text and bright product colors;

4. Twitter and Facebook carry only new-release information, staying focused on Instagram, the platform better suited to showing images.

![A screenshot of Mansur Gavriel's Instagram](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921200023x4Qrf5TPRqyiIl8F.jpg-w600)

Mansur Gavriel hasn't released sales figures, but [it became profitable in the third quarter after the brand was founded](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/intelligence/mansur-gavriel-created-first-post-recession-bag). [Manu Atelier, meanwhile, has been selling well, with US$800,000 in retail sales and nearly US$400,000 in operating revenue by the end of 2015](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/turning-point/manu-atelier-and-the-eva-chen-effect-2).

Worth noting is that while most traditional fashion brands still treat the internet as a new channel, many emerging niche brands have already realized that the internet is a complex of many channels. Appearing on different platforms can fully signal a brand's positioning and its assumptions about its target consumers.

Still, purchasing power from China is plain to see. [One of Manu Atelier's founders, Beste, once said](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/turning-point/manu-atelier-and-the-eva-chen-effect-2): "Customers from China, and from Turkey too, pretty much bought out our stock at Vakko (note: the famed Turkish department store, at the time our only physical retail outlet)."

This is hardly unrelated to the poverty of the physical shopping experience in China. Young Chinese consumers are ahead of what the market supplies; the goods they want often cannot be found in stores at home, and the internet becomes the best tool.

Chen Zhan, senior research director at the Kantar TNS market research firm, told QDaily that consumers facing ever-greater variety within a category is one of the shifts in consumption trends: "Hot topics rotate extremely fast these days. Take Xiaohongshu — it's a fashion landmark of sorts, and what it recommends always comes with a pretext and a story — like where a brand's inspiration came from — so people feel there's something new to choose. Overseas shopping lets everyone see new products and designs. Social media also keeps feeding everyone news."

The flood of brands entering China, the vast amount of information social media provides, the growing number of products people have bought — all of these have given Chinese consumers a much deeper knowledge of the fashion and luxury business.

Dazhenzi, who has spent ten years as a daigou agent, told QDaily that her clients are almost all working women aged 26 to 36, and that over the years her clientele has changed: "More and more people can afford more expensive things now, they're not as picky as when they first encountered the brands, and they no longer mythologize foreign brands." Chen Zhan, for his part, says that nowadays "Chinese consumers know more and more about brands — which brand, what price point, what counts as truly luxurious, what tier things are — the distinctions are extremely clear."

[In 2015, China's cross-border e-commerce spending was estimated to reach 259 billion yuan, accounting for 6% of total e-commerce consumption in China](http://www.mckinsey.com/industries/high-tech/our-insights/crossborder-ecommerce-is-luring-chinese-shoppers). Worth noting: when it comes to high-end fashion consumption, the role of China's best-known e-commerce platforms, Tmall and Taobao, can only be described as "delicate" — they seem to be unquestionable channels riding on enormous traffic, yet the flood of counterfeits and the highly fragmented competition among daigou agents keep eroding brand heat.

All of this was of course felt by the affordable-luxury giants of the day. Back in 2014, Coach CEO Victor Luis announced the brand's transformation: it no longer wanted to be "accessible luxury" but hoped to position itself as "modern luxury." Stuart Vevers, recruited from Loewe, became design director, and the results now look considerable.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609211949025U1ekPsRBCXnKZiT.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609212014272vQ0H4A7qljgyXVP.jpg-w600)

Yang Baoyan, Coach's president and CEO for Greater China, told QDaily that what they want to do is achieve transformation across three areas: product, store environment and marketing strategy. Coach is no longer in a rush to open stores, has reduced promotions at its directly operated stores, closed 74 retail stores worldwide in fiscal 2015, and has renovated many locations into "modern luxury" concept stores — such stores already account for around 40% of the total in the mainland.

Michael Kors, meanwhile, is still struggling. CEO John Idol said the company has realized that seeing its products discounted everywhere makes consumers doubt the brand's value, and that going forward it will no longer participate in department-store special sales. He added: "We have to correct the mistakes we made in the past; the damage to us has already gone too deep."
