Behind their success may also lie their insight into the essence of needs, a great deal of hard work, and their passion and curiosity about the world.

Even Ronan Bouroullec himself can't quite explain how he and his brother won the favor of those big brands.

Since 1997, when the then-unknown pair was discovered by Italian design master Giulio Cappellini, the Bouroullec brothers' path in design has been smooth sailing. Those home-furnishing brands you know so well — Vitra, Artek, Magis, Alessi, Flos, Kvadrat, Iittala, Cappellini, Nanimarquina, Hay and so on — all have long, stable working relationships with the studio of the Bouroullec brothers from France. Known for work that is simple and practical yet full of design sensibility, they are regarded as the design stars most worth watching since Philippe Starck.

On a weekend in early March, Ronan Bouroullec visited China for the second time. He came to the Design Republic showroom on Jiangning Road in Shanghai, where an exhibition titled 《居所》 ("Dwelling") had been set up. Here you can see quite a few works by the Bouroullec brothers — for instance, the Belleville Chair Ronan designed for Vitra, the Clouds installation for Kvadrat, and the Run furniture series for American furniture brand Emeco.

We also met Ronan Bouroullec in this showroom. Compared with his optimistic, cheerful younger brother Erwan, Ronan tends to play the more demanding role in the duo. He is an absolute perfectionist with a strong critical spirit.

In conversation with us, Ronan Bouroullec was always quick to respond. "(The design language) changes every day. I think that to find a good answer, learning to question is very important. We always encounter many problems, many solutions, and we have to make many decisions, but we must remain critical," Ronan Bouroullec said. "Sometimes it's also necessary to keep a certain distance from things — it helps us think from a broader perspective and try to find a wise answer."

The Bouroullec brothers' way of working is piecemeal. Ronan finds it hard to define his way of working; he describes his day as always spent drawing, discussing, walking around, sitting... He hands his finished drawings to his design assistant, who 3D-prints them into models.

In the eighteen years since founding the studio, he has worked together with his younger brother; they often argue, but they always make up quickly afterward. "We're both just trying to design better work," Ronan says. Designs born out of disagreement often surprise them, which has something to do with the fact that the two happen to have complementary professional backgrounds: Ronan studied applied arts and is good at color and installations, while his younger brother Erwan is more skilled in lighting design, display, and interior design.

Unlike his brother, who is good at designing on the computer, Ronan's design work always starts with sketches, then moves to models, and then to products. Some people think his way of working looks a bit outdated, but he doesn't mind.

The Bouroullec office is located in northeast Paris; its predecessor was a three-story shoe factory. Besides the two brothers, there are four young assistants. Though the headcount is small, the office feels terribly cramped: everyone's desks are jammed right next to each other, piled with clutter; their drawings and project materials fill an entire wall; downstairs in the workshop hang all kinds of tools, including wrenches of different sizes, tape, wire, and screwdrivers. It is here that they spend two or three years polishing a single piece.

Down-to-earth labor and a close-knit way of working bring high efficiency — they have become increasingly prolific, from 9 pieces in 2014, to 11 in 2015, to 16 in 2016. At its peak, this six-person team had as many as 20 design projects in hand at the same time.

Perhaps every designer has their own design language. The Bouroullec brothers, however, like diversity and change. They move between large spaces and small objects, between mass industrial production and handcraft, between simple expression and colorful hues. The duo calls their designs "solutions" — meaning that beyond outward form, what they really care about is how to solve real-life problems through better design.

This is vividly on display in the works the Bouroullec brothers have handled. Designs made to solve needs look uncomplicated, carry no flamboyant personal style, and seem to fit perfectly with everyone and every setting. This is also why they can design for almost any brand — not just home furnishings, but architecture, interiors, even electronics.

Bouroullec designed the aim series of pendant lamps for Italian lighting brand Flos. Long cords give them the greatest possible freedom; like vines, the lamps wind and spiral through the air, and from simple to luxurious they can meet the lighting needs of different settings.

Bouroullec also likes to design with a "modular" mindset. Several of their products regarded as classics embody the idea of evolving endlessly varied forms from the most basic modules.

Their most representative "modular" works, Algues and Clouds, were designed for Vitra in 2004 and 2009 respectively. The "cloud" vases, designed in 2015, are a set of flower-arranging objects made of black ceramic. The bodies take the shape of clouds, with distinctive wavy rims. Several bottles of different heights can be combined freely to form new vase groupings. After Zaha Hadid died in 2016, the Bouroullec brothers turned these vases white, filled them with flowers, and placed them inside the fire station — Zaha's first built work, at the Vitra Campus in Weil am Rhein, Germany — in her memory.

Beyond that, they also strongly emphasize solving needs, yet they believe good design should not stop at the functional level. Above that, it should achieve more — comfort and beauty, for instance.

From the television the Bouroullec brothers designed for Samsung, you can see their insistence on pursuing both beauty and practicality.

Ronan Bouroullec regards ridding the world of all "ugly things" as his mission. He believes that the black box we take for granted — the television — can also become part of the home, blending completely into its surroundings through elegant form. In 2014, when Samsung approached the Bouroullec brothers about a collaboration, they proposed this idea. It later became the "Serif TV." Seen from the side, it is an elegant serif capital "I"; the back is covered in fabric. It can sit on a cabinet on its own, or be fitted with a stand to stand on the floor.

It seems the secret of every designer's rise to fame can hardly be summed up simply. The Bouroullec brothers are no exception. Behind their success may lie their insight into the essence of needs, a great deal of hard work, and their passion and curiosity about the world.

At the "Design Celebration" event on March 11, we listened to a conversation between Lyndon Neri of Neri&Hu and Ronan Bouroullec. In addition, on the day after the Design Celebration ended, QDaily had a brief chat with Ronan Bouroullec. We combined these interviews and picked out some interesting views — perhaps you can find the secret behind Bouroullec's design in them.

### 1. My brother and I often disagree, but that's also essential to design

My brother and I often argue. In Chinese culture, the younger brother usually listens to the older brother, but in France it's different. He started out as my assistant and later became my partner, working with me. We grew up in the French countryside, living on a farm. From childhood we were exposed to art, and whatever I did, my brother tended to like too. He's also very good with computers, and good in many ways.

In fact, our design work is a process of inspiration taking shape, and it also requires good technique to make good design. In the studio, my brother and I sit facing each other, and we critique each other's designs. For instance, he'll say: it's well done, but there might be a small detail that could still be improved.

Design is not art; it's not work that concerns only the individual, so you have to learn to work with people. In our design work we deal with engineers, the CEOs of client companies, and craftspeople to decide the form, function, and aesthetics of a piece. Everyone plays their own role in the design process.

### 2. I don't really mind being copied, because I didn't invent the chair

I don't think copying my chair design is anything special. The chair itself is not a brand-new invention — it's not as if there were no chairs in the world before. I've been talking about solutions; what we look for is a solution, and I've been researching this since 2000. For example, how do you design better office furniture? Everyone works in an office, often more than 12 hours a day, and during that time everyone wants a chair that is convenient and comfortable.

In that regard, if you simply make a direct copy of my work, I think that's fine. But I hope that when you copy, you also take into account some of my thinking when I designed the chair. That is to say, this is a good design method of mine, and you can share it. If my design inspires you in some way, I think that's wonderful too.

I know there are now many well-known furniture manufacturers whose products may draw inspiration from other people's designs. So you can see that some of their product details are very similar.

### 3. An important phase in life

Around 2000 and 2002, we met some clients and some other important designers. They wanted us to rethink the concept of the office, including the kind of super-large office that can hold ten thousand people. Actually, at the time I had no experience in this area, and our studio had only three people. So I found it strange that this client suddenly brought us such a big project. After two years of effort, our ideas gradually took shape, the staff grew to six, and we began working with computers. That phase was very important to me.

### 4. If you don't make something distinctive, it's not interesting

Our very small studio handles many projects. But when we take on a project, we have to make sure it's one we're willing to do.

Samsung came to us before, saying they wanted to do a television project. In fact, it also embodies a spiritual element of the electronic age. I told them we could work together, and that we hoped to add interactive elements to the TV we designed. At the time, we wondered: could the TV be embedded in a wall, or in a cabinet? When we thought of that, I grew more and more interested in the project — that is, making the TV screen part of the furniture.

Here, I can't say this is a completely new invention. But when you do a project, you have to feel it's connected to your heart. You can make a carpet, you can make something similar — but if you don't make something distinctive, there's no point.

### 5. I want to work with the best companies

I don't chase the number of companies I work with; I chase working with the best companies. Vitra is one very famous company, and it's large in scale. We hope to join hands to innovate and open up new directions.

### 6. As designers, one of our missions is to find better solutions in our work

Often, when I do industrial design I need to find some basic principles and see which ones suit the industry. If you want to find better solutions, you have to consider the characteristics of the industry — use robots? Or use other materials?

At the same time, beyond industrial mass production, I'm also very interested in handcraft. I work with artisans from Italy, China, and Pakistan, who all have techniques passed down through generations.

So for a designer, one of our missions is to find better solutions in our work. And we hope to help the relevant industries and workers find better answers too, hoping their skills can be passed down in this ever-changing world.

For example, I have a partner in Pakistan. It's a family of hereditary craftsmen, and their products are still sold in South America, Brazil, and elsewhere. When I observed them, I found that they not only have good craftsmanship but are also part of society. When I was doing this project in Pakistan, I discussed this issue with the local elders, so the project ended up lasting nearly three years and involved 150 artisans in total. We know that such a project also had a positive impact on the local society and economy.

### 7. Should we broaden the audience for our products?

The so-called Nordic style refers to the modernist trend that originated in the last century, combining practicality and aesthetics. The question is: how do you create home furnishings that serve ordinary families? Maybe the family only has a small room of 25 or 30 square meters — how do you serve them? How should their furniture and kitchen be designed? This is the direction we care about.

We hope some new companies will also join in, answer such questions, find more affordable solutions, make product prices fairer, and serve a wider audience.

### 8. The world is changing, and people have come to value the importance of design. There are many ways to become a designer

When it comes to good design — including design integration — Apple has always been a great example. In fact, through these designs it changed the whole world — not just surface design improvements but entire revolutionary changes, a brand-new concept introduced by a team of only ten people.

That is Apple's story. In France, design is not regarded as an industry or a profession, and there is no government agency dedicated to design. But design does affect economic development; good designers grow very fast and provide good products that change people's lives. So I think people should have a deeper understanding of the designer as a profession. In Italy, for example, design had a major impact on social change — the people of the Renaissance were painters, sculptors, and also designers.

As far as I know, young designers today have increasingly broad careers, and they need to consider globalized demand. For them, there are many ways to become a designer: you can choose to work for a company, or you can choose to design independently. If the former goes well, you can live on licensing fees later on. But if it doesn't go so well, you have to think about survival. You can do a lot of work, but the most important question is that you need to have a method, or a strategy, and know how to proceed.

### 9. To young designers: "You should get out to the front lines"

I'm always being asked what message I have for young designers — does this mean I'm being treated as an old designer now?

Actually, I think you're all younger and should do better than us. My advice: as a designer you must go into factories, go to the front lines of life, and understand different companies.

For designers, we must work with people, so you must understand the situation of different industries and understand what changes you can make. I don't think this is very difficult. One very important thing is to have a good idea that can be realized. And you have to share your ideas with others and let everyone know what direction you're moving in. I think that's my advice to all of you: don't see yourself as an expert, but as someone cooperating with others and learning.
