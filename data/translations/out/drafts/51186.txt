"Many people go online, but they don't know which things are actually facts and which are opinions."

On the afternoon of March 13, at The Bookworm, the English-language bookstore in Beijing's Sanlitun, writer and journalist Ian Johnson told QDaily: "These days none of us can be good reporters anymore, because the good media outlets are all gone."

But in most people's eyes, Ian Johnson is unquestionably a good reporter. From the 1980s to the present, he has worked in journalism for more than 30 years, living and working for extended periods in the United States, China, and Germany, and writing for well-known outlets such as The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, The New Yorker, and The New York Review of Books. In 2001, he won the Pulitzer Prize, the highest honor in American journalism, for his reporting on China. Five years later, he became a Nieman Fellow at Harvard. The Nieman Fellowship is a Harvard program for leading journalists from around the world. Last year, he also received the Shorenstein Journalism Award for his contributions to reporting on Asia.

Ian Johnson, 56, was born in Montreal, Canada, and later emigrated with his parents to Florida in the United States. But he has lived in China for nearly 20 years — longer than in any other country — and his finest reporting has mostly been about China. Unlike many foreign correspondents based in China, however, Johnson is not keen on simply covering Chinese politics; instead, he has turned his attention to China's vast social, religious, and historical landscape, and to the relationship between society and government. He is the author of *Wild Grass* and *The Souls of China*, among other works.

[Ian Buruma, professor at Bard College in New York and editor-in-chief of The New York Review of Books, wrote](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0151014183?_encoding=UTF8&isInIframe=0&n=283155&ref_=dp_proddesc_0&s=books&showDetailProductDesc=1#product-description_feature_div): "Ian Johnson is not only an outstanding journalist but also a tireless researcher. He is a first-rate writer." [Writer and journalist Peter Hessler commented](https://www.amazon.com/Souls-China-Return-Religion-After/dp/1101870052/ref=tmm_hrd_swatch_0?_encoding=UTF8&qid=1521213693&sr=1-1): "Most books about China focus on politics or economics; *The Souls of China* is the finest exploration of the cultural and moral life of ordinary citizens."

![Ian Johnson's student ID card from his exchange at Peking University. Source: Ian Johnson's personal website](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180316221300oaXcdLvzqP3mQyiI.jpg-w600)

Ian Johnson first came to China in 1984. That year, the 22-year-old came on exchange from the University of Florida to Peking University. At the time, his favorite activity was riding Flying Pigeon bicycles with friends, using 《纳格尔百科指南》(*The Nagel Guide*) to track down the temples and Taoist shrines scattered around Beijing. 《纳格尔百科指南》was a travel guide compiled before the Cultural Revolution by a group of French scholars and embassy staff, listing the locations of many temples later destroyed or closed during the Cultural Revolution. He also watched Peking University students unfurl a "Hello, Xiaoping" banner during the National Day military parade, and the festive scene reminded him of Woodstock.

After his half-year exchange ended, Ian Johnson returned to the United States. After graduating from college, he first worked for a year and a half at the Orlando Sentinel in Florida, then quit to study Chinese for two years at National Taiwan Normal University. In Taiwan, Ian Johnson not only had the good fortune to witness Taiwan's economic takeoff in the 1980s but also met his future wife, who was then his neighbor, doing research in Taiwan through the Free University of Berlin. During his studies in Taiwan, he and his future wife also took a trip to Berlin and fell in love with the city. So in 1988, Ian Johnson went from Taiwan to Berlin to learn German, and then enrolled at the Free University of Berlin to study China's economic reforms. While studying, he kept writing for the media. "There was so much to write about because the Berlin Wall had fallen," Ian Johnson said.

Out of his interest in China, after working as a reporter in New York for two years, he finally got the chance to be posted to Beijing, serving successively as Beijing bureau chief of the Baltimore Sun and China bureau chief of The Wall Street Journal. He stayed for nine years. During those nine years, he not only wrote a large volume of news reports but also completed his first book, *Wild Grass*. *Wild Grass* tells three stories about grassroots rights defense in China at the end of the 20th century. Ian Johnson believes civil society is an important force in China's modern transformation.

"The driving force of change comes mainly from people we rarely hear about: the small-town lawyer who decides to sue the government, the lawyers who stand up to support people whose homes have been forcibly demolished by the government, the women who try to expose police brutality. Some fight for the local interests of their families or villages; others are inspired by idealism," Ian Johnson wrote in *Wild Grass*.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180316222043B8byahFcNftiDu4R.png-w600)

In 2001, although the manuscript of *Wild Grass* was finished, the 9/11 attacks happened that year, and American publishing houses postponed all books on topics other than terrorism and Islam. So *Wild Grass* was not published until 2004. And because the three terrorists in the 9/11 attacks had all become radicalized in Germany, Ian Johnson developed an interest in German mosques. That year, he went to Germany and became bureau chief of The Wall Street Journal's German bureau.

While in Germany, Ian Johnson began investigating the Munich mosque, research that eventually became the book 《慕尼黑的清真寺》(*A Mosque in Munich*). Ian Johnson discovered that the Munich mosque was the European headquarters of the Muslim Brotherhood, the Islamist organization. And it was the Muslim Brotherhood that turned the mosque into a grassroots political organization serving partisan goals. Seen historically, since World War II, Islam has been used by governments as a political tool. So no religious belief is wrong; what is wrong is extremism. That is also the root of current problems such as terrorism and ISIS.

"I found three groups that supported mosques in order to achieve set goals. The first was Nazi thinkers, who planned to use Islam as a political weapon during World War II — a strategy that was later continued into the Cold War. The second consisted mainly of members of the American CIA, who built on the Nazis' work and hoped to use Islam against communism. The third group was the Islamist radicals, who saw the mosque as a foothold in the West. These three groups had one thing in common: their goal was not to build a place of worship, but a center of political — even violent — activity," Ian Johnson wrote in *A Mosque in Munich*.

![《慕尼黑的清真寺》(*A Mosque in Munich*), published under the name Ian Johnson in mainland China by Shanghai Translation Publishing House.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180316222207C0XLGobP1lAFO74f.jpg-w600)

In 2009, Ian Johnson returned to China to continue observing, researching, and writing about Chinese society. In 2017, he published his new book *The Souls of China*. The book describes the revival of Han Chinese religion since the Cultural Revolution. Ian Johnson believes that roughly 400 to 500 million people in China now have religious beliefs. Every society should have its religious beliefs. Chinese society is returning to a normal state. "I think many Chinese people are searching for a new set of values. They may feel that society is missing some values — you often hear people say, 'society has no bottom line.' Some people feel that religion can help in this respect and bring some values," Ian Johnson told QDaily.

Now Ian Johnson is not only a Beijing correspondent for The New York Times; he also teaches American students at The Beijing Center at the University of International Business and Economics, and serves as a consulting editor for the academic journal *The Journal of Asian Studies*.

Faced with a world in upheaval, Ian Johnson keenly feels the decline of newspapers and the changes in the public. "The problem in many countries, I think, is that people can't tell facts from opinions. Many newspapers have gone under, so many people no longer know the basics. It's hard to vote. Real democracy is not just about whether people vote for Trump or Hillary; it's also about democracy at the grassroots level. (Through the media), citizens get enough information to make rational decisions," he said.

This article is part of QDaily's interview with Ian Johnson; more topics will appear in future installments of the series.

### Religion is a political question, but not a tool of politics

**Q: Your new book *The Souls of China* describes the revival of Han Chinese religion. In your view, what does this religious revival mean? And what influence will it have on Chinese society in the future?**

**Z:** I think many Chinese people are searching for a new set of values. They may feel that society is missing some values — you often hear people say, "society has no bottom line." Some people feel that religion can help in this respect and bring some values.

Actually, I don't think this is only China's problem; other countries around the world have it too. Because over the past century, as the era of modernization arrived, most societies in the world became economies centered on economics. Whether you run a museum, a school, or a company, the first question is making money — as if making money is the most important thing.

I think many people feel that such a society is missing some values. Many people, whether in China or around the world, are looking for values — traditional religions as well as new beliefs — and they may feel these are useful to them.

**Q: So you think the religious revival has helped establish new values, right?**

**Z:** Yes.

**Q: But you think it has no connection to political reform? Why?**

**Z:** Not necessarily a connection to political reform. But I have also noticed that the Chinese government pays attention to new values too. Things previously condemned as superstition are now treated by the government with relative openness and tolerance. Recently, the government has also launched many "intangible cultural heritage" (ICH) projects — of course, ICH is not necessarily related to religion, but much of it is.

For example, take Mazu — you could say she used to be part of Taoism, but Mazu temples do not belong to the Taoist Association; they are cultural centers. Every year there are certain activities, all supported by the Ministry of Culture. Many people also go to the temples to burn incense, feeling it gives them some psychological comfort. Beijing's Miaofeng Mountain — its temple fair is a national-level ICH item, and there are many incense societies, associations organized by worshippers, which also receive district- or municipal-level support. Although the deity of the temple is Bixia Yuanjun, the temple doesn't actually belong to the Taoist Association; it is not a site for religious activities but belongs to a listed company. In the 1990s, Mentougou (the district government) put all its tourism assets into a tourism development company (Beijing Culture), which then listed in Shenzhen. Jietai Temple, Tanzhe Temple, Miaofeng Mountain — many of them are in it. But for two weeks every year, from the first to the fifteenth day of the fourth lunar month, many people go there to burn incense, feeling it can give their lives meaning and their faith support.

**Q: Your previous book was *A Mosque in Munich*, which at the end raised real-world questions. It feels that, in your view, the roots of today's problems like terrorism and ISIS are the same as what *A Mosque in Munich* describes: governments have all used religion as a political tool. No religious belief is wrong; what is wrong is extremism. Can one put it that way? Could you elaborate?**

**Z:** That's the danger. If you turn religion into a tool of politics, it easily becomes politicized. My view is that religion is best not turned into a tool of politics. But in some countries, many religions have in fact been politicized. You can see this in Western history — Catholicism and Christianity too — and in recent decades it has been Islam, in some Middle Eastern countries.

Specifically, the book (*A Mosque in Munich*) is about the Muslim Brotherhood. They have a fundamental problem: they divide the world in two and view the world in an extremist way. Half of the world's people are good, pure Muslims, and the other half are bad, impure Muslims. Look at the terrorists — the people they kill most are Muslims. If you don't believe in Islam, or you are a bad Muslim, you are not counted as a person, so I can kill you. Other people are not people, they are black; only people on our side are good, are white. I think this is the biggest misunderstanding.

**Q: So you also think they kill mostly the so-called impure Muslims?**

**Z:** Yes — impure, not good enough. So ISIS is also a form (of extremism), pretty much like the Muslim Brotherhood. (But) some people in the Muslim Brotherhood are not terrorists; they are intellectuals. I have also learned of some who are very learned, but fundamentally their thinking contains this kind of misunderstanding (meaning extremism). So although their behavior is not that of terrorists, their thinking can lead to the thinking and behavior of terrorists. Because if you say some people are not people, then the next step is: I can kill you, no problem.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180316222743OFmYdcpzeng1tCA7.png-w600)

**Q: I'd like to ask a general question: if a government uses religion as a political tool, what consequences or effects will that have for society?**

**Z:** Because in fact every society has many different religions — including Islamic countries like Iran, which also has Christians, Jews, and so on. So I feel that if you only recognize and support certain religions, what happens to the others? Take the UK: it has a state religion (church), the Church of England. It receives a lot of government support, which creates big problems for other religions.

You can encourage the differentiation of religions within a society. Because other religions are not necessarily enemies; sometimes religions can coexist harmoniously. If you only support some of them, they may turn into enemies.

The Chinese government now relatively supports Taoism, Buddhism, and some folk beliefs — ICH items like Miaofeng Mountain — but regards Islam, Christianity, and Catholicism as problematic. Because they involve foreign histories and contexts, there seems to be no problem for now, but in the long term (there may be problems).

**Q: Brent Fulton, who studies Christianity in China, says the main problem facing China's underground churches is not political; large-scale urbanization has had a huge impact on China's ever-growing churches. What do you think of this view?**

**Z:** Urbanization is actually very interesting. For example, 30 years ago, when I first came to China, 80% of people lived in the countryside; now it's basically half and half. So many people have left the countryside for big cities, and most of them have lost the basic infrastructure of religion. Because many of China's traditional temples and religious activities used to be concentrated in the countryside. Now that people have moved to cities, where should they go? How is the question of faith to be resolved? Some religions are relatively flexible and can cope with this problem more easily. Take Christianity: it basically doesn't need a dedicated place (to provide religious services) — I can do it in my living room, a few people meet on Sunday. But if I practice Taoism or Buddhism, there should be a temple; without a temple, what am I to do?

This is not to speak ill of the (current) government. Because over the past 100 years — before 1949 — many temples were already destroyed. There have been very concrete investigations: in the late Qing dynasty there were roughly 900, almost 1,000 temples within Beijing — that is, inside today's Third Ring Road. How many temples are there within the Third Ring Road now? I don't know. Twenty at most. So there is a shortage of concrete places; you want to worship, but there's nowhere to go. The suburbs may have a few more, but getting to the suburbs is a hassle. So the concrete problem is: people have arrived in the cities, but the cities have no temples.

### The mistake shared by strongmen and the need for strongmen: thinking problems are simple

**Q: Here's a big question. In recent years, many people feel the world is trending toward ever greater closure. Globalization and universal values have both run into problems. What do you make of it? Why do you think it has become this way?**

**Z:** Maybe many people feel the problems are very complicated, so they feel the country needs a strongman as a leader to solve them. This is probably one of the reasons Americans elected Trump and Russians like Putin so much. Exactly why is hard to say.

**Q: Do you think, looking at history, that the emergence of strongmen can solve problems?**

**Z:** No. Because these problems are not that simple. For example, Trump says he wants protectionism — raising tariffs and restricting steel imports. That way you can protect the American steel industry, people in the traditional steel industry will get rich, and they will recover their former status. That's too simple. Because if you do this and restrict steel imports from Europe or Canada, they will likewise restrict your exports.

So such strongmen always think solving problems is simple. (Solving them) needs a strongman to do it. "They're all weaklings; I'll do it — we need a tough guy." But the problems we face are in fact very complicated; not everything is as simple as he thinks, and he can't solve them all by himself. Reality is very complex and involves many parties — governments and non-governmental actors alike. Environmental protection, for instance, may be the world's biggest problem, and solving it also requires many people working together.

**Q: What do you think of nationalism in China today? Many people feel that China now closely resembles Germany during its rise as a great power around the turn of the 20th century — nationalism running high — and that further development along this line is dangerous. What do you think?**

**Z:** I don't really want to compare China with Germany, because in the end Germany started World War II. I don't think China will do that at present. But in some respects, there are indeed similarities with Germany (of that era): both are rising.

**Q: Why do you think China won't start a war?**

**Z:** Because the world's institutions are still fairly stable now. Back then, there was no international organization like the United Nations. Of course, many wars arise from misunderstandings — the North Korea–US issue, for example. But probably other countries would now mediate these misunderstandings. (So) at the moment I don't think China will start a war. Of course, there are still wars internationally, but few. And most of them are started by the United States, invading this country and that. It's also unlikely that a war breaks out between two major powers, especially when both have nuclear weapons.

**Q: What about a trade war?**

**Z:** I think that's more likely, though still not certain. In the West there are many populists. France is doing better; the Italian election just now also brought populists in. Germany is avoiding this. Both the UK and the US have it. I don't quite understand Trump's approach. His approach is unlikely to work, but at the moment no one is restraining him. People often say he only talked that way during the election campaign and wouldn't do it as president. Or that he wants to do it, but the Republicans won't necessarily let him. There are many such claims, and they have all turned out wrong. Now with the steel matter, I don't know whether he will really impose the tariffs.

### The decline of traditional newspapers, and Trump — like many Chinese — who can't tell facts from opinions

**Q: Is there anything that particularly worries you about society today?**

**Z:** The problem in many countries, I think, is that people can't tell facts from opinions. Many people go online, but they don't know which things are actually facts and which are opinions. There used to be relatively reliable news sources — there are still many good news sources now — but many people no longer know what's good. We all now live in a post-truth era. Real news they take for fake news. Trump keeps saying The Washington Post and The New York Times are "fake news." I think it may be the same in China. I've seen articles on WeChat that look professional and very convincing, but are actually not the case. This is a real headache. Because both people and governments have to make decisions. If you go by this nonsense, these half-true tidbits of information, it's hard to make good decisions.

**Q: So how do you think this problem can be solved?**

**Z:** That's a big question. Hard to say; I don't know. Probably we still need to strengthen education. They have done many experiments — lots of people can't tell the difference. My sister teaches at a high school; once she sent me a piece of fake news about Obama — she couldn't tell either.

**Q: What do you think makes for good public discussion? What should we pay attention to when expressing ourselves? Because a very prominent situation now is that high-quality public discussion is rarely seen — name-calling and taking sides are the mainstream.**

**Z:** When Weibo was hot, it was a space for public discussion; now that's completely gone. Now everyone uses WeChat. WeChat is a lot like Facebook: what you see is all posted by your friends, more or less the same opinions, so it's relatively weak in public-ness. Weibo is a bit like Twitter — you can still see other people's opinions. But many people on Twitter also only follow the people they want to follow. I don't read the tweets of Trump's supporters either. Old newspapers had many problems, (but also many good points). For example, I worked long ago at a local paper in Florida, the Orlando Sentinel; basically everyone read that paper. The paper wasn't necessarily very good, but it carried some basic information and ran Associated Press reports. AP isn't a great media outlet either, but it's passable; it would report what Congress did yesterday, what decisions it made — content that is fairly objective.

If someone cared about sports news, while flipping through the paper they couldn't help but glance at the headlines and would pick up a few things. But now we can manage to care only about what we want to care about and skip the rest entirely. If you want political news, you won't look at AP either. Old newspapers had problems because they were monopolies — a city had just one paper — but they still served a public function. They would put the news they considered most important on the front page. Even if you didn't want to read it, some things you would still end up seeing. Now you can read nothing but sports news if you like.

We used to visit newspaper offices and ask the circulation department where they sold the papers. The answer back then was simple: when people buy houses they subscribe to newspapers, so every middle-class family subscribed when it bought a house. Now it's completely different. My sister lives in Florida; when I go there for a morning walk, there are no newspapers at people's doors anymore. They used to roll the paper up in a plastic bag and toss it at subscribers' doors at 6 a.m. Now only 10% of households subscribe.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803162235167GgLvqfwWpSUsDAc.jpg-w600)

**Q: How do you view this change?**

**Z:** I think that, basically, for most people it's not a good thing. Not because I worked at The Wall Street Journal and The New York Times, but because newspapers used to have a public-education function. Although those papers were out to make money, 80% of them still had a sense of public service. We tried to put important news on the front page, covering national affairs and new problems, so that readers would learn the information they needed to know. Most American schools are in communities, and a school has a committee (school board); some of the committee's decisions make for very grassroots reporting, but they are important and very useful for residents. For example: what is this committee going to do? How should education be run? This committee also votes. Liu Yu, an associate professor at Tsinghua University, has a book called 《民主的细节》(*Details of Democracy*) in which she also wrote about this. But now many newspapers have gone under, so many people no longer know what the basics are. It's hard to vote. Real democracy is not just about whether people vote for Trump or Hillary; it's also about democracy at the grassroots level. (Through the media), citizens get enough information to make rational decisions — school committees, for example.

**Q: Do you have any ideas for solving these problems? Or do you feel it's very hard?**

**Z:** There are many models. For example, wealthy people set up foundations to support media development. Because a newspaper had many sections and was comprehensive — most people wanted to read the sports section, but still had to subscribe to the whole paper. Now online you can just watch sports. Newspapers used to make money from advertising, say, ads from car companies, but now car companies don't go to newspapers for ads anymore; they can advertise online.

### **Traditional Chinese medicine has its merits, but it needs to become science — and the government should do it**

**Q: What will your next book be?**

**Z:** I want to write about traditional Chinese medicine. I'm very interested in it. In the West there are very few books about Chinese medicine.

**Q: Why write about traditional Chinese medicine? Any thoughts?**

**Z:** I haven't started writing yet. I think the question of "how to treat illness" differs in every society and is very symbolic of the society it belongs to.

**Q: What's your attitude toward traditional Chinese medicine?**

**Z:** It can treat many things, though it also has some shortcomings and can't cure everything. I think it's an empirical method of healing. It's not arrived at through the path of science, with rigorous argumentation, but it has been refined through hundreds of years of people's experience. There are also debates, because many people wrote a great deal about Chinese medicine over those centuries.

**Q: So it doesn't matter that it's not science — it has experience?**

**Z:** Right. I don't think it's absolute; some things can be turned into science. Turning them into science requires experiments, and experiments are expensive. Say you want to run an experiment on a cold-remedy granule now; who makes money from it afterward? There's no patent, and pharmaceutical companies can't profit from it. So the government should actually do this.

**Q: I'm personally curious: in your view, how does one become a good reporter? Are there any standards for a good reporter?**

**Z:** These days none of us can be good reporters anymore, because the good media outlets are all gone (laughs). But I think it's best to find an editor who can help you. While you can learn reporting in school, it's best to have someone to guide you, something like a master-apprentice relationship. Without someone to help (steer you in the right direction), it's very hard to do it all on your own. So I think it's best to find a good editor who can help you revise your articles and gradually help you improve — someone like a mentor. It's like carpentry: to become a good carpenter, you definitely need a good master carpenter to guide you. That's a way of training.

**Q: An editor who influenced you a great deal?**

**Z:** There were some at The Wall Street Journal, and also Peter Hessler. He also gave me a lot of help; he's a very gifted person, and when he reads my books he gives me comments. For big, complex pieces, I also ask him to read them for me and give me comments.
