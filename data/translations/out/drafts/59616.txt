When a word like "history" is set before us, it is usually swathed in grand narratives. We wanted to sort things out, as best we could, through more interviews at the micro level. We began this record with "housing," the topic people care about most in their personal lives.

Starting from Laoximen subway station in Shanghai, Wang Jiazhou crosses Zhonghua Road and walks down Menghua Street toward the home that lies one lane away from the Confucius Temple, where he is going to see his parents. The high-rises and wide avenues vanish in an instant; what lies ahead are narrow lanes and a low skyline.

This area is called "Laoximen" (Old West Gate), originally the site of Yifeng Gate, the west gate of the Shanghai county wall built in the Ming dynasty. The old walled district is now known as the "Old City" (laochengxiang), cradled by Zhonghua Road and Renmin Road — two roads that were once the moat. The Old City is regarded as the birthplace of Shanghai as an urban center, and even after Shanghai opened to foreign trade in the late Qing, it remained the Chinese-administered district adjoining the concessions.

Wang Jiazhou graduated from a technical secondary school and works as a driver on a 120 ambulance (China's emergency number). Born in 1985, he came into the world in a small town called "Gucheng" ("Old City") in Le'an County, Fuzhou, in eastern Jiangxi — actually a new town, whose name comes from a nearby village also called "Gucheng."

In 1957, uranium was discovered at Xiangshan near Gucheng, and the next year geologists arrived to sink mine shafts. Soon this became China's largest uranium mine — Mine 721 — and specialists streamed in from all over the country. By its peak in the early 1990s, Gucheng, home to the mine's headquarters, had a population of 50,000.

Wang Jiazhou's father, Wang Le, was an educated youth who had gone out into the world from this very courtyard by the Confucius Temple in Shanghai's Laoximen. In 1970, while doing manual labor in Le'an as a sent-down youth, he was by chance recruited into Mine 721, and he lived and worked there for 29 years until returning to settle in Shanghai in 2000. His mother, Yao Lan, is from Pingxiang, Jiangxi; at age six she moved with her parents to Mine 721, later went to work at the mine, and retired in 2005, after which she came to Shanghai to rejoin her husband and son.

![Menghua Street](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181224115235fldrULc3651W8MVQ.JPG-w600)

In the courtyard, Wang Le is washing vegetables at a row of sinks, his voice booming, his laughter hearty. Yao Lan had been chatting with her husband; now she nods and smiles at the visitor.

Their home is one of five units created by partitioning a row of tiled houses about ten meters wide with wooden boards; it is further split into two levels. The ground-floor room is about two meters high, two meters wide and five meters deep. A large bed fills the entire width of the room at its innermost end, taking up nearly a third of the floor space. A wooden ladder stands at the doorway, and you must climb hands and feet to reach the loft — another bedroom where, because of the sloping roof, the area you can stand in is even smaller than below, though the roof rises high at its peak, and daylight comes in through a dormer window. Neither level has a bathroom; the kitchen is tacked on outside, two square meters, just big enough for a stove and one standing person.

![The dormer window](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181224112853Rs5FKPOkS08pfDnM.JPG-w600)

![The over-street building](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181224114218P9HGsmKwdMo2aIZh.JPG-w600)

Wang Le says that because of the added-on structures and a later-added side door, casual visitors don't notice that this is actually the last courtyard of a traditional three-courtyard Jiangnan mansion. They live in the rearmost row of houses, which would originally have served as storage rooms, so it is lower than the houses in front. His grandmother, having brought her children from Hangzhou to Shanghai to reunite with her sisters, lived here earliest. The rooms would have been larger then, but by his childhood the house already had its current layout. He was the fifth of six siblings, and at the time the whole family of eight crowded into these two levels totaling 17.5 square meters.

When Wang Le and his son returned to Shanghai in 2000, Wang Le's mother and his third brother with his daughter were still living in the house, so the father and son borrowed a 10-square-meter single room from a neighbor, at a monthly rent of 300 yuan. In 2005, Wang Jiazhou's mother came to Shanghai from Jiangxi and they all lived together in the rented room. In 2008, the third brother and his daughter moved out; Wang Le and his wife moved back into the old house to live with his mother, and Wang Jiazhou stayed in the single room. He was 23 then, and for the first time had a room of his own. When Wang Le's mother died in 2010, Wang Jiazhou gave up the rented room and moved into the old house with his parents. He married and moved out in 2013, and his parents have lived in the old house ever since.

Starting that summer, the government gave the houses here a fresh coat of paint, replaced the roof tiles, and even installed flush toilets. The toilet takes up one square meter of the Wangs' cramped living space, one meter from the dining table, with no partition. In the northern part of the Old City, two large blocks are undergoing mass relocation this year. I asked Wang Le whether the government renovating the houses meant they would not be relocated. "Not necessarily — the government's decisions change often too," he said.

He told me that although the house counts as a "shantytown" dwelling, it could still sell for 2.6 million yuan, mainly because it is school-district housing zoned for several of the Old City's best schools. But six household registrations are tied to the house: besides Wang Le's family of three, there are his third brother and daughter, and the two-year-old child of a niece. As for selling, the niece has not made up her mind.

So the matter sits. For 18 years, the Wang family has watched the neighborhood change. A few years back, on every fine day, a row of old people would sit out in the lane; now nearly all of them have died. Of the 13 households around the courtyard, all old neighbors of decades standing, only 7 remain — the young people have all moved away, some houses stand empty, and the other 6 households have become low-income migrant workers.

### "The Luckiest Arrangement"

Wang Le was born in 1953, in the last of the "old three classes" — the high school cohorts of 1966–68. In late 1968, Mao Zedong issued the call for "educated youth to go to the countryside," and being sent down was the inevitable fate for young people his age; the only question was where.

The next year, the school sent notice that he was to go to a military reclamation farm in Heilongjiang. But everyone knew that remote, bitterly cold Heilongjiang was the worst option. Just then, the Shen family's eldest daughter from next door sent word from Fuzhou: Fuzhou could take in all the younger brothers and sisters from the courtyard. This sister, ten years Wang Le's senior, had gone to Yongxiu, Jiangxi, in 1960 to attend the Communist Labor University; after graduating she was assigned to Fuzhou, and in 1968 had just been sent down to a local village for re-education. Her production team had no quota for taking in educated youth, but she did not want her own brother and the neighbor children to suffer elsewhere, so she went specially to request a few slots.

The familiar companions could go to the same place — Jiangxi, neither hot nor cold, said to be somewhere "no one ever starved to death" — and the young people and their families were overjoyed. In April 1970, Wang Le and his neighbor friends took the train together to the countryside of Le'an, Fuzhou.

"To this day I still think it was the luckiest arrangement," Wang Le says.

The first year they grew rice, with more than two days off each week. The government provided a monthly ration of 40 jin of rice, half a jin of oil and half a jin of meat, plus 8 yuan in pocket money. When the year's grain and oil ration ran out, the production brigade took two buckets of rapeseed oil — originally meant for sizing cloth at the brigade's ramie-fabric factory — and gave it to the educated youth to eat. Another half year passed, and Mine 721 came to the village to recruit workers. Everything Wang Le knew of Mine 721 at the time was that the only bus on the road belonged to this big work unit. In the end, every one of that cohort of Shanghai educated youth signed on. Wang Le says no one then ever expected to return to Shanghai; seeing a chance to enter a state-owned enterprise, he decided on his own, without time even to tell his family.

Once at the mine, none of that cohort of Shanghai educated youth went underground; all were assigned surface work. Wang Le guesses someone in Fuzhou was looking out for them. From the second year on, all newly hired young men went down the shafts. The new workers came from all over the country, but the two biggest groups were the educated youth from Shanghai and young workers transferred from mining and industrial enterprises in the north, each numbering one to two thousand. Jiangxi natives were a minority, mostly from Fuzhou and Nanchang. As in many large state-owned enterprises, the lingua franca of Mine 721 was Mandarin.

No one knew exactly what effects a uranium mine would have on health — only that it was bad for the body, and that those who worked underground tended to develop liver and lung diseases. For this reason, Mine 721 employees were paid better than at ordinary enterprises: two cans of milk powder per person per month, plus several jin of eggs, vegetable oil and pork.

There were also older, skilled workers. Yao Lan's father came from a Jiangxi mining-machinery factory and was assigned to the same workshop as Wang Le; Wang Le did maintenance while his father-in-law did machining.

In 1981, Wang Le married Yao Lan, then a telephone operator, in Liantang Village, at the time the mine's headquarters. The following January the two moved into a 16-square-meter workers' dormitory in Gucheng, six kilometers away. It was a three-story Soviet-style building with four households per floor, a corridor running down the middle, rooms on the south side, each family's kitchen on the north, and one shared bathroom per floor.

Built in 1964, its blue-brick exterior looked brand new again every time it rained. A river called Gongxi ran along the eastern side of the small town, with a riverbed 200 meters wide; over the following 20 years, Wang Le would watch the sandbars in the riverbed keep growing until, at a glance, they looked like a seaside beach. He believes this was caused by Mine 721's ever-growing population. In the years when electricity and liquefied-gas supplies fell short, people went up the hills to cut firewood, and a small hill could be stripped bare in a few months. At the mine's peak in the early 1990s, Mine 721 under the Ministry of Nuclear Industry had 11,000 employees, and Gucheng, as the living quarter for workers and their families, reached a population of 50,000.

![The three-story Soviet-style workers' dormitory in Gucheng, provided by Wang Jiazhou](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181224190834W10fUmjYEsli3PIq.jpg-w600)

"In our Wang family, I was the first to have a TV, the first to have a sofa, and the first to have a son!" Wang Le raised his voice when recalling these old stories.

In 1985, Wang Jiazhou was born at the Gucheng Hospital of Mine 721, the best medical facility in all of Fuzhou. That year, Wang Le went to the supply-and-marketing cooperative in neighboring Chongren County and spent 1,190 yuan — roughly 30 months' wages — on a 14-inch Japanese ORION color TV, and added a three-seat sofa to the tiny living space.

"Our work unit was pretty ahead of its time," Wang Le says. Though deep in remote mountains, the living conditions and cultural atmosphere of the state-owned enterprise were far superior to those in the surrounding locality. The mine handed out fresh fruit and frozen hairtail and other nonlocal foodstuffs year-round; by the late 1990s, every small work unit had its own TV station, and Gucheng's grand auditorium, with 1,996 seats, broadcast holiday performances live to every mining area (Wang Jiazhou often watched his father sing his heart out on those broadcasts). New loanwords from outside kept entering the vocabulary of Mine 721's workers — they were saying "cool" in the 1990s — and were dubbed "mine speak" by the locals.

Yao Lan says that because there were so many Shanghai people, every year after Spring Festival, when everyone came back from family visits to Shanghai, you could see right on Gucheng's streets what down jackets and what bags were in fashion in Shanghai that year. Wang Jiazhou says that one year, KFC — in Shanghai or Nanchang, he no longer remembers which — ran a "schoolbag meal deal," and after the holiday, classmates came to school one after another carrying KFC schoolbags. He also says that Gucheng in the 1990s was just like the film *Shanghai Dreams* — news arriving from outside always stirred restlessness in the little town's minds.

Mine 721's ore was sent by rail to the northwest for refining, supplying the key raw material for the development of nuclear weapons and nuclear power. Its rise and fall depended entirely on the country's nuclear-industry policy. In fact, from the 1980s and 1990s onward, the state began imposing production limits on Mine 721. Yao Lan remembers that by 2000, ore from Africa was not only cheaper than Mine 721's but of better quality. The system of state-owned enterprises running their own society also made Mine 721's organization bloated. "Ninety percent of people never went down the shafts," Wang Le says. "Landscape section, housing section, welfare section, the TV university, the water and power company, the radio station — they had every kind of department, just like a city," says Yao Lan. By the late 1990s, the mine began encouraging workers to transfer out, even giving receiving work units a subsidy of 20,000 yuan per person; each transferred employee got two cubic meters of timber to pack up their belongings, and the mine sent trucks to carry them off to all corners of the country. Those receiving units were as far away as Jiangsu-Zhejiang and North China, and came from entirely unrelated industries — a glucose factory, for instance.

"In that period, almost every day some family was packing up. In the end, one-third of the people transferred out," Yao Lan recalls. "On the one hand, everyone wanted to return to their roots in old age, but the more important factor was not wanting the next generation to grow up under the shadow of nuclear radiation." She and Wang Le acknowledge that radiation never caused panic at Mine 721 — but having the next generation face it was another matter entirely.

### "You've Come to Shanghai to Suffer With Me!"

In 1979, amid the wave of educated youth returning to the cities, Yao Lan came to Shanghai with Wang Le for the first time. Wang Le had no plans to return to the city; he was preparing to marry Yao Lan in Jiangxi.

Wang Le had long given Yao Lan a "vaccination" — the family home was very small — but without saying how small, so when Yao Lan walked in she was still shocked. At the time Wang Le's father lay in bed coughing, a coal stove burning in the corner. Wang Le himself was not used to it either. "Every time I came back to Shanghai, it took me several days to recover," he says. It was not only the smallness; there was also no toilet, no bathroom, and he, accustomed to the modern life of the state enterprise, felt the letdown in Shanghai.

"You've come to Shanghai to suffer with me!" As he said this, Wang Le turned and smiled at his wife.

In 1987, two-year-old Wang Jiazhou, carried in someone's arms, came to Shanghai by train for the first time. From then on, the family returned to Shanghai at least twice every three years. The route never changed: board the train at Jiangbian Station in Gucheng, take the 123-kilometer Xiangle line built in the 1960s to haul ore, pick up the pre-bought tickets from Wang Le's local friend in Nanchang, then ride a hard seat from Nanchang to Shanghai.

"So at three he could already draw trains, and he drew them so well!" The avatar Wang Jiazhou most likes to use online is a photo of himself at age ten. In it, wearing a T-shirt and shorts, he licks a popsicle facing the camera, while on the railway bridge behind him a red train speeds past. That was his train-watching experience while visiting his father, who was helping build the Daya Bay Nuclear Power Plant in Shenzhen.

But he only formally became a railfan after returning to Shanghai. In 2004 he stumbled on a few train forums — a whole new world, crowded with people obsessed with arcane knowledge and eager to exchange it — a great enrichment to the often lonely life of his post-return years. Since then he has spent every vacation on what railfans call "operations": riding trains to survey particular lines and examine everything about the railway — route design, station placement, infrastructure, the human geography along the way. In 2014, for instance, before the new Guanjiao tunnel on the Qinghai–Tibet line was completed, he made a special trip from Shanghai to ride the famous Guanjiao switchbacks west of Qinghai Lake one last time — the loops known as "the ladder to heaven," soon to be history.

As we chat, the door stands open and the neighbors are coming home one after another, calling in to say hello now and then. Though the talk is all family matters, the family minds not at all that outsiders can hear. It seems the cramped quarters and mixed-tenancy living have given them a different notion of privacy from people in commodity housing. In earlier years, the Wangs were the only household in the courtyard with a round tabletop; neighbors hosting guests would come to borrow it. If his own family entertained, the tabletop would be carried to a neighbor's house — in effect borrowing the neighbor's room. The neighbor would finish dinner early and go out for a walk, giving up the room. There was even a case where a neighbor had so many wedding guests that they borrowed several neighbors' houses, setting one table in each.

These are the things he saw after returning to Shanghai in 2000.

### The Whole Family Registers

Wang Jiazhou's household registration was moved back to Shanghai in 1995. It should not have happened that early. That year, the Confucius Temple area was rumored to be slated for relocation, and to prevent residents from suddenly adding heads to their households to win more compensation, the government planned to freeze household registrations. The neighborhood committee therefore allowed the children of educated youth to move their registrations back before the freeze. In Wang Le's memory, besides that time, relocation was rumored once more in 2001, and never raised again afterward.

In 2000, with Wang Jiazhou about to finish junior high, his parents felt it was time to send him back to Shanghai; any later, and the language adjustment would be harder. The family generally spoke Mandarin at home; Wang Jiazhou had learned Shanghainese from his father and the Shanghai people at the mine, but his accent and vocabulary had already diverged from Shanghai's own.

One day that May, Wang Jiazhou set out alone with a bag, formally embarking on his "return to Shanghai." He took a five-hour train from Jiangbian Station to Nanchang, picked up his ticket and had a meal at his father's friend's home, then rode 12 more hours in a hard seat to reach Shanghai. But nothing was that simple. That year, the whole family made 13 person-trips between Jiangxi and Shanghai — for Wang Jiazhou's high-school entrance exam, school selection and enrollment, or simply because his parents came to keep him company when they feared he was lonely.

On one of those trips, after leaving Shanghai, Wang Le went on to Lianyungang. He wanted to see whether he could transfer to work at the Tianwan Nuclear Power Plant. The pay there was far better than at Mine 721, and if the transfer went through he could focus on earning money there. He says that even then, like when he left home as a youth, he had never thought he absolutely had to return to Shanghai in this lifetime. But after learning Tianwan held no opportunity, he went back to Gucheng and handed in his resignation.

At the end of 2000, 31 years after leaving home, Wang Le returned to Shanghai to settle.

"Dad had a pretty hard time in that period," says Wang Le — referring to his father's first six years back in Shanghai. During that stretch Wang Le worked five or six jobs as a cook, sometimes in restaurants, sometimes in canteens, the shortest lasting half a year, the longest just over a year, with a few months at home in between.

Meanwhile, in those years Yao Lan banked large amounts of overtime to earn three months of vacation to come to Shanghai — in her words, "working three months as an hourly maid": caring for her aged mother-in-law, tending to her son and husband, and looking after the daily needs of a niece not yet married.

Like nearly all returned educated youth, they once faced the problem of household registration. Because settlement policy was unclear and left room for subjective discretion, Wang Le spent a whole year, from 2004 to 2005, going to the police station on every Thursday "station-chief reception day" to negotiate. His registration was finally resolved in 2005. Yao Lan's had to wait until 2010, five years after she came to Shanghai.

There is a [claim](http://wangzongren1952.blog.163.com/blog/static/9959590120110910125808/) that among Shanghai's one million-plus educated youth of that era, 30 percent did not return to Shanghai in the post-1978 wave of returns. As for how many of them, along with their spouses and children, eventually came back to Shanghai, relevant statistics are hard to find. But of Mine 721's retired educated youth alone, over a thousand returned to Shanghai — not counting their children and spouses. The registration problems of the old educated youth caused a large number of disputes with their families of origin. The main reason was the house — siblings who had become heads of household were unwilling to let them register, fearing they would come to carve up the property. To register smoothly, not a few old educated youth signed documents renouncing their claim to the property. But at the time of relocation or property transfer, the conflicts often flared up again.

In the impression of Wang Le and his wife, the most typical pattern among returned Jiangxi educated youth was for the children to be registered in Shanghai while the parents remained in Jiangxi, drawing their Jiangxi pensions even though physically in Shanghai. Some, having no house to live in, worked a few years in Shanghai and went back to Jiangxi. Whole families registering in Shanghai are extremely rare. Wang Le believes his family was able to register at the old house largely because his mother was still alive then — though his third brother is also a man who honors brotherly affection.

Retiring 14 years early in order to get his Shanghai registration and start paying into social insurance in time qualified Wang Le to receive Shanghai's monthly pension of 4,800 yuan — twice what he could have drawn in Jiangxi had he not retired early. Among the more than a thousand retired educated youth from Mine 721 who returned to Shanghai, the overwhelming majority draw Jiangxi pensions. Because fraud cases occurred, starting in a certain year they must mail their fingerprints to Jiangxi every year.

### "Tear It All Down and It's No Longer Shanghai's Old City"

The smallness of the room has one benefit — it is warm. But Yao Lan says that since the government replaced the roof tiles, the once airtight loft has become drafty on all sides, and the heater can't warm it. I followed up: is she looking forward to relocation? "How could I not? His grandmother used to say in her later years, 'I probably won't live to see this place relocated.'" As Wang Jiazhou reached marrying age, the parents grew more anxious.

![Dense rows of electricity meters in the corridor of the old house](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812241148264uBWqYRnc5wdmXGz.JPG-w600)

At Spring Festival in 2007, Shanghai housing prices were going crazy. The family of three spent the holiday at Wang Jiazhou's maternal grandmother's home in Pingxiang. Mother and son had merely gone out for a stroll when they came upon a new residential compound by the railway selling 110-square-meter apartments for 118,000 yuan, with a deposit of 200 yuan. On the spot, the two decided to buy one, purely as an investment.

In 2011, in Huaxin Town, Qingpu District — a far-flung western suburb of Shanghai — Wang Jiazhou spotted a small-property-rights three-bedroom unit selling for 320,000 yuan, one of several dozen buildings with a second phase planned. He sold the Pingxiang flat and bought this one, thinking of it as a possible marital home.

But Huaxin Town, after all, was a new district only just carved out of farmland, lacking any atmosphere of life. Starting in 2013, using the convenience of driving his ambulance all over the city, Wang Jiazhou made a point of scoping out housing in several suburban central towns, and finally settled on Jiading Old Town, which he judged to have the best living atmosphere. In 2016, he sold the Huaxin unit and bought a two-bedroom in an old compound next to the Jiading Old Town museum, at 18,000 yuan per square meter.

"Of course we hoped relocation would give our son a marital home, but we couldn't wait for it, so Jiazhou could only buy and sell here and there on his own," Yao Lan says.

Wang Jiazhou married in 2014 and lives in his wife's family's home; the Jiading flat is currently rented out. Wang Le and his wife are planning to move to Jiading after Spring Festival. Wang Le says he is a nostalgic man who likes the convenience and historical depth of Jiading Old Town. Like the old Shanghai county seat in history, Jiading Old Town is still surrounded by its moat, and not far from their home there is also a Confucius temple.

Unlike Yao Lan, he is not eager for relocation. "If we could knock through to the house next door and have two times this space, I'd still be willing to fix it up properly and live here," Wang Le says. "But right now it is really too small; renovation won't solve the problem." He even feels that with twice the space, compared with the relocation compensation terms of seven or eight years ago — resettlement housing closer to the city center and likely larger too — he would still prefer to stay in the old house.

Starting this year, north of Fuxing East Road, the two great blocks of Yalong and [Fuyou](http://news.cqnews.net/html/2018-11/18/content_50153388.htm) — also part of the Old City — have begun mass relocation, involving more than 10,000 households; at the same time, south of Fuxing East Road, they have started renovating the old houses.

![Jinjiafang in the Yalong block, being demolished](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181224114103gMRrDUpKPTBJfE5v.JPG-w600)

"Tear it all down and it's no longer Shanghai's Old City — you might as well call it 'Huangpu New City' or 'Nanshi New City'; it's all been cut to pieces," Wang Le says.

"The other day the media said a few streets here are going to be protected. I have mixed feelings about it. I don't want to leave either; I hope it gets renovated well — but the premise is improving the living conditions, and living here right now is really inconvenient," Wang Le adds. "Extracting some households is the best solution — then separate bathrooms and kitchens could be added." As to which households should be extracted, he thinks it should be those whose homes stand empty for long periods.

He recalled the Laoximen of his own youth, once the most commercially bustling stretch within the ring roads, where the flagship stores of a batch of time-honored brands lined up along Zhonghua Road — Qiaojiashan, Dafugui, Quantai, Lao Tongsheng. "I've noticed Shanghai's business model has changed. It used to be linear; now it's all turned into points — everything has moved into malls, and the streets have no one left."

"The resettlement compounds are dead and deserted — better to go back to Mine 721," he added. But this was only an expression of a certain sentiment, because Mine 721 was no longer somewhere one could return to. Last year he and Yao Lan went back to Gucheng once and saw the grass below their old house already waist-high. Mine 721 was restructured in 2002; of its original 10,000-plus employees, only just over 100 were kept, and Gucheng was essentially abandoned. The original residents moved to housing compounds financed and built in Fuzhou, Yingtan and Nanchang. Yao Lan and Wang Le received home-purchase quotas at the time too, but knowing they would never come back, they gave them up.

"Who would have thought of real-estate investment back then?" Wang Le says.
