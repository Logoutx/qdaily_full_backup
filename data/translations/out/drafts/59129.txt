When "whoever's hot gets in" overrides the logic of business itself, unknown risks follow. But for now, everyone is still caught up in the eager chase for opportunity.

**Some are the results of ongoing change — change that will go on to reshape our lives; some are interventions in the business world by forces even less controllable, forcing market participants to adjust their strategies from time to time; and some come from the companies themselves — once a company grows to a certain size, how does it reconcile itself with the public, with consumers, with government authority, and with other environmental factors.**

**The country you have determines the companies you get. In 2018, the business world changed as much as it always does. The big business stories of 2018 only confirm this.**

On the fifth floor of Shanghai's Shimao Festival City on Nanjing East Road, at the Cat's Kitchen experience store, COO Chen Zhuyu sat before a spreadsheet listing the problems she needed to solve:

"How do we cut the waiting time for customers eating noodles at the noodle counter? How should we set the food-prep process and standards? How do we train the offline store staff? Extremely trivial stuff."

Cat's Kitchen, a maker of food short videos, opened its first physical store in October 2018: 380 square meters, 4 million yuan in upfront investment, with four zones — a drinks bar, a noodle counter, retail, and an event space. The store manager had worked at MUJI; some of the staff came from Uniqlo.

"The team we put together this year is entirely new hires," Chen told QDaily. In the month or so since the soft opening, the retail side has run into quite a few problems. For instance, the store is still categorized on Dianping as "snacks and fast food," yet the dining area actually has only five seats.

The same misunderstanding surrounds Wutai, a new project that opened on level B1 of Beijing's Chaoyang Joy City in June 2018. Self-media called it ["a sci-fi version of Food Republic where you can eat at 27 vendors in one visit"](http://www.sohu.com/a/248926731_409172); its own descriptions are far more elaborate: ["a cross-over, content-driven new form of commerce," "a future consumption venue for young people," "a lifestyle magazine flowing through the mall"](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/oNLgP2GZE9imS4aatiLKSw)...

With a total area of 1,400 square meters, Wutai occupies what was originally Chaoyang Joy City's cluster of parent-child businesses. Now, 300 square meters are leased out for dining and retail, mostly to Beijing's internet-famous brands, with a projected brand turnover cycle of three to six months; the rest is exhibition space, a theater, and a lounge area. At the entrance you can see posters listing this week's events.

![From a tenancy-mix perspective, their line of copy may be more accurate: good food (dining) and good things (retail) are the new social currency (events).](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210211627Cof8V5Wc1kOFT4Ig.gif-w600Gif)

![The most recent event was a salon co-hosted with Tencent Video's Once Upon a Bite. Image: Dianping @Jinhuachengmiao](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210211711zCoY5mtlGkP6fdWA.JPG-w600)

Zheng Zheng, assistant general manager of Chaoyang Joy City, began paying attention three years ago to Wutai's operator Woodstock of Eating, a team that runs food markets, because its ["on-site events and experiences are exceptionally strong"](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/40306486). The space is still changing rapidly. Reportedly it will be reworked again in early 2019, carving out a small area for "urban culture."

["If you owned a shopping mall, you should really hate the word 'experience.' But now it's the other way around — everyone wears it as a badge of honor."](http://gz.news.fang.com/open/25819241.html) In 2017, Ronnie Chan, chairman of Hang Lung Properties, offered this assessment of the trend that had swept through shopping centers in recent years, in an interview with Winshang. At the time he argued that anything championing experience inevitably meant sacrificing sales per square meter — that is, a mall's sales per square meter of floor space.

But experience didn't let up in 2018. In May, Lujiazui Group's new commercial complex Jingyao Qiantan announced a partnership with sports platform Hupu, handing a 200-square-meter space over to the latter to operate. The fitness app Keep opened gyms at Beijing's China Central Place and Mofang shopping center. The official account Yitiao opened three Shanghai stores simultaneously in September. And a wave of brand flagship stores upgraded with experience as their selling point — the representative project being Nike's four-floor "Shanghai 001" at Shimao Festival City.

As supply and demand in the consumer sector inverted, physical retail — homogenized after years of sameness — has gradually had its functions replaced by e-commerce, and malls that long counted luxury and fast fashion as their standard anchor tenants urgently need an IP, a batch of new faces.

The new faces first of all have to be distinctive ("very hot, but not the kind of hot you see on every street"), popular within a certain crowd (no introduction needed, a guarantee of sales), and come with their own traffic (their own marketing budget on social networks)... Plus one point usually left unmentioned, which is what Ronnie Chan said: ["experience is a synonym for low rent."](http://gz.news.fang.com/open/25819241.html)

More than one brand told us in interviews that their store-opening schedules over the past year had been accelerated by malls' eagerness for new brands.

For these new tenants of malls, the reasons for opening stores are even more varied. Ordinary business expansion, flagship-store upgrades, online revenue hitting a ceiling, adding a physical concept to raise financing, prodding from new investors, or simply feeling that a physical store is a billboard with higher conversion rates... any of these can point to the decision to negotiate quickly, move in, and renovate, giving birth to an even grander expansion plan.

Each gets what it needs; the match is instant.

### 1.

The journalist Joan Didion wrote in 1979 that America's shopping malls had become "cities where no one lives but everyone consumes." Today, China's shopping centers have gone a step further, hoping to shape themselves into communities where people can both consume and live — something that government planning, developer investment, and the expansion of subway networks are all helping bring about.

The only problem is that there are already too many malls.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210042006Nnktd4HWcf8YaK50.jpg-w600)

Shanghai is the fastest-growing city in the country for shopping centers, with more than 230 urban commercial complexes. After a decade of development, Shanghai's [retail floor space per capita](https://www.jfdaily.com/wx/detail.do?id=104148) has gone from close to Tokyo's level to three times Tokyo's; the ratio of commercial floor space to resident population far exceeds the international norm of (0.7–1.2):1, reaching 3.5:1.

Over the past three years, new supply of Shanghai retail property has still grown at more than 20% per year; as of November, Shanghai had 43 newly opened projects in 2018 and 20 stock-renovation projects, with total floor area exceeding 2.9 million square meters.

Absorbing that much commercial space requires enough brands to move in — will there be enough tenants? This became a widespread concern among retail real-estate professionals in 2018.

"There aren't as many traditional anchor stores anymore," said Zhou Changqing, general manager of strategic consulting at RET. "Traditional high-end luxury or relatively standard brands are vacating space, and malls are doing tentative, experimental leasing — some of it to new brands, some of it built into themed blocks, the way Joy City handles it. Some space is even going to co-working or cultural areas."

"First stores" — an increasingly loose concept — became a keyword in the marketing of many mall projects this year. Zhongshang Data noted in a report that Shanghai opened 602 "first stores" in 2018; "the quantity and quality of first stores are bound up with those of newly opened malls."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812100419491Cni96hqXLQTA8sK.jpg-w600)

Unprecedented flagship stores, newly introduced international brands, internet companies opening offline stores, new brands/formats incubated by big companies... all can count as first stores.

On November 26, HKRI Taikoo Hui in Shanghai's Nanjing West Road business district held the first-anniversary celebration of its opening, while unveiling the Champion streetwear store on the mall's first floor; the latter had spent the previous two years active on Taobao as a daigou brand. Zhou Ying, CEO of HKRI Taikoo Hui, said she was "full of confidence" in Champion because it is "a brand people already love very much." On opening day, a queue line was cordoned off at the store entrance.

"HKRI Taikoo Hui has actually had more than 40 'first stores' since it opened," Zhou said. The example she cited was the [Starbucks Reserve Roastery](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/47953.html) that opened in December 2017.

This Champion at HKRI Taikoo Hui is not Shanghai's or China's "first store." The brand's China agent opened its first physical store at Hangzhou Tower in September 2017, then moved into Shenzhen, Xi'an, Harbin, and other cities in turn, opening its Shanghai first store at K11 in April 2018; HKRI Taikoo Hui is the third in Shanghai.

International brands usually prioritize first-tier cities when expanding retail; Champion is a counterexample of the past two years, the result of the interplay between rents and the channels controlled by its China agent. In fact, under HKRI Taikoo Hui's original plan, this streetwear brand selling a few hundred yuan sweatshirts would most likely never have gotten in at all.

Jointly developed by Swire Properties and HKR International with an investment of 17 billion yuan, the project sits in Shanghai's Nanjing West Road business district — the last large commercial land parcel in the city center of the past decade, with intense competition around it. When mall planning began in September 2014, its intended positioning was between top-tier luxury Plaza 66 and the light-luxury Jing An Kerry Centre.

The giant logos of luxury and fast fashion once occupied the first-floor anchor positions of shopping centers. Now they are either pushing into lower-tier markets or turning cautious about opening stores, while the brand capacity of core business districts was already near saturation. To fill the spaces anchors used to occupy, newly opened malls have had to start thinking about other options.

"Historically, we used to open three stores in a mall: one adidas Originals, one adidas neo, one adidas performance," James Grigsby, adidas's senior director of retail expansion strategy, told QDaily. In the future, "we will combine two or three of the brands into one. In that case, the average store size may go from 100–120 square meters to around 200–250 square meters."

![adidas Asia-Pacific Brand Center](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210213751JLYwR2C40lBzHIFe.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210213751BK7McXJHwTGxOtnm.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210213751KDVsENSYMjpmel39.jpg-w600)

"Sportswear brands are extremely active and will basically become a highlight of all newly opened projects," said Yao Yao, director of research at JLL. Judging from consumption trends, fast fashion is not expanding as strongly as sportswear.

As long as sportswear brands are willing to open hugely expensive flagship stores in malls, they can get better first-floor positions than the traditional sporting-goods floors (usually the fourth or fifth floors). But the street-facing Champion on the first floor of Shimao Festival City is just an ordinary retail store.

"The spot K11 gave it (level B1) is relatively more reasonable," commented a former leasing professional. "Professionally speaking, it's a sports-streetwear brand; if a mall has a streetwear zone, it should be placed in the streetwear zone — not given privileges just because it's hot. But the reality of supply and demand means it can open on the first floor of Nanjing East Road."

![Also on the first floor: under its new positioning, HKRI Taikoo Hui built an international beauty corridor encompassing 26 brands. Image: HKRI Taikoo Hui](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210222424F8enbUV91ZBriupy.jpg-w600)

"These days it no longer helps to be on familiar terms with Zara's store-expansion manager," said a practitioner who has worked on multiple retail real-estate stock-renovation projects over the past two years. "This is the result of the crude Ctrl-C-Ctrl-V development model of Chinese commercial real estate's 30-year golden period."

Li Deming's sense of it runs roughly through these stages: after 2013, luxury consumption was hit by the "eight-point rules" on official frugality, and malls began noticeably adding dining formats that the internet couldn't easily replace; around 2015, "parent-child zones" became popular among community malls, which raised the share of entertainment and leisure; starting in the second half of 2017, a wave of so-called experience stores and internet-company physical stores began to emerge.

The ultimate goal, of course, is to attract customers, keep brands alive, and let malls collect rent over the long term. Unlike fast fashion or even sportswear brands in their day, the "first stores" that come in under all kinds of labels are not necessarily money-making stores. Every combination is still in the middle of an experiment.

![The giant brand hoardings at Grand Gateway 66, where roughly 100 retail and dining brands are being replaced. Image: @Shangyeka (kaga); photographed on November 6.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210215531IL3W9C5gKa0RmyAk.jpeg-w600)

Grand Gateway 66 in Shanghai, which topped mainland China's shopping centers in rental income for three consecutive years, underwent a massive renovation over the past year covering nearly 30% of its commercial area, with some 100 retail and dining brands replaced. Two notable features: a bigger share of sportswear brands, and dining swapped for internet-famous brands with higher average spending per customer — such as Lady M, LeTAO desserts from Hokkaido (its China first store), and Oriental House, a popular street-side eatery from the former French Concession.

Shimao Festival City's renovation logic is closer to the current "trendy" approach — bigger anchor stores (53% of retail is a collection of various major brands' flagship stores), more dining options (total dining area shrank, but the number of outlets rose from 15 to 33), and a separate 21% of floor area carved out for "experience formats."

"We hope foot traffic and transaction volume after reopening will be double what they were before," said Xiao Tao, general manager of Shimao Commercial Investment Shanghai, at the leasing-results press conference in June.

![The renovated Shanghai Shimao Festival City, whose design concept is the "urban theater"](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210215443UEJzFL0C8ODYBu34.jpeg-w600)

### 2.

In the second half of 2017, Cat's Kitchen decided to open an offline store. The initial plan was to test the waters with a one-to-three-month pop-up, and it first approached Jing'an Joy City, said to be the best in the business at teaming up with IPs. "At the time Joy City was adjusting its own positioning, and its anime-and-comics block was still leasing," Chen said; the location wasn't good enough, so they decided to drop it.

Before long, leasing managers from malls in Shanghai's core districts were knocking on the door, but weighing the options, only Shimao Festival City could offer suitable area and rent terms.

"Some malls even want to talk sales per square meter with you: 'How much monthly revenue do you think you can hit after opening?' At that point I felt that even if I talked to them about my vision for the offline experience, we were unlikely to match. If you're going to measure me by that, I can't tell you for certain how much I'll do in the future — I've never run an offline store; what basis would I have for judging my future sales?"

But the malls' enthusiasm accelerated Cat's Kitchen's plans; the pop-up was skipped, and the physical store opened directly.

Chen now measures the store against two indicators: hard revenue, and the frequency of in-store events and the traffic/effect they generate.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181211000108TPf640J3ZaBM8OYp.jpeg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181211000108QKNuqbBwnRgSv0OT.jpeg-w600)

Not long ago, Cat's Kitchen invited 20 fans to the store for a livestream sponsored by Shangni cookware, "simulcast to Bilibili, Taobao, and the client's Tmall store"; peak viewership on Bilibili was 120,000. "They (Shimao) often ask what events we have and what online promotion we've done."

Those online numbers carry their own halo for the mall partner — a persuasiveness laced with novelty.

Hupu's partnership with Jingyao Qiantan rests on similar logic. Jingyao Qiantan sits next to Shanghai's Oriental Sports Center, and its planners vaguely sensed that they needed a better content creator to cultivate the mall's sports atmosphere.

The two sides began talking in 2016. The preliminary plan was for a 200-square-meter open space on the mall's second floor to be operated by Hupu. The design isn't finished — the site is still a construction zone — but the vision has already been worked out down to details like "spend a certain amount to redeem Hupu coins and buy Hupu merchandise."

In the vision, NFL exhibitions, sports-themed pop-up stores, game-watching parties, and the like can all be crammed into this space; the exact event frequency hasn't been decided. Each side treats the other as a marketing investment/business. Hupu will set up a project team next year, putting in about ten people to marshal the company's sports and star-athlete resources and fill the venue as frequently as possible.

"A real-estate brand tying up with a culture brand like us — they certainly can't pin their hopes on how many fans we bring over; that's unrealistic, right? The point is that I create a sports atmosphere that serves the people who live and work nearby. At the same time we can get some real income," Hupu said, adding that bookstores now seem to be standard equipment for malls — so why not sports spaces too, going forward?

If you think of a mall as a brand, they can no longer afford the cost of producing high-frequency social content, so handing space over to third parties that offer experiences or richer choices has become one approach.

"This is a core shift in reality," Scott Malkin, founder and chairman of Value Retail, said of Asia's shopping malls in an interview with [The New York Times](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/58529.html). "The war in retail is over. Alibaba won. That means physical retail is no longer about distributing goods; it's about building brand equity."

Yet when brand-marketing methodology increasingly tilted toward "internet fame" is grafted onto malls, it becomes a far more complex system: a mall must combine hundreds of different brands — so how does it convey a distinctive temperament? Moreover, a shopping center doesn't truly own the brands; while transferring risk, it also loses control. This poses an enormous challenge for leasing today.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210042105r6k9EpBwi04yuYha.jpg-w600)

Under the new thinking, buyer/curated-store formats are also multiplying — like I.T at Shanghai's LuOne, or the bálancing under Bailian Group. Wutai at Chaoyang Joy City is actually another form of "buyer": it "buys" apparel, but also "buys" dining and offline events.

As Beijing's most typical community commercial complex, Chaoyang Joy City has 49 shopping centers within a 20-minute drive, and more than 200,000 square meters of commercial floor space within a 2-kilometer radius. When it opened, Chaoyang Joy City's original positioning was "family city," with an Aeon supermarket and department store, Jinyi Cinemas, and the like.

In 2011, Chaoyang Joy City cleared out some community-oriented brands and brought in fast fashion like Zara and Gap (that Gap was also a "China first store" at the time); in 2012, word came that the Aeon department store would withdraw, its space replaced by a hundred fashion brands.

Just as the many new malls built in Shanghai's Minhang district divert customers from the Xujiahui business district, community commercial complexes, under a well-developed subway network, likewise have to compete with the shopping centers of core business districts.

In October, Zhongsheng World Mall in Xinzhuang, Minhang, opened an offline store for the lifestyle self-media brand Yitiao — the first step in founder Xu Husheng's "all-in on offline stores." For the three stores open so far, inventory and shipping pressure is still borne by suppliers; Yitiao itself owns no merchandise.

![Yitiao at LuOne, CapitaLand's mall in Shanghai](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812102159431u47mapr9LeG2WIg.jpg-w600)

Explaining the benefits of opening stores from a marketing angle in an interview with [Newrank](https://www.huxiu.com/article/264122.html), Xu said: "Put a few hundred million yuan in, get 100 stores, and you can run them for six or ten years. A few hundred million yuan burned on ads is gone in half a year, and the results are hard to say." The remark is certainly incisive about the inefficiency of using ads to drive traffic, but it also exposes other problems. Given that each Yitiao store is at least around 700 square meters, "a few hundred million yuan for 100 stores" means fixed investment per store of only around a million yuan or so — not counting follow-up investment, and it has to be sustained for years. That can hardly be called a rigorous claim.

Whether malls or brands, the companies we interviewed treat indicators like profit and sales per square meter as trade secrets when talking about experience formats — or consider the numbers not worth discussing. The point is "people come, and it spreads."

Unlike traditional commercial-retail measurement, the way new brands do the math is to compare the cost of a physical store against their own online costs.

"(For new media,) advertisers have a magnitude threshold in mind for follower counts — tell them you can deliver 10 million impressions and they're already quite satisfied. If you say you can do 20 or 30 million, they'd rather put the budget into other vertical media," Chen said. Cat's Kitchen's online advertising revenue growth hit a ceiling starting last year. A cross-channel video ad priced at 800,000–1.2 million yuan is hard to push any higher. Turning to open offline stores both helps raise Cat's Kitchen's brand premium and influence and brings new retail revenue — if business is good.

In fact, retail is a harder business for most people.

"Still feeling our way; right now we can only give ourselves a passing grade." Liang Chao is the founder of YOHO!, a Nanjing company that started as a street-trend media outlet, began running an e-commerce business in 2008, and formally entered physical retail in 2018. On October 13, YOHO! opened a streetwear curated store called YOHO!BLU at Shimao Festival City on Shanghai's Nanjing East Road — just 400 square meters, the company's second new store format besides the YOHO!STORE flagship it opened in Nanjing last year.

Liang said this YOHO!BLU at Shimao is mainly testing an omnichannel brand-incubation model; the company is still in talks with all the mainstream domestic real-estate developers, and the physical-store plan keeps being adjusted.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812102202473SioRBOgmzUaGIcs.jpeg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210220247l4dxTihqUzFarpnC.jpeg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210220247RQUvgMLVXKJS1qBx.jpeg-w600)

In October, YOHO! announced it had raised US$25 million in an E+ round, led by C Ventures, the fund founded by Adrian Cheng — founder of K11, under Hong Kong's New World group. The real-estate-backed fund also invested last year in food short-video platform DayDayCook. Unlike Cat's Kitchen, DayDayCook's offline store takes the form of a cooking-teaching kitchen with 99-yuan classes, which opened at Shanghai's K11 in early 2018 — and the "first batch of anchor tenants" list previewed by Jingyao Qiantan also includes a "DayDayCook Food & Lifestyle Experience Center."

A [report](https://www.google.com.hk/search?safe=active&rlz=1C5CHFA_enHK802HK802&ei=InIOXP65OYOB8gW044XYAQ&q=%E4%B8%96%E9%82%A6%E9%AD%8F%E7%90%86%E4%BB%95+285+%E8%B5%B7%E9%9B%B6%E5%94%AE%E5%A8%B1%E4%B9%90%E9%A2%86%E5%9F%9F%E7%9A%84%E9%A3%8E%E6%8A%95%E6%A1%88%E4%BE%8B%E4%B8%AD%EF%BC%8C%E6%9C%89+86+%E7%AC%94%E8%B5%84%E9%87%91%E5%B0%86%E5%85%A8%E9%83%A8%E6%88%96%E9%83%A8%E5%88%86%E7%94%A8%E4%BA%8E%E9%93%BA%E8%AE%BE%E7%BA%BF%E4%B8%8B%E9%97%A8%E5%BA%97&oq=%E4%B8%96%E9%82%A6%E9%AD%8F%E7%90%86%E4%BB%95+285+%E8%B5%B7%E9%9B%B6%E5%94%AE%E5%A8%B1%E4%B9%90%E9%A2%86%E5%9F%9F%E7%9A%84%E9%A3%8E%E6%8A%95%E6%A1%88%E4%BE%8B%E4%B8%AD%EF%BC%8C%E6%9C%89+86+%E7%AC%94%E8%B5%84%E9%87%91%E5%B0%86%E5%85%A8%E9%83%A8%E6%88%96%E9%83%A8%E5%88%86%E7%94%A8%E4%BA%8E%E9%93%BA%E8%AE%BE%E7%BA%BF%E4%B8%8B%E9%97%A8%E5%BA%97&gs_l=psy-ab.3...7626.8756..8875...0.0..1.184.1927.3j13......0....1..gws-wiz.lafDt9neDJE) by CBRE shows that among the 285 venture-capital deals in the retail-and-entertainment space in 2017, 86 involved funds to be used wholly or partly for building out offline stores; dining, sports facilities, lifestyle, and other formats that ride the consumption-upgrade trend and depend heavily on offline experience were the most favored by capital.

And for today's mall leasing managers, even Xiaohongshu can become the next hunting ground for brands.

When "whoever's hot gets in" overrides the logic of business itself, unknown risks follow. RET's Zhou Changqing said that in watching first-store statistics, he found that the pace at which "first stores" sink from first-tier cities to second-tier ones has accelerated from one to two years down to three to six months — and the elimination cycle is speeding up too.

### 3.

On October 28, 2018, "Yousheng Pinjian's" first global offline store — 1,000 square meters on the fourth floor of Shanghai's Qibao Vanke Plaza — closed down, only half a year after opening in April.

Like most new-retail formats, "Yousheng Pinjian" had a complicated set of financial backers, and the headlines at its opening were lively: "Is Xiaomi's strength offline? Behold its new species, 'Yousheng Pinjian'!"

"Yousheng Pinjian" is generally taken to be a Xiaomi new-retail format, but in fact it is a different matter from Xiaomi Youpin under Xiaomi. Yousheng Pinjian is a retail company jointly invested in by Korrun — a Xiaomi ecological-chain company and travel-luggage supplier — and Hongzhang Capital.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210220448CGJ6nR2eFWPMAcVr.jpeg-w600)

Reportedly the first "Yousheng Pinjian" store cost more than 10 million yuan, and soon after opening, [reports](https://www.jiemian.com/article/2116399.html) criticized its insufficient product variety — too little to fill such a large space, and unable to stir any desire to shop.

Meanwhile, more commercial real-estate projects are about to hit the market. Shanghai's Hongqiao CBD, planned as a municipal-level commercial center covering 86 square kilometers, has announced more than 35 commercial projects — all said to open within the next eight years; so far, two have opened. One of them, Shui On's The Hub, opened at the end of 2015, over a year behind schedule, according to [media reports](http://news.winshang.com/html/048/1882.html).

In May 2018, the Shanghai Municipal Commission of Commerce drew up a three-year plan, "Building the 'Shanghai Shopping' Brand," which noted that Nanjing West Road — Shanghai's most representative international business district — has per-capita spending of less than one-fifth of New York's Fifth Avenue and an international-tourist share of less than one-seventh of Tokyo's Ginza. Policy hopes to support progress toward that goal, as reflected in the various policy relaxations and special-case approvals granted to the Starbucks Reserve Roastery. Another data point: at the end of 2017, Shanghai's resident population was 24.1833 million, down 13,700 from the end of the previous year.

Against this backdrop, if shopping centers want to become "cities where everyone lives and consumes," differentiated competition has entered a somewhat feverish stage — and it is also the time for doing business in the most solid, down-to-earth way.

Such a precedent has already played out once in the American department-store industry. Sears, which declared bankruptcy in 2018, became a place where the American middle class lived and shopped during the department stores' glorious 1990s, adding giant cinemas and amusement facilities; the huge Snoopy and Mickey Mouse figures in mall atriums — doesn't that look familiar?

![A 1990s American mall, and the recent Pokémon Park at Nanfeng City in Hongqiao, Shanghai](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210214958XaoV0N8MmcZkDpt6.JPG-w600)

Real-estate research group CoStar's latest data says that since the start of 2018, American department stores and shopping centers have closed more than [146 million square feet](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/12/07/us-mall-owners-have-been-slow-to-change-for-years-but-now-embrace-it.html) of retail space. The complexity of China's physical retail is that, on top of coping with a massive amount of floor space, there is also the shock brought by the internet. This is why more and more internet-company offline experience stores appeared in 2018.

"I feel this step-by-step progress is a kind of swept-along advance," Li Deming said. "It reached that point in time just as e-commerce development also hit a bottleneck."

Whether this trend can ultimately solve both sides' problems at once remains unknown.
