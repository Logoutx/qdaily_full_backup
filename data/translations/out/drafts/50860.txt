We talked with writer Li Ge about her new book, the reading stories she shares with her daughter, and more

Book critic Li Ge has published her first non-criticism book, 《小鸟睡我家身旁》(The Bird Sleeping Beside Me). On the title page, she notes that it is dedicated to the "two women" she loves most—her mother and her daughter. They are, she writes, "where her blood comes from and where it is going," and the people who gave her "the courage to embrace the frightened moments of life."

What the book records are mostly scattered details of daily life. A "date day" at the botanical garden with her daughter on a fine autumn weekend, packed with kimchi sushi and sliced fruit. On a restless, sleepless night, her daughter stretching out her tiny arms to hold her, saying "there's nothing to be afraid of." The first train trip with her daughter to see the sea. The little notes her mother stuck up around the house. The ginkgo fruits her husband picked up with her daughter on the way home from school.

The tone of the record is light. Setbacks and moving moments in life are all breezed past lightly within short passages.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180306221705CXNMwpG5QcgOy24K.jpg-w600)

This gentle style is a far cry from the "sharp" style of Li Ge's early years. The birth of her daughter, in her view, brought her more or less some "subtle influence": "You become more able to appreciate some of the beauty of life, to be a bit more spiritualized."

Her daughter Pipi is the most important participant in this book. The vast majority of its content is connected to her. From pregnancy onward, Li Ge began collecting her funny little stories. And the illustrations and ceramic works printed on the pages are all Pipi's own. After the book came out, Li Ge gave her a share of the royalties, as a mark of respect for the fruits of her labor.

The book began simply as a personal record of Li Ge's. Before the publisher invited her to submit it, she had no intention of publishing it; she wanted to print everything into a little booklet and give it to Pipi as a gift when she grew up, so she could see how cute she was as a child.

After deciding to publish, in order to "choose a degree of balance," Li Ge selected part of the content. "From a mother's point of view, every moment of a child is precious, but to readers it might feel like petty, trivial clutter, and fail to hold their interest. You just look for a balance point in between—something the other side might respond to, not too fragmented. The overly fragmented bits you keep for yourself," Li Ge said in the interview.

On the night the sample copies arrived, Li Ge's mother and Pipi both wanted a first look. "My daughter very cleverly lulled my mother to sleep, then hid off in a corner reading it by herself, and I could hear her giggling away. It contains some details that she's embarrassed about but that are really funny—she must have forgotten them herself. Like how she would hide under the bed to pee and things like that," Li Ge said.

What is relatively rare among books that chronicle parent-child relationships is that Li Ge shares quite a few details about reading together with her daughter. At first, they would read different books by the same author; later, their reading lists began to overlap. Recently, because she needed to add book reviews of Russian literature, Li Ge started rereading Dostoevsky's works, and along the way her daughter tagged along reading Aitmatov. Because her daughter loves plants and animals so much, they also read Michio Hoshino's books together.

Still, their evaluations of the same author and the same book often differ. Li Ge's principle for handling this is "each plays her own game," letting each "follow her own orbit."

This policy of non-intervention she has earnestly carried into every aspect of life, though at times it challenges her own aesthetics. "I think the greatest disaster in life is someone who interferes with you in the name of love. You can put yourself in their shoes—you certainly don't want to impose that on others. A life that isn't respected might as well not be lived at all; that's how I see it," Li Ge explained in the interview.

She devoted a fair amount of space to reflecting on the role of mother: "Being a mother is just one of my identities; I don't need to locate myself through a sense of authority over my child. I have my world, and she has hers. So I never use my child's photo as my avatar, nor call myself 'so-and-so's mom'—this also lightens the load for the child, since no one wants to live as another person's projection. So the more you love your child, the more you must strengthen your own self-construction, fortify your own spiritual world, and pull yourself away from the child, lest you inadvertently crush the other person and run roughshod over another's life—this boundary between self and others is something Chinese families commonly lack."

The following is part of QDaily's exclusive interview with Li Ge:

**Q: QDaily**

**L: Li Ge**

**Q: I noticed that, judging by publication dates, many of your books came out after your child was born, giving the impression that you became more productive after having a child.**

L: I started very late. I was about 27 when I began writing, and 29 when I had my child; the books came along afterward, so it's a coincidence. For example, you write for a year or two, someone asks you to do a book, and it just happens to overlap with that time. Four books this year—two are reissues, and two are expanded editions. Like 《私语书》(Private Words), which has sold for eight years and has now come out in three editions—that counts as publishing too.

**Q: Looking back now at the reissued books, what do you think of their contents?**

L: It's the view of a middle-aged person looking at a young person. I feel I really had literary guts back then—a kind of talent that comes with youthful energy, very daring in speech, willing to express even views that weren't fully formed, with a blade-like gleam. I don't have that anymore. But now I might sink a bit deeper, be more considerate and compassionate—it's the middle-aged person's view of the young.

**Q: Do you think the disappearance of that blade-like quality is an inevitable trend with age, or a change particular to you?**

L: I think as you get older, you see all living beings, see other people, and gradually develop a mindset of empathy—there's no way to stand on an absolute standard and criticize others. Maybe that's part of it. More than anything, it's a kind of understanding toward one's own kind. Still, I quite like young people now; I think it's good that they dare to speak.

**Q: Your reading is quite eclectic—for instance, you read books on architecture.**

L: All artistic disciplines are interconnected. Take architecture: among the seven arts, it's one with a very high degree of socialization. For an architect to persuade a client to take on his project, he needs great power of persuasion and expression. You'll find that many architects, like film directors, are a perfect combination of personal talent and social skill.

It's just that his ideas are expressed through spatial form. You'll find that many architects write beautifully—like Kengo Kuma, or the Louis Kahn type; their command of language is simply too good. And they're not like literary writers, whose work is descriptive, lyrical, emotional stuff with a bit of speculation added at most. Architects have the cool, precise, dry expression of an engineering student, and I find that fascinating.

And it's a reference for literature—many architects' ideas can be applied directly to literature. For example, as Kengo Kuma says, a building is not an isolated building; he opposed that modernist school of Le Corbusier's, saying "I don't like isolated buildings." He likes the connection between people and nature; he believes architecture is a kind of connection. Isn't that exactly literature? Literature, too, should not be an isolated spiritual work; it should be a connection between people and life. It's the same—artistic disciplines are all interconnected.

**Q: Is there a difference between the books you chose to read early on and the books you choose now?**

L: Yes, and it's probably a universal phenomenon. I think people like reading novels when they're young. In my twenties I read English, American, and Russian novels with something close to madness. Later I read fewer and fewer novels, and now I read fairly widely. When you're young you're very curious about the external world and about other people's psychology, so you want to pry into others' lives and states of mind. As you get older you become very interested in the history and knowledge of this world and want to learn, and things change.

**Q: Is your daughter now like you in your younger days, preferring storybooks and such?**

L: Every child really has her own reading genealogy. So some people ask me to make reading lists for their children, and I say I honestly can't—it has to be formed slowly through long-term living and experimenting with the child, a tailor-made list for your own child. My daughter, for instance, likes small animals, likes natural science—she really likes astronomy, geography, plants, and animals.

**Q: Would you count that as an innate preference of hers, rather than something formed through a lot of postnatal exposure to natural science content?**

L: Our family has a relatively well-developed reading environment, so her range of choices may be a bit wider, but you clearly notice that she'll pick a certain category of things to read, and then you know she truly likes this.

**Q: Because she likes natural science books, do you deliberately guide her to read books outside the natural science category, to broaden her horizons?**

L: Balance, you mean? I give her room to choose, but I don't particularly restrict her. She's very curious and interested in everything. And it's not just her—I'd say all children are like this. It's hard to find a listless child who's interested in nothing.

**Q: You two read different books by the same author, and now your reading lists have begun to overlap—what have you read together?**

L: Recently we've been rereading a lot of books. Lately we've been reading Michio Hoshino—he wrote a lot about polar animals, my daughter loves him very much, and his prose is fairly simple and easy to understand.

Recently I may need to add a Russian book, so I'll probably reread Dostoevsky and Aitmatov, and she follows along reading too. For example, I recently bought her Duku's classics-for-children series, which includes 《诚实的贼》(The Honest Thief) by Dostoevsky. I hadn't paid attention to this short work before, but it's actually quite well written. She's reading it, and I'm following along.

There's also Paustovsky's 《一篮云杉果》(A Basket of Spruce Cones). I had read 《金蔷薇》(The Golden Rose) and 《一生的故事》(The Story of a Life) before, but didn't know this one existed. So my daughter is reading it, and I'm reading along.

**Q: Do you have any discussions after reading?**

L: It's a natural spiritual secretion—you don't deliberately exchange anything, but you definitely want to share casually with people around you; sharing is a human instinct. She says her thoughts, and I find them fun and interesting. She's naturally unpretentious, of course, and can't produce very elaborate ideas.

Lately she's been reading a book by Shen Fuyu called 《诸神的踪迹》(Traces of the Gods). I haven't read that one yet.

**Q: How does she pick the books you haven't read?**

L: Whatever she's interested in, I'll buy a whole batch for her—she never lacks books. And given the nature of my work, friends also send me books.

A book we both love very much is 《动物园的生死告白》, written by an animal keeper. We both love it—adults and children alike love it; I recommend it highly.

**Q: Are your reasons for liking it the same?**

L: She likes watching the lives of animals; I like the life experience and philosophy the keeper perceives through the whole process of being with the animals. My understanding may be more abstract.

**Q: When you express your understanding to her, what's her reaction?**

L: I genuinely think a person's capacity for understanding has little to do with age. Her father is already 50, but his understanding of many things falls far short of the child's—very dull and numb. This child is extremely sensitive and can understand a great many things.

**Q: That may be a person's nature.**

L: Right. But precisely because of that, both of us live more painfully than he does. We're both very sensitive and tender-hearted, with big reactions to everything. In most things, anyway, his resolution is lower than ours.

**Q: Are there any thoughts she has that you didn't come up with while reading?**

L: I think there must be. For example, some books she introduced to me—like 《重返狼群》(Return to the Wolf Pack), which I really hadn't noticed. When I first got the book, it was made very unattractively—a big book with loose typesetting; it simply wasn't within my aesthetic scope, so I had no intention of reading it. But after my daughter read it, she kept telling me about it—the story of the little wolf Greenie who was taken in by humans. She described it to me in detail: how that little wolf had been bullied since childhood by the family's very mean dog, and how the dog even tricked it into eating pepper powder, making the little wolf sneeze nonstop. I found it very interesting.

**Q: Do you ever evaluate the same author very differently?**

L: Oh yes! That happens all the time. The ones I like, she doesn't; the ones she likes, I don't. It happens constantly. Things I simply can't get through, she reads with relish. I can't recall specifics offhand. Take novels: I like *A Dream of Red Mansions*, but she likes *Water Margin* and *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*—she likes that kind of world of chivalry with clear black-and-white morality. I prefer the sentimental, tender, love-laden stuff.

**Q: When you disagree, what do you do?**

L: Each plays her own game—she plays hers, I play mine. Follow our own orbits.

**Q: I think sometimes parents want to persuade their children, to get them to believe in the things they're sure of.**

L: This kind of high-pressure output is something I don't approve of. A child is an independent being with her own values; she doesn't need that kind of thing. Kids spend most of their time at school, and among themselves they've been reading some castle series or whatever—vulgar through and through, scary stuff that gives kids nightmares. Even that I didn't interfere with, but later she dropped it herself, finding it too ugly, too tacky. She prefers the more refined books we keep at home. She has the ability to weed things out herself. I remember reading Chiung Yao's romance novels when I was young, but I couldn't possibly read them now. There's a natural weeding-out, a growth—no need to force things excessively.

**Q: In the book you also mention that you worry she'll suffer setbacks because of her independence.**

L: It's certainly a process like that.

**Q: Will you help her prepare anything, to spare her some setbacks?**

L: I think this kind of thing is outside the scope of what parents can control. Take Michio Hoshino: he loved Siberia and polar animals from childhood, and in his teens he worked a job on his own and traveled around the United States for three months without calling his parents. When he grew up he went to Alaska to photograph bears and such, and in the end he was killed by a bear. As parents, they certainly wouldn't have wanted their child to die in that kind of accident, but is that something parents can control? The child bears the consequences of her own choices, and I think that's enough—it's the manifestation of a free will unmanipulated by parents. As for those unpredictable factors, they can't be within us parents' considerations; as long as it's what she chose and is willing to bear, that's fine.

Since the new book came out, I've received many letters... One young woman wrote to me in the middle of the night, saying her parents love her very much, but she was doing everything she could to escape them. She said: "I saw in a video that my mom's hair is getting thinner and thinner. I know she worries about me, but I really can't get too close to her. After reading your book, everything suddenly became clear to me." (That's what) a sense of boundaries is. Many people don't realize this problem; in fact they inflict a lot of aggression on the other person. And the other side always feels, "I love you."
