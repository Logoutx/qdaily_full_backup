We've distilled all the essential designs and ideas from 2017 for you.

In early April every year, the sun doesn't rise over Milan until seven in the morning, and doesn't set until after eight in the evening.

At six in the afternoon, the Salone del Mobile—the Milan International Furniture Fair at the Expo exhibition center on the city's outskirts—closes, and visitors laden with all kinds of tote bags stuffed with brochures of new furniture products board the metro in batches, heading for the Milan Cathedral, the Brera district, or the youthful, buzzing Lambrate district. On a few special days, most of the exhibitions here stay open until ten at night. Well-dressed people hurry off to club parties hidden in the side streets, while lightly dressed young people drift from one exhibition hall, gallery, and bar to the next, beer or ice cream in hand.

That, more or less, is the scene at Milan Design Week.

In 2017, the collaboration between Tom Dixon and IKEA that had been hyped to the heavens on social media, the latest home collections from Louis Vuitton and Hermès, the magical installation Studio Swine designed for COS, the partnership between mischievous Spanish designer Jaime Hayon and stone company Caesarstone, Cassina's 90th-anniversary celebrations... In those six short days, at least 350,000 people around the world were kept running off their feet, wishing they could split themselves into five copies to cover the more than 2,000 exhibitions, big and small, across the entire city of Milan.

![Image: [architectours](http://www.architectours.it/milan-design-week-2017-suggestions/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413143807OFCuH9lTYoGBgykm.jpg-w600)

Stefan Holwe, founder of Berlin travel-goods design company Horizn Studios, calls Milan Design Week the "Super Bowl" of the design world. But for more people, it is the design world's "fashion week," with the spring/summer and fall/winter seasons bundled together and broadcast all at once—all the latest aesthetic trends and material currents, the most famous designers and the best-resourced brands gathered in one place. If you're a designer, Milan Design Week matters as much as Paris Fashion Week does to a fashion editor.

These days, Milanese know their city hosts a Design Week every year. But most people haven't accepted the new name for long; for a much longer stretch, they were used to calling it Salone del Mobile—the Milan International Furniture Fair, a trade show with a 56-year history created to display and promote the Italian furniture business, which gradually evolved into the world's largest home-furnishings trade exhibition.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413135617R5OI6NbAYzsSfDuc.JPG-w600)

Today, Milan Design Week in the true sense has two parts: inside the city and outside. The inside part is called Fuorisalone, comprising the various independent exhibitions and events; the outside part—in Milan's Rho district—is the Salone del Mobile, birthplace of the Milan International Furniture Fair, where the entire exhibition is now held in the Milan Expo pavilions.

Though it grew out of the furniture fair, Milan Design Week evolves and expands every year, and its structure is becoming complex.

This naturally has to do with the growing involvement of big companies. Giants of electronics manufacturing—Samsung, Apple, Panasonic—or carmakers like BMW and Audi, internet companies like Airbnb, even fashion brands like Nike and COS have all set up exhibitions here, showing off their companies' new design products or design concepts.

Sportswear brand Nike, for example, invited 10 designers to put on an installation show about "movement" at Milan Design Week 2016. Food company PepsiCo used Design Week to launch its latest bottle design. This year, Airbnb invited designers to give visitors guided tours inside a 15th-century old house, and carmaker Audi displayed two sound-collection installations in the courtyard of a seminary to demonstrate the company's self-developed artificial intelligence technology.

Some people think this is a good thing. [Marcus Fairs, editor-in-chief of Dezeen magazine, wrote in praise of the phenomenon last year](https://www.dezeen.com/2016/04/14/milan-design-week-2016-megabrands-nike-audi-marcus-fairs-opinion/): "These shows are great fun—in fact, they're the best part of the city center. In the old days these brands weren't welcome—they're making Milan so special with a completely new kind of marketing and experimental exhibitions."

But others think this complicated situation has left the furniture fair looking identity-less. "Hunting down those exhibitions in the city is exhausting; to reach a small show in a peripheral area you have to fight your way through crowded streets," said [design critic Alice Rowsthorn in an article written for Frieze magazine](https://frieze.com/article/design-4). "And most of it is shameless propaganda that doesn't have much to do with furniture at all. That's why British designer Jasper Morrison once suggested renaming the 'Milan Furniture Fair (Salone del Mobile)' outright as the 'Milan Marketing Fair (Salone del Marketing)'."

Jasper Morrison may have hit the nail on the head, but people don't seem to mind Milan Design Week becoming something that sits between a commercial exhibition and an innovation platform. Because this way, everyone can find a place that suits them in the bustle.

Tom Dixon and IKEA seem to read the changes happening to Design Week in exactly this way. This year, Multiplex—curated by the designers themselves—and the IKEA Festival that IKEA held in the Lambrate district both embodied this quality of binding creativity and selling directly together.

With the exhibition set in a 1950s cinema, Tom Dixon's show this year was basically divided into an installation area upstairs and a pop-up shop area downstairs. The installation area, made up of one screening room and several other public spaces, mainly presented the full design concept of the modular sofa Tom Dixon created in collaboration with IKEA, as well as Dixon's exploration—together with 75 students from Parsons in New York, the Royal College of Art in the UK, and design schools in Japan—of the possibilities of future modular furniture.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2017041314012302hTXo6ZH3xNEDCs.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413140408X0ifDQjptyM18YeN.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413140423ehTy2KQWBaCvowp4.JPG-w600)

On the ground floor, a broad corridor was flanked by temporary shops including Sonos, IKEA, the IOTA Project (a project aimed at creating jobs for the world's unemployed, focused on producing beautifully woven goods), and Tom Dixon lighting. Unlike the purely presentational function of an ordinary exhibition, these were real shops—people could buy a Tom Dixon lamp, a handmade wool blanket, the latest IKEA sleeping bag, or a Sonos Bluetooth speaker right there on the spot.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413140704fQTp0mEiXMu1whxL.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413140717tIzYjSZV4Wp7yBOR.JPG-w600)

From the Multiplex cinema entrance you could conveniently hop on a shuttle bus straight to the Lambrate district, also home to the much-watched IKEA Festival. IKEA had rented an old factory building of more than 3,500 square meters this year and set its new-product exhibition inside it.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704131417447dzUTOjI9lc1Zh5q.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413142125QoB2lhNKMScJIL6j.JPG-w600)

Rather than an exhibition, it was more like a comprehensive market. Just inside the entrance, American designer Kevin Lyons—whom QDaily previously covered as a participant in the poster design for IKEA's art festival—was covering a huge white wall with the little monsters he creates.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413141800nQtkvGDswd1iHhfg.JPG-w600)

Farther in, London artist Faye Toogood made an exhibition about "play" called *Enfant Terrible*, and visitors could also grab an IKEA anti-slip mat and take a slide ride in this area. Beyond that, there was a living room drifting with the sound of cello playing, a study like a green-plant workshop, a drawing robot that creates through pre-written programs, and more.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413141815y2XgWZPfN9sMSw7b.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413141833yNT1ZmVksiUBAXWg.JPG-w600)

![Images above: British artist Faye Toogood made an exhibition about "play," *Enfant Terrible.*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413141846gchyq5Q1WjFDmMYo.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413141951eu9GIm1K5oCrwxgv.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413142055z2QBWNJDlrYTObw3.JPG-w600)

![A drawing robot developed by students at ECAL, the University of Art and Design Lausanne.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413142112KVgH0dDmkzeZqMwl.JPG-w600)

IKEA hid its own furniture among all these themed activities and display areas. And whether posters, colorful rugs, or modular sofas, aside from a portion of new products not yet officially on sale (such as the VIMLE series IKEA designed with Hay, which goes on sale after October this year), most were items you could buy immediately.

Technology and the internet are, of course, changing Design Week too; more smart furniture and lighting appeared at the furniture fair and the concept shows. At this year's furniture fair, though, "invisible technology" and "using technology to create a sense of luxury" became the keywords. Designers began asking: "How do you hide technology behind craft? How do you integrate technology and traditional materials more organically?"

The project "Electronics Meets Crafts," which won this year's Milan Design Award for best storytelling, was an attempt to explore exactly that question.

Japanese electronics brand Panasonic partnered with GO ON, an alliance of traditional craft workshops in Kyoto, on a project named "Electronics Meets Crafts."

GO ON's members include Masataka Hosoo of Hosoo, the Nishijin weaving house; Hosai Matsubayashi of Asahi-yaki, a tea-ceramics workshop; Tatsuyuki Kosuge of Kohchosai Kosuga, a bamboo-craft workshop; Takahiro Yagi of Kaikado, a tea-caddy maker; and numerous other artisans. The group began its activities in 2012.

The Panasonic–GO ON project aims to develop home appliances that can influence the human five senses and memory, and released its first official product line in September 2016.

The works shown at this Milan exhibition include a speaker shaped like a tea caddy, made in collaboration with Kaikado, with music controlled by opening and closing the lid; a "moon lamp" created with Kohchosai Kosuga using traditional Japanese bamboo weaving, producing light-and-shadow effects rich in natural rhythm; a sake warmer made with Nakagawa woodcraft that can be used on an induction cooktop; and a wooden bucket for chilling sake.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2017041314295237U0TWtuZphlVARj.jpg-w600)

![Image: [fashion snap](http://www.fashionsnap.com/news/2017-04-10/goon-panasonic-milan/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413143411y3ofWI9vhitrXLBY.jpg-w600)

The jury's evaluation read: "Turning the exhibition theme into a flowing history, in which technology is hidden in the story behind the craft tradition."

Similarly, Bang & Olufsen launched its new wall-speaker series BeoSound at Milan Design Week, for which the brand held an exhibition called "A New Way of Living with Sound." Here you could hardly find any trace of high-tech elements—only geometric colored tiles decorating the walls.

![Image: [dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2017/04/03/bang-olufsen-beosound-shape-tile-modular-speaker-system-milan-design-week-2017/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413143953boBmaHzKr4OwnXyk.jpg-w600)

"We want indoor music to exist like sunlight and air—both ergonomic and natural," said Marie Kristine Schmidt, B&O's vice president of design and marketing. "Modern luxury is not about mass production. I think we're moving toward a trend where we don't need too many things—we need the things that truly make us happy."

"I believe that in the future, the real challenge for industrial designers will be to constantly find a balance between the functionality offered by new technology and the emotions a product produces in people," said Claudio Luti, the head of the Milan Furniture Fair, in the trend report released alongside the fair each year.

Tony Chambers, editor-in-chief of Wallpaper, once told QDaily at Singapore Design Week about the distinctive theme of this year's Handmade project at Milan Design Week. Chambers perceptively pointed out that in today's volatile, conflict-ridden social environment, people might prefer objects full of spiritual power.

![This year's Holy! Handmade exhibition by Wallpaper is set inside a deconsecrated church.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413153014jKQLYbuZU9IE1eJ5.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413152959GnbpKE7lAaVcCN4q.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413153004v1aVNPCSTFpEb72o.JPG-w600)

His words happened to speak for a group of designers at this year's Milan Design Week. Among them: New Spring, the interactive sculpture Studio Swine designed for fashion brand COS, which drew long queues at its door every day, set inside a theater covered entirely in black. At the center of the venue, a tree-shaped sculpture that sprays white smoke balls glimmers. The designers said they hoped to respond to the current "change and crisis" with this calming, relaxing installation.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704131442045AS9xPnzUYW0JCpa.JPG-w600)

"We own too many things—sometimes paring down is itself a luxury," Alexander Groves, British artist and co-founder of Studio Swine, explained to visitors at the exhibition's opening. "We're very happy to design something that doesn't need to be sold—designers can simply design an experience, rather than more goods."

"This year's Milan Design Week is different," a staff member explained to QDaily at the pop-up store Moroso created this year in collaboration with publisher Phaidon. "Last year the atmosphere was completely different. To celebrate our collaboration with designer Ron Arad, we painted this place pitch black to display his work. But this year, as you can see, we invited 16-year-old artist Achilleas Souras to work with us. Together with Moroso, he built this shelter tent made from life jackets recovered from Greek beaches. We believe Europe is changing; a lot happened last year, and we should be paying attention to that."

![The smaller piece is a previous work by artist Achilleas Souras. The larger one was made specially for the Milan Design Week exhibition. Souras insisted on completing all the design and fabrication himself.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413144324szTva4JVyQ5g3e0E.JPG-w600)

"This year's Milan Design Week theme is not a dramatic or entirely different interpretation from 2016, conceptually," CNN commentator Fiona Sinclair Scott wrote in [an article on this year's Milan Design Week trends](http://edition.cnn.com/2017/04/07/luxury/milan-design-week-2017-luxury-redefined-in-8-objects/). "The design industry is much like art and fashion—very sensitive to changes in the world—so recycled materials and the shift away from material waste will remain major topics in design from here on."

At the Triennale Design Museum, Belgium's ECO-Oh! company presented an exhibition called "Let's Recycle Life," introducing the results the company has achieved in its R&D of plastics recycling technology.

"We sort the plastics—plastic wrap is one category, and containers, bottle caps, plastic electronics and so on are another. Then we wash and grind them down," an Eco-Oh! staff member told QDaily. "We use our special technology to turn these different kinds of plastic into hard, wear-resistant boards. Throughout the entire process, all the plastic is 100% recycled."

Three colleagues from Danish textile brand Kvadrat founded a textile company called Really together, and jointly developed a stiff fabric made from recycled cotton and wool that can be used to make furniture, book covers, and more. This year, Really also staged an exhibition at Milan Design Week called "Really: Designing for Recycling," attempting to explore how to solve the ever-growing problem of reusing discarded clothing, bedsheets, and the like.

![A bench made from material developed by Really. Image: [fuorisalone](https://fuorisalone.it/2017/en/events/141/really-designing-material-for-circularity)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413144824VN0Cx1LXISq47iJU.png-w600)

"We believe recycling is bound to become a trend of the future," the Stockholm-based company Bemz told QDaily. An independent online retailer that makes sofa covers for all IKEA sofas, it was invited by Tom Dixon to exhibit at Multiplex this year: "Through our designs, we hope people won't throw things away so easily. Sometimes a tiny change can make you fall in love with something all over again!"

Design Week is simply too big; there were too many exhibitions we failed to see, too many events and forums we couldn't attend within those six days. Below, we've put together several of this year's important furniture exhibitions—a report on some of the shows we flagged in QDaily's Design Week preview:

### Louis Vuitton: Objects Nomades

If you wanted to enter the Louis Vuitton showroom after press day, you more or less had to queue for a little while.

The exhibition's popularity owes a great deal to its roster of designers, all familiar names—Brazil's Campana brothers, Tokujin Yoshioka, Marcel Wanders, Nendo, Atelier Oi, and others. They added 10 new pieces to the fifth edition of LV's furniture collection on the theme of "travel."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413145210StRypKAr0PXljfIc.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413145213uAnXClWIiKvRoHB1.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413145214XQWAdIB9p8hwE31t.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413145214Z5aPV2zNt9EBJ8GK.JPG-w600)

Seeing this exhibition is a visual pleasure in itself—especially when you step into a mirror room filled with chandeliers assembled from Atelier Oi's designs, and momentarily feel as if you've walked into a dazzling palace.

Also worth mentioning is a petal chair designed by Tokujin Yoshioka. To echo the "travel" theme, most of the furniture here can be disassembled and packed away in an ingenious manner, and this cute, compact petal chair is no exception. It is made of just a few bent, irregularly shaped boards fitted together; the principle is surprisingly simple, yet it forms a fluid kind of beauty.

### Hermès

Hermès, which loves equestrian culture, applied the elements of this aristocratic sport to full effect in this year's exhibition. You would never imagine that leather, metal buckles, and rope knots could have so many variations and uses.

The brand's all-new furniture products launched on its design table this time include tableware, tables, stools, small dining carts, coat hooks, silk scarves, and more, divided into seven series using materials such as wood, lacquer, wicker, crystal, and metal.

At the start of the exhibition, the docent told the audience: "Usually, leather goods are inseparably linked to the horse. Hermès liberates leather from the constraints that hinder the animal's free movement, letting leather goods bring a more authentic beauty to living spaces. Our exhibition carries two meanings—beauty, visible to the eye; and functionality, always easy to carry and use."

Though it has always emphasized its craftsmanship and materials, and designs and manufactures its furniture products itself, the Hermès studio also collaborates with famous designers and architects. This year, Pritzker Architecture Prize–winning architect Álvaro Siza designed a small triangular stool, product design studio Barber & Osgerby designed a black coffee table, and French designer Pierre Charpin designed a dinner-plate series.

### Jaime Hayon X Caesarstone

In his first collaboration with Caesarstone, Jaime Hayon of Spain kept up his trademark mischievous-boy persona. This time he used all kinds of stone to design a playground—clown-shaped cabinets, bird-shaped tables, plus rotating round seats and a colorful palace—and named the project "Stone Age Folks."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704131500203mFahyYwL8xWGiZ9.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704131500351b9rOoulLzIqPnNh.JPG-w600)

As for the colorful palace, Hayon said its inspiration came from the Crystal Palace, the exhibition hall of the 1851 Great Exhibition in London's Hyde Park. The designer mounted 40 kinds of stone—each different in color, transparency, and shape—onto a black iron frame. Under the lights, both the exhibition hall's walls and the palace's interior floor reflected beautiful, enchanting colored light, like daylight filtering through a church's stained-glass rose window onto the floor.

![Image courtesy of Caesarstone.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413145904UAcQXiqjFZYHD1oI.jpg-w600)

### Nendo

This year Nendo collaborated with fashion brand Jil Sander, setting up an exhibition of its studio's work in a two-story showroom. It included T-shirts co-branded by the design studio and Jil Sander, furniture the designer created for brands such as Alias, Cappellini, and Moroso, and concept lamps for British lighting brand Flos. Taken as a whole, it was something like a retrospective of the Nendo studio's work over the past two years.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150546iSLRtqj7QF8msfZd.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150546fTSLJ0Cou6FjBEx4.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150546aXuUDmvfB4WVit7q.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150546KLgMTsH2PZf9yVEp.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150546m158Ziqwuozg02Fl.JPG-w600)

Even so, nothing stopped this small Nendo showroom from becoming another hot spot in Milan this year—the queue outside Nendo's door was even longer than Louis Vuitton's. Visitors flocked in to see the installation that had already become famous before Design Week even began: a set of transparent silicone vases.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150558IJw7SWEFBDl42yca.JPG-w600)

The vase set is submerged in a glass water tank; the thin, bluish-transparent bodies are made of silicone and sway in the water like jellyfish. Seen from any angle, they form a marvelous visual image.

### Cassina 9.0

Cassina's 90th-anniversary celebrations this year have two parts. One is a showroom behind the Milan Cathedral, on the boutique street, which displays not only classic products including chairs designed by Gio Ponti but also new sofa series by designers Patricia Urquiola and Konstantin Grcic.

The other venue is inside the Fondazione Giangiacomo Feltrinelli building, designed by the architecture firm Herzog & de Meuron. This is a long building with a pointed roof; the Cassina 9.0 exhibition curated by Patricia Urquiola occupies the 1st and 5th floors of one corner of the building.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150854tg1V9XUfe084J5pW.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150835QYtGTvjLIPlNxBr9.jpg-w600)

Although the brightly colored walls on the first floor and Cassina's furniture create a fun-filled interior space—and the brand and curator even deliberately added VR equipment and other cutting-edge technology experiences—the fifth floor is undoubtedly the highlight of this exhibition. Not only does its position at the high point of the building let visitors take in the views of Milan, but Cassina also brought out the restored Refuge Tonneau, designed in 1938 by famed designers Charlotte Perriand and Pierre Jeanneret.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150908x2b3RmVKXNfEwgQz.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150909HeisZ7yj3KhVcrpW.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150909TlQhBrgzySe4X7EV.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413150910jq9a63ceHWSZ2fUF.jpg-w600)

This miniature mobile building was dreamed up by the mountaineering-loving Charlotte Perriand; Pierre Jeanneret later joined in, and the two completed the interior decoration together. The furniture inside is extremely pared-down and practical—a model of mobile architecture for extreme weather.

### *Melt* (《融》)

The *Melt* (《融》) exhibition at Tortona Design Week is now in its fifth year. In the past, this series of exhibitions was usually curated around a single material, but this year the curator—Zhang Lei, co-founder of the Hangzhou design studio [Pinwu](http://www.pinwu.net/)—brought together all the industrial design work created with 50 designers over the past five years into one exhibition. It can be considered a full overview of the "Melt" project.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413151350EclRL6oy78rQdBZj.JPG-w600)

According to data released by the exhibition, it presents 51 kinds of handicrafts native to China, with materials spanning five major categories—bamboo, silk, clay, copper, and paper—and nearly 500 traditional materials.

The exhibition itself is not large, and is divided into three rooms. Because the content is so rich, the exhibits are densely arranged—decorations, chairs, vessels, fashion, art installations, and more. Among them, the largest and most eye-catching works are "Ledi Garden," made by Pinwu co-founder Jovana Zhang from 15 giant paper-cuts, and "Lake," created by Christoph John, co-founder of the Yuhang Rong Design Library, using very fine copper-wire weaving techniques.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413151408VKlXFwEsRhbM1xgf.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413151409DXfA5ZnMiC2Ns7Ih.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413151410bTrW2CpUYzJ0XnQS.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413151417IJT2stby0dqPXWLi.JPG-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170413151417JkctRsNSDIyXQUTP.JPG-w600)

Another interesting part is that the exhibition shows detailed processes of many handicraft techniques, as well as the properties and uses of different materials—such as how to make a silver-patterned lacquerware wooden box, and the characteristics of different embroidery techniques. Even for Chinese visitors, these details are relatively fresh points of interest.
