It has also been a decade in which LGBT activity in China grew at a pace visible to the naked eye.

Shanghai Pride has reached its tenth year.

The first to do it were some foreigners living in Shanghai, who borrowed the form of the annual "Gay Pride Month" in Europe and America each June and created a local version of their own. Today this "import," modest in scale, has instead become the longest-surviving gay festival in China—though as the internet has spread the term "Pride," people who never understood this community have become more puzzled by the word "pride."

Charlene, who wears a neat salt-and-pepper pixie cut, is one of the founders and, ten years on, the only founding member still around—but she has little ambition. Charlene, 45, has never considered herself an "activist." Or at least, when people ask her about it, she explains: "At first our goals were very small; we didn't even have goals. We weren't trying to fight anything—we just wanted to let people see who we are."

The entire Pride team is volunteers; there are no full-time staff, Charlene included. She came to Shanghai right after breaking up with her girlfriend and works in the semiconductor industry. She says Shanghai Pride has undergone one clear change: today's team, unlike in the past, is no longer dominated by foreigners—80 percent are Chinese. Attendees, too, are getting younger.

At its core, Pride is the resistance of sexual minorities (LGBTQ) to long-standing social stigma and discrimination, and to the guilt and shame they feel about themselves. So-called pride is, more than anything, a form of self-encouragement.

It is also, in a sense, a response—a response to the unfair treatment and neglect pervasive in campuses, workplaces, and family life. A publicly held Pride refuses to privatize the problems of sexual minorities, thereby silencing them in public discourse. As Richard Mohr, a philosophy professor at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign and gay activist, pointed out: "Compulsory personal privacy is the closet that confines gay people."

The birth of Shanghai Pride's first edition was a complete accident. Many members of the Yahoo group "Shanghai LGBT," of which Charlene was a member, were leaving, so friends decided to organize a farewell party; someone suggested they might as well make it a Pride festival. [One of the founders, Lemay, describing the preparations for the first edition in a 2016 interview with the US-China Press](http://ny.uschinapress.com/weekends/2016/06-28/97736.html): "The main work wasn't designing the program—it was contacting and negotiating with the local government, learning what could be done and what couldn't."

### 1.

Shanghai Pride began in 2009. The timing was fortunate.

The 2008 Beijing Olympics closed, showing foreigners a more open China, followed by the 2010 Shanghai World Expo, during which large numbers of expatriates came to Shanghai. That year, Raymond Peng (Peng Zhizheng) came from Malaysia to study in Shanghai. He had no friends in the city. "Google still worked back then—I remember in July I searched 'Gay event Shanghai,' and the first result was a bar, the second was Shanghai Pride."

"At first everyone connected through a Yahoo Group—there was a mailing list. The people with more internet awareness were foreigners or the more middle-class ones, and communication was entirely in English; there were parties, barbecues, and film screenings."

Perhaps by coincidence, around 2009, gay community organizations of various kinds began to spring up across China, many of them positioning themselves as "outward-facing," working with educators, psychologists, the media, and even LGBTQ people themselves. Examples include PFLAG China and the Beijing LGBT Center, founded in 2008, as well as Shenyang Pride and the Weibo account @tongzhisheng in 2009.

"The emergence of any single organization—whether PFLAG China, Shanghai Pride, or the Beijing LGBT Center—may be the result of some contingent factor. But the fact that these organizations appeared in concentration after 2006 is the outcome of a broad overall trend: the diversification of the gay movement's agenda," Wei Wei, an associate professor in the Department of Social Sciences at East China Normal University who has long studied China's gay movement, told QDaily.

The earlier year of 2005 was not so lucky. That year, Professor Cui Zien, the first person on the mainland to publicly come out as gay, organized the "First Mainland Gay Culture Festival" in Beijing; it was shut down by police and the event was terminated. This associate professor at the Film Theory Research Office of the Beijing Film Academy had openly acknowledged his gay identity in class in 1991, for which the school revoked his lecturer qualification and barred him from teaching for the next ten years. After returning to the classroom, Cui Zien helped the Peking University Film Association host the "First China Gay Film Festival" in 2001, which had a wide impact.

The first Shanghai Pride did not go smoothly either. Rehearsals for *The Death of Matthew Shepard* 《马修之死》, a play about the murder of an American college student by anti-gay attackers, were interrupted by police; multiple booked venues backed out at the last minute; and in the end an event prepared over three months was compressed into one week. They wore pink clothes for a picnic in Shanghai's Zhongshan Park and held an opening party at a bar that evening.

The second Pride ran into the World Expo: no public events could be held anywhere in the city, so the timing was pushed from June to November.

Such situations still happen today. Shortly before Shanghai Pride opened, Raymond Peng, who coordinates the festival and selects films for the film festival, received cancellation notices from several venues, whose owners said they could no longer help host Pride events. Another source of nervousness for the organizers: two months earlier, [Weibo announced a "clean-up" of gay content](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52153.html), and the string of incidents led Raymond Peng to wonder whether they should avoid the word "gay" as much as possible in Weibo and WeChat pushes.

But by the time the event was about to begin, he had dropped the idea; nine years of organizing experience led him to proceed with the original plan. "Every year we keep the venues undisclosed anyway… If they really wanted to intervene or shut us down, better to do it early—at least we'd still have backup plans."

When Raymond Peng first joined Shanghai Pride he was not a core member, just working the door at the barbecue party. He remembers that day's event was held at the Paulaner restaurant (Expo site) on Pudong's Riverside Avenue, whose large outdoor garden could hold several hundred people. After that, Pride never had a similar large outdoor party again. "It's very hard to find a place like that now—the main difficulty is finding a restaurant that big," Raymond Peng said.

Over the decade, a large share of Shanghai Pride's events really have been held in bars.

"We deliberately pick different bars for our events because we want to expand the community, and we don't deliberately choose gay bars—those are our backyard. If other events can't find a venue or hit a bottleneck, we go to them," Raymond Peng said.

Over the decade, many consumer-facing venues have also sprung up rapidly across China—bars, gyms, and clubs have all grown massively—and these spaces happen to provide the gay community with many occasions to socialize, helping the whole community expand.

Anchored in these settings, the gay community has long ceased to exist, as it did before the 1990s, only in dim corners of parks or amid stigmatizing AIDS narratives and negative news reports; today it has become part of everyday life.

But nothing warrants excessive optimism. Lucca 390, a gay bar on Shanghai's Panyu Road, took on three events this year that had been abruptly cancelled elsewhere. Chen Dajun, Lucca 390's brand operations director, told QDaily: "Actually, we can't necessarily pull them off. All we can say is that so far we can still do them tomorrow. For us, we're willing to take on the events, but if you ask why we can do them—there's really no why. Everyone is under pressure."

### 2.

Although it's called "Shanghai Pride," the event as a whole has nothing particularly high-profile about it. By its tenth year, attendance had grown from the original 3,000 participants to just 6,500. Events are mostly held indoors—bars, restaurants, commercial hotels, consulate venues, and the like.

The only "public" event is the "Pride Run," begun in 2013.

Sports at past Prides were mostly indoor activities like badminton and volleyball, but hampered by venue-rental problems, they considered which sports would be more convenient. A volunteer suggested running to Charlene—"anyone can run, and it doesn't cost anything."

"Why did we choose running? Because we can't have so many people walking together—that would look like a parade, wouldn't it?" [Luo, an organizer from Taiwan, said in a previous interview with the US-China Press](http://ny.uschinapress.com/weekends/2016/06-28/97736.html).

Even so, the "Pride Run" drew attention. On the day before the June 2014 event, Charlene received a notice from the hotel saying the police hoped they would send someone to the hotel immediately for a meeting.

"At the time we didn't want our friends with Chinese passports to go, because we didn't know what would happen, so we thought we'd send someone with a foreign passport." Since Charlene worked at the time in the Zhangjiang Hi-Tech Park far from downtown, the one who went was Raymond Peng.

Nearly ten police officers talked with Raymond Peng at the hotel. They asked what exactly the event would involve, whether there would be decorations on site, and how many people would gather. "I was scared out of my wits. I had already told my boyfriend my bank account password, and photographed everything," Raymond Peng said.

Charlene saw the conversation as a signal. "They know whatever we do. Actually, we're not trying to hide anything either. For us, safety comes first—we understand the run can't have too many people."

At 9 a.m. on June 9, 2018, nearly 100 people gathered outside the Nike flagship store on Huaihai Middle Road in Shanghai—though half of them had come for the release of a special-edition Jordan jersey, and only the other half were Pride Run runners. This was one of the tenth Shanghai Pride's six Pride Run routes, and the only one with media present. The other five routes, of varying lengths, started from Ferguson Lane, Jing'an Park, the Long Museum, the Shanghai Swimming Center, and the Bund, all converging on the Andaz hotel in Shanghai's Xintiandi. The six routes were designed partly to split up the crowd, and partly as a nod to the six colors of the LGBT rainbow flag.

Nike began sponsoring the Pride Run three years ago, providing support in personnel and supplies, including coaches and pacers on site, and sponsoring "Betrue For Equality" T-shirts for the runners.

Every runner who reached the finish line at the Andaz hotel received a bag of souvenirs from the organizers and a medal engraved with "Shanghai Pride 10." Then, in the semi-open space outside the hotel's first floor, they ate a buffet-style "pink brunch" while a DJ played music.

"I ran 1 hour 40 minutes, starting from the Bund." Luo Yao, a student at Donghua University in Shanghai, took the phone off his arm that had been timing him; the 15-kilometer route he chose was the longest. Luo Yao told QDaily, "I also took part in the earlier rainbow ride, I'm going to tonight's party too, and I'll go to the choral festival as well."

Evie Wu, who handles brand communications for Shanghai Pride, said about 70 brands and businesses sponsored Shanghai Pride this year, with Nike the sponsor closest to mass consumption.

Under the banner of a branded event, the Pride Run looks all the more "routine." But there are misunderstandings. Among those queuing outside the Nike flagship store that morning, some thought it might be a "corporate team-building" event or a Nike-organized "Color Run."

Another event that is not exactly public but also drew numerous participants is [PFLAG China](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52173.html), founded in Guangzhou in 2008 by A Qiang, a native of Anhui. Unlike Shanghai Pride, PFLAG China is more local, and its founder A Qiang has already quit his job to run the organization full-time.

On June 9, after the Pride Run, the third-floor conference hall of the Andaz hotel was about to host PFLAG China's "10th-anniversary special gathering." The barrier PFLAG China has broken through is the "parents" of gay people. This mutual-aid organization has now grown to 56 cities and 3,000 volunteers, with more than 1,500 registered volunteers using real names.

At 2 p.m., the atrium outside the conference room was packed. Representatives of PFLAG China chapters from around the country enthusiastically hawked souvenirs; a red-carpet-like route lay on the floor, except its color was the rainbow of gay pride. At that moment, a group of mothers in qipao filed in, entering the conference room first. Then came the delegations one by one, walking the rainbow carpet while the "host" introduced, interviewed, and admitted each of them. Some men were dressed as women, some wore pink cheerleader outfits with rabbit ears, and some waved flags and cheered.

But most of the visitors crowding this floor, with its heated souvenir hawking, were "our own people"; they weren't really there to raise money so much as to enjoy the bustle. This counted as the largest indoor event PFLAG China had ever held.

The venue was packed—600 people in total. When the lights dimmed, the mothers in qipao walked onto the stage and began a fashion show to express their support for the community, while a father who looked around 50 stood quietly at the very back of the hall, weeping.

### 3.

Beyond Shanghai, Chinese cities have seen Pride-like events, but they rarely survive this long and lack visibility. Take Shenyang Pride, founded in the same year as Shanghai Pride: the event has virtually no presence online. Other organizations hold similar public events under different names—for example, "Love Aid" in the Northeast has run a Northeast Gay Culture Festival since 2012, likewise featuring a series of gay-themed activities and performances.

These events are scattered across the country and in the past rarely had close connections, which has much to do with each organization's mission being different; but even so, they all face the same problems locally: venues, people, funding.

In June and September 2014, Chengdu and Chongqing each hosted a conference called "SpeakOut," then one of the larger LGBTQ-themed events in central and western China, with audiences sometimes of two to three hundred, sometimes four to five hundred, in a format modeled on TED. Its founder was Matthew, then still a college student. But before the event began, they ran into a blow familiar to every gay organization: the venue abruptly broke the contract for commercial reasons, and no other venue was willing to lend space either.

According to Liu'er, a core member of the SpeakOut team, speaking to the media: "The venue wouldn't say exactly why—they just said the hotel was located in a specially administered zone and everything had to give way to their conferences." But whatever the reason, the venues were trying to signal to the team, indirectly, that breaking the contract was not their own wish.

In 2015, Xiao Xin, also a college student at the time, took part in the Beijing Queer Youth Leadership training, an event jointly organized by many gay organizations. He says this was when he began joining offline gay-rights activism. But Xiao Xin observed that many events either shrank in scale or were cancelled.

Compared with the offline organizations Xiao Xin encountered, the subtitling group "QAF Chinese Site" (note: QAF is short for the British/American TV series *Queer as Folk* 《同志亦凡人》; the subtitling group, founded in 2005, began merely as a fan forum for the show) that he first joined never ran into trouble. The gray zone created by the fact that gay films are banned from import in China and thus have no rights holder became the subtitling group's lifeline. Xiao Xin was mainly responsible for the QAF Chinese Site group's new-media accounts and external exchange and cooperation. Before he took over the Weibo account, it had 150,000 followers; it has now grown to more than 500,000.

Shanghai Pride's first edition had 30 volunteers in total; by last year that had grown to 300. "This year we've realized a problem: Pride has grown so big that volunteers alone can no longer sustain it. It feels like we're already running a company—of course, we're not," Raymond Peng said. "If I were to devote myself fully to the LGBT movement, it wouldn't just be a job—it would have to become a life plan, and with existing resources, that's very hard."

### 4.

Another year of great importance to China's LGBT community was 2014. That year, for the first time, positive coverage of LGBT issues in Chinese media exceeded negative coverage.

According to media-monitoring data from the Rainbow Media Awards, before 2011, Chinese media coverage of gay topics centered on two themes: AIDS and negative news reports. But the discursive space Weibo created gave more people a voice. In 2011, a homophobic remark by Lü Liping set off waves of opposition from the gay community on Weibo, and public figures such as the renowned sexologist Li Yinhe, the stars Song Dandan, Kevin Tsai, and Chapman To all joined the discussion.

That year, [on December 18, Li Yinhe published a blog post on Weibo, "A Response to the So-Called Exposure of My Lesbian Identity" 《对所谓拉拉身份曝光的回应》](http://blog.sina.com.cn/s/blog_473d53360102vcjd.html), publicly describing her 17 years together with a transgender partner. Within 24 hours of publication, it was reposted by netizens more than 50,000 times, drew over 20,000 comments, and received more than 30,000 likes.

On another front, late that year, the case of A Zhen—who had undergone "gay conversion therapy" at an institution called the "Chongqing Xinyu Piaoxiang Psychological Counseling Center" and sued the institution—likewise ignited heated discussion on Weibo, and A Zhen won the case.

Weibo has always been the online stronghold of LGBT activism. "When Sina Weibo was first promoted, it aggressively invited Hong Kong and Taiwan stars to join. In that era, *Kangsi Coming* was a very important show for gay people. One of the Weibo accounts Kevin Tsai followed back then was @tongzhisheng," said Hua Zile, a post-'90s who decided in 2009 to open the Weibo account @tongzhisheng, curating gay news from the internet.

Sina Weibo's financial report for February 2013 showed total revenue of about US$66 million and registered users surpassing the 500 million mark. A few months before this report was released, Weibo, three years after launch, had officially dropped the "Beta" label and become a formal version. In that version, a feature was added letting users select a "sexual orientation" in their profile settings; if you chose a sexual orientation matching your gender, your profile would display that you are "homosexual."

The anonymous Weibo platform allowed some gay people who had not come out to find space for free expression there. To borrow the words of Yale history professor George Chauncey, hugely influential in the study of gay history in modern cities: "Such privacy is only possible in public space."

Of course, this free space is not without limits; they still worry about all kinds of things—even something as small as liking a Weibo post on a gay topic.

The many trending topics on Weibo did, to a certain extent, demonstrate public acceptance of gay topics and the openness of attitudes, and this heat peaked with the nationwide legalization of same-sex marriage across the United States in 2015.

This event made same-sex human rights a mainstream topic on the internet, and many gay people saw it as a victory for gay human rights.

Hua Zile still remembers that after the United States announced the passage of the same-sex marriage ruling in 2015, "the whole internet went into celebration; many businesses jumped on rainbow marketing—you'd see lots of merchants riding the wave." @tongzhisheng also reposted the news at the time; according to Hua Zile, the post was reposted more than 13,000 times and had over 10 million views.

But recently, when Hua Zile tried to find the old post again, he discovered it had already been deleted.

The optimism that appeared in 2015 vanished quickly.

On February 12, 2015, director Fan Popo's documentary *Mama Rainbow* 《彩虹伴我心》was pulled from major websites.

On February 22, 2016, the gay-themed web drama *Addicted* 《上瘾》was pulled from the entire internet.

On March 2, 2016, the "General Rules for Television Drama Content Production" formally took effect. The rules stipulated that TV dramas must not "portray obscenity and pornography," including "depicting and displaying abnormal sexual relations and acts, such as incest, homosexuality, sexual perversion, sexual assault, sexual abuse, and sexual violence."

In June 2017, the China Netcasting Services Association, an organization under the broadcasting regulator SAPPRFT, adopted the "General Rules for the Review of Online Audiovisual Program Content," defining homosexuality as "obscene, pornographic, and vulgar in taste," and restricting its online dissemination.

On September 30, 2017, "Walking Together," the best-known gay forum board on Tianya, China's most famous online forum, ceased operation—over 18 years it had accumulated 730,000 posts and 21.1 million replies.

Media-monitoring data from the Rainbow Media Awards shows that domestic mainstream media coverage of LGBT topics reached an all-time high in 2015 with 867 items; by 2017 that number had halved to 447, returning to 2013 levels.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180615011440bRdW9UaNktC0AzxM.png-w600)

In 2013, Peng Yanhui founded the Tongzhi Equal Rights Promotion Association (known as the "Equal Rights Promotion Society" for short), dedicated to using legal means to address discriminatory treatment of gay people. China previously had no similar organization with legal redress as its mission. From the sharp decline in media exposure, he sensed that civil society was changing. He still has an anti-discrimination lawsuit against SAPPRFT's "General Rules for the Review of Online Audiovisual Program Content" on his hands, but he believes the present is a stage of confusion: "They do feel they need to adjust their strategy going forward, but exactly how—no one really knows."

On this, Wei Wei believes China's gay movement should adjust its strategy to find room to survive, and he mentions PFLAG China as a good breakthrough: "I think what's needed now in advocacy is rhetoric the government can accept. For example, PFLAG China's trump card is family. Family is the most central issue for Asian gay people—not something like religious discrimination. However strong the Chinese government is, it still has to take into account people's identification with family values."

This year, PFLAG China's public foundation raised a total of 2 million yuan, held under a national special fund called "Rainbow Partners." "It will be used for activities like improving gay people's lives and promoting family harmony," said Cheng Lin, convener of PFLAG China's Beijing chapter; the specific plans have not yet been announced.

### 5.

On the evening of June 9, most of the people who had taken part in the day's Pride events gathered at the opening party, at 471 Zhapu Road in Shanghai. The old building, put up in 1931, is one of Hongkou District's landmark structures; originally a Buddhist temple, it now houses a bar called "The Pearl," which regularly hosts drag queen performances.

That night's party was far grander than The Pearl's usual events. At 11:30, the final drag show began, and the performers came straight down into the crowd, performing "This Is Me," the ode to misfits from *The Greatest Showman* (《马戏之王》). After the show ended, more and more people began dancing with strangers on the dance floor.

At the opening screening of the Pride Film Festival, we also ran into Xiao Kang and the friend who had brought him, Alevin. On the screen was this year's Pride Film Festival best picture, [*Pink Pill* 《粉色药丸》](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/54165.html). Xiao Kang said: "There's a lot of stuff online, but I still hope there's a physical space where everyone can discuss gay films together, where you can take part in an event this formal."

Shanghai Pride's events will continue into early July, but the densest stretch—including the closing party and the film festival's closing—ends on June 17. Meanwhile, Pride parades are taking place around the world, with feathers, rainbow confetti, and rainbow flags occupying city thoroughfares. Their commemorations will be broader, and longer-lasting.
