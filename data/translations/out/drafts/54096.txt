**"Sometimes, the point is not to change the world, but to explain it."**

**At a critical moment like this one — a moment when we need to work out how to explain this world and how to live with the future — we need to hear wise, independent voices.**

**We invited more than 20 professionals from fields including economics, law, sociology, and communication studies to answer our questions.**

**The questions are numerous and complex, but they are intimately bound up with the future lives of the younger generation. Our interviewees hope that the world left to the young will function well and keep advancing in civilization; and that amid a complex, bewildering world, they can remain independent and rational.**

Ten years from now, where will you be? Ten years from now, what goals will you have reached?

This question, posed by the economist He Fan, may go to the heart of what "middle-class anxiety" is really about at the present moment.

We began with a general anxiety. Middle-class or not — whether ordinary young people who have just entered society or even those still in school — everyone feels especially anxious. To a large extent, it is composed of conspicuous problems: public services and social security in housing, education, and medical care leave people feeling worn out and insecure; they worry that their income and wealth will shrink; they worry their children will "lose at the starting line"...

Why does it turn out this way?

Yan Fei, who has long followed Hong Kong issues, found that compared with the mainland, Hong Kong's anxiety is more tangible: median income fell from HK$19,000 in 1997, twenty years ago, to HK$17,000 in 2017 — a drop of 15% — while average home prices over the same period rose from about 1 million in 1996 to 5.4 million, a fivefold increase. Add to that the uncertainty over changes to Hong Kong's political system — Hong Kong people's anxiety is real: "they can't see a way forward, can't see a future." Mainland anxiety, by contrast, is built on a stage of rapid wealth growth; the swings in income and soaring housing prices closely resemble Hong Kong's, but for the most part it is more an emotion or a state of mind, and its uncertainty is more multifaceted.

For Hong Tao and Zhang Bin, the contrast is with the 1980s — even the 1990s — which were likewise full of uncertainty and rapid change, yet anxiety never became the backdrop of the whole society.

Why do we have this worry now?

Three propositions are worth attention when it comes to the backbone stratum of Chinese society. The first concerns one's own circumstances — the practical anxieties of what is known as the "three big mountains": "housing, children's schooling, and medical care for the elderly." The second concerns whether this backbone stratum can expand — that is, the danger of its members sliding downward, and the possibility of more people rising into the middle class: the former is the root of their anxiety, while the latter is the question of class solidification that has been increasingly discussed since 2016. The third, in a broader sense, is whether they can become the main force of a modernizing transformation — as Huntington put it, when economic growth produces a large middle class in society, it will push the democratizing transformation of society as a whole.

In the future, what kind of society will the middle class — and their children's generation — live in?

"Backbone stratum of society" is an official formulation; our interviewees sometimes replace it with conventional terms like "middle stratum" or "middle class." Strictly speaking, these cannot be substituted for one another. As Ren Jiantao puts it, the middle class is not a concept of wealth but a concept of rights. It is also on this point that China's "middle class" is far from reaching the stage of being entrusted with greater missions — it is hard for it to bear the meaning of the "middle class" explored by the political scientist Huntington. In Huntington's hypothesis, he emphasized that "if you want to create democracy, promote economic growth." To be sure, both we and our interviewees discussed this possibility to varying degrees.

In any case, anxiety — especially the anxiety of the backbone stratum of society — can be seen as a force that drives us to think more. And through thinking about it, we may hope that it will ultimately become a force pushing society forward.

For example, one of our interviewees spoke of how to dissolve the anxiety permeating society through greater political participation: "I think that when a country reaches a certain stage, this anxiety has a certain inevitability. I don't think everything is abnormal. But the state needs to give people a channel to vent. In fact, press freedom and parliamentary politics, to a considerable degree, let people express their anxiety, anger, and dissatisfaction."

The economist Wei Sen pointed to the "elephant in the room" behind much of this anxiety: "Several thousand years of human history have proven that only a market economy is a system capable of generating economic growth. We should develop the market economy without wavering, build a path of rule of law, and move forward along it. If we waver even on this — if I go back to a planned economy... if we talk about abolishing private ownership — then what are we doing today?"

At the core of the anxiety is still that everlasting question associated with the "three gorges of history": what kind of future is China heading toward?

### 1) The middle class is not just a wealth concept; it is also a rights concept

**Ren Jiantao, Professor of Political Science, Tsinghua University**

> The so-called middle class first of all presupposes a system that recognizes the legitimacy of citizens' private property — namely, that citizens' private property is inviolable. Only under such a system does a middle class that genuinely safeguards social stability emerge.

For China, the Party and the state's policies now also place particular emphasis on building a middle stratum for society. It is not called the middle class. Because an olive-shaped society is the most stable kind of society. The living conditions and character of the middle class are oriented toward safeguarding social stability.

But borrowing Huntington's formulation, some basic questions must first be clarified. First, the so-called middle class first of all presupposes a system that recognizes the legitimacy of citizens' private property — that citizens' private property is inviolable. Only under such a system does a middle class that truly maintains social stability emerge. So the middle class is not a concept that can be borrowed at will. Where affirmation of citizens' private property carries too many attached conditions, a middle class in the strict sense does not exist — so I do not really endorse much of the domestic sociological research done in the name of the middle class.

Second, the so-called middle class is not only a state of assets but also a socio-political state. When the property concept is respected, the middle class, first of all, emphasizes the sound and orderly development of society, because it is possible to rise from the middle class into the asset-owning class. The space for upward mobility is open. What it fears is that, due to unstable policy and unpredictable development, it will fall from the middle class into the propertyless class. Thus the middle class faces double pressure: moving upward, the pressure to become an asset owner; falling downward, the risk of becoming propertyless. Therefore, propping up the middle class has, for a normative modern state, a very subtle socio-political effect. The so-called construction of a nationwide welfare system is meant to solve precisely this problem: restrain big capital, support the propertyless, and build up or strengthen the middle class.

But where is the big problem? Does the state have reason to support the middle class by expropriating assets? Or to expropriate assets in order to impose state welfare on the propertyless? In this sense, Huntington did not consider things so carefully. Because what Huntington considered was, under conditions where the middle class in a country like the United States had already assumed a stable socio-political function, how to transplant that solution to the construction of stability in newly democratizing societies as a whole. What he failed to anticipate is that in newly democratic societies, one of the most important problems is the instability and illegitimacy of the middle class's own property. Because legal protection of its property or assets is subject to many political conditions.

So for China today, if we want to borrow Huntington's theory, the first problem we probably need to solve is the further constitutional strengthening of individual property rights — in particular strong protection through China's civil-law rules now being formulated. Cases including the Gu Chujun case and the five major property disputes have shown that the property rights of China's asset holders cannot yet be guaranteed — let alone those of the middle class. Therefore, for China, property protection may be the key link of institutional improvement that we must carry out first in order to cultivate a middle class. This is the most important thing. In short, legal protection of property still needs to be markedly strengthened.

### 2) The stability of the middle class also comes from its own need for stability and from legal guarantees

**Ren Jiantao, Professor of Political Science, Tsinghua University**

> If there is no guarantee of social security, he falls silent out of fear and dare not say a word — and the middle class loses its socio-political function.

Second, regarding the socio-political function of the middle class, we must acknowledge that we should not assume society simply becomes stable just because the middle class grows larger — no such simple, direct relationship exists. The middle class, first of all, has a passion for social engagement. To continue our earlier topic: the middle class is the group most attentive to social public affairs. So the middle class is not merely a property concept; while its property concept is protected by law, its public engagement is the most important marker of the middle class's social character. To protect their enthusiasm for attending to public affairs — the middle class fears falling into the propertyless class and hopes to develop into the asset-owning class — and at this time they are most concerned with the first theme: social fairness and justice. The so-called stability that the middle class maintains for society and the state lies precisely in the importance it attaches to, say, the rule of law emphasized at the Fourth Plenary Session of the 18th CPC Central Committee. Can rule of law achieve self-transcendence, and effective governance through rule of law? Through rule of law, the middle class gains a sense of social security.

The former — through legal protection — gains legal security of property. The latter — through participation — gains social security. Therefore, they will actively safeguard the established social mechanisms. In a society without justice and without rule of law, the middle class cannot perform this social function. We must be clear: we cannot, like the sociologists, simply transplant the concept of the middle class and then go count how many middle-class people we have, or ask what social functions the middle class has. That is static research, or functionalist research. If the research is dynamic, the identity of the middle class is in flux. In structural terms, have its legal status and socio-political status been guaranteed? If they can be guaranteed, the middle segment of the olive-shaped society can truly keep growing. Because if there is no guarantee of social security, he falls silent out of fear and dare not say a word — and the middle class loses its socio-political function. Once the socio-political function is lost, power may close in, and legal protection of property may be treated carelessly: there may be constitutional principles, but no departmental laws to follow up; there may be departmental laws, but no civil or criminal procedure to follow up.

And if legal procedures do not follow up, the second function cannot be performed either.

### 3) Serenity and rationality: the middle class is also a social mentality

**Ren Jiantao, Professor of Political Science, Tsinghua University**

> So many "volunteer fifty-centers" have an intensely revolutionary complex. In fact this harms the stability of our regime. Yet some high-level figures in the regime do not understand this piece of political common sense; they doff their hats and crown the "volunteer fifty-centers," granting them political immunity. Whatever abuse they hurl is taken as justified, as long as they are being patriotic — without anyone realizing that they are running a patriotism business.

Third, more importantly, the middle class is a way of life. For China today, a rather restless society, the middle-class social disposition is relatively serene. By social restlessness I mean: everyone wants to get rich, and this becomes the hardest material lever for the middle class. There is nothing wrong with that. But the problem is that when people only want to get rich, it is hard for them to enter the middle-class mindset. I am restless; as long as I can make money off others, I will do any bad thing — that is not a middle-class mentality. So the state attaches such importance to food and drug safety, yet food and drug safety has not fundamentally improved. Where does the reason lie? Everyone stops at nothing to make money. There is no middle-class mentality. The middle class is also a social mentality — serenity, rationality, the pursuit of peaceful reform. We do not want revolution. So many "volunteer fifty-centers" have an intensely revolutionary complex. In fact this harms the stability of our regime. But some high-level figures in the regime do not understand this basic political science; they doff their hats to the "volunteer fifty-centers" and crown them, granting them political immunity. Whatever they say, however they curse, is taken as justified as long as they are "patriotic" — without anyone realizing that they are making a business of patriotism. This is a tremendous injury to the middle-class social mentality. This kind of irrationality, this restlessness, this refusal to pursue reform and desire to solve all social problems through violent revolution, to enter an ideal state in an instant — that is absolutely impossible. So the middle class is also a social mentality; this must be emphasized. In a sense, Huntington's theory is instructive, but it cannot be transplanted directly. China's social science community — friends in sociology in particular — should pay attention to distinguishing some of these subtle differences.

### 4) What is the middle class, and what are its characteristics

**He Fan, Professor, Peking University HSBC Business School**

> It is not that just because I am a citizen, I have the ability to participate in public decision-making. No — like swimming or riding a bicycle for you, it is something you have to practice.

The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences once produced a report on China's various social classes, and it contained some rather interesting views. One view is: who exactly is the middle class? Judging by economic income alone, the middle class you identify does not match its self-identification. Some people have middling income but do not consider themselves middling — for example, the lower ranks in foreign-funded companies; some actually do not earn that much, but consider themselves middle-income. But if you factor in other social resources, like welfare, then it roughly matches. The economic and social resources you hold match your self-positioning. However, classic sociological theory holds that the middle class is a conservative force — basically with families and property, ideologically conservative, going to church on Sundays, living a dull middle-class life. But in China, you find that on the ideological dimension — the spectrum of thought — it is actually the lower strata who are more conservative morally and politically, while those who are more radical ideologically are precisely the middle class. So it differs from traditional theory.

I think China's biggest problem is that we lack experience of participating in public affairs. Because improving governance cannot rely on government alone; there must be interaction between government and citizens. This benefits not only civil society but government itself. Because you know whom to communicate with. If the government faces a mass public directly, communication is very difficult, because a mass public is hard to control; but if there are social organizations, you can communicate with the public through this channel and many risks can be reduced. This makes it necessary for everyone to learn how to participate in public affairs. But right now we fear all non-governmental social (organizations), and have no such opportunities, so people's ability to participate in public affairs is very low, and their latent tendency toward violence is very high. Because you don't know how to communicate and interact. Observe, for example, the many homeowners'-rights campaigns in urban neighborhoods: in the first stage, basically everyone participates, and the language used is all "Cultural Revolution" language — "down with the profiteering merchants" and such. Then you find that in the second stage things become more rational; some people who have worked in government, or some lawyers, gradually become leaders. Because they know how to communicate and negotiate. This process is in fact a process of education.

Every one of us is now a primary-school pupil of political and social governance; you need opportunities to learn. It is not that just because I am a citizen, I have the ability to participate in public decision-making. No — like swimming or riding a bicycle for you, it is something you have to practice. In fact we do not have this ability; how can we cultivate more of it in the future?

### 5) With expectations anchored in the future, anxiety may diminish

**He Fan, Professor, Peking University HSBC Business School**

> Right now, from top to bottom, we focus on the short term — on whether this year's GDP (growth) is 6.5% or 6.7% — what meaning does that have?

The anxiety of the middle class may not lie so much in mobility, because the middle class has already flowed up to this level, and the passage upward from the middle class is still relatively open. The loss of social mobility mainly concerns those at the bottom: originally you could change your class position by joining the army, passing the college entrance exam, or through marriage. Now, fewer and fewer people join the army; you no longer go to university — can't afford it; and marriage used to include many cross-class marriages, whereas now marriages are matched within social equals, and you can't find a marriage partner outside your own class. So all the channels are sealed off.

The anxiety of the middle class, I think, mainly comes from the lack of long-term expectations about the future. Right now there are many future matters we cannot think through — where does the anxiety lie? First, how much money will I need for retirement? In some relatively mature economies, after paying your pension contributions and taxes, you can spend all your money, because you feel there is no problem. Here, why do you earn so much money and still feel insecure? You don't know how much you will need when you grow old. There is no sound pension insurance system. Relying on the pension you paid will definitely not be enough; you can only rely on yourself — but how much do you need relying on yourself? It is a bottomless pit. Then you have no good public medical care or medical insurance system. If I fall ill someday, how much will it cost? There is no very stable expectation of that, either. Children's education, I think, is more like an arms race — everyone else is anxious, so you are anxious too. There is no way out; it is a prisoner's dilemma.

But the main problem, I think, is that there is no particularly long-term expectation. This is related to how quickly our policies change now. So, for long-term growth, long-term investment is necessary; for long-term investment, people must be made to form stable long-term expectations. In this sense, current policy must give people an anchor in the future, so that everyone has a stable long-term expectation. Maybe this cannot be done in a year or two, but people must be told that this is the direction we will take from now on. Where will we be ten years from now? What goals will we reach ten years from now? Anchor the expectation ten years out. If I believe that we will indeed get there in ten years, I can plan on a ten-year horizon. Right now, from top to bottom, we focus on the short term — on whether this year's GDP (growth) is 6.5% or 6.7% — what meaning does that have? None. If expectations can be anchored in the future, then people's sense of anxiety will probably diminish.

### 6) Modernization itself is a process that makes people anxious

**Wu Fei, Professor, College of Media and International Culture, Zhejiang University**

> Didn't Nazi Germany grow out of precisely such widespread anxiety? Hitler was a product of Germany's anxiety.

Anxiety actually has multiple facets. First, modernization itself makes people anxious. Because modernization emphasizes efficiency and speed, and looks at consequences — an orientation of this type does not pursue (slowness). I think this is a universal effect in the whole course of modernity's advance. Take the civil servants we used to talk about: a cup of tea, a newspaper, half a day gone — no tension, a leisurely life. Although their wealth was insufficient and their speed and efficiency were low, you find they took relatively little from nature and the environment. It was in a relatively balanced state. Anxiety was relatively minor then. But today is different: everyone else is progressing, everyone else is taking wealth, everyone else is growing rapidly. This is a very important change brought by modernity.

Actually, back in Simmel's day, the West also went through a process of widespread anxiety. Today it is the same — Western society is still very anxious. So I think this is the first big direction. It is a result brought by modernity itself. Because modernity itself is guided by instrumental rationality — it looks at effects — rather than (value rationality). This is the big universal phenomenon we should note.

Another source of anxiety may be the uncertainty we feel about our own lives. We don't know whether the environment we live in is safe; we don't know what tomorrow will look like. This type of anxiety is the second level. Our security is challenged on many fronts — terrorism, food safety, the environment, water, pollution; all of these things can (be a problem). Without a sense of security, you are bound to be anxious.

The first level may put you in a competitive environment where you fear falling behind. The second is that your security is affected. Against these two big environmental backdrops, psychological anxiety seems quite normal. I think this is a problem that cannot be solved.

But anxiety itself also has a good side. You seek security, the possibility of finding shelter. Whether perfecting a system or pursuing the guarantees of professional licenses, you seek this thing. For human society, a better mode of governance will slowly be found. Even the "community with a shared future for mankind" proposed by the CPC is, in fact, within humanity's universal anxiety, an attempt to seek a sense of community. Just as Christianity back then stressed "all men are brothers within the four seas" — we are all children of God. In fact this is a possible consolation (that is, universal values). So I think anxiety is unavoidable, but humans are, after all, a highly adaptable animal. This is also an important reason why humans remain the paragon of all creatures to this day. Despite anxiety, we find from within it a goal and a possibility of breakthrough, a possibility of appropriate adjustment. Maybe one day in the future we will find a different way of living, but today we don't know.

Now many people have begun practicing tai chi or yoga, trying to slow down the pace of their lives; more people are becoming vegetarian, believing in God or Buddhism — they are always looking for something to lean on. So the third level of anxiety I want to discuss is actually the relative absence of faith. As Marx said back then, everything traditional has been shattered, and no new values have been established. Under such circumstances, the relative emptiness of spirit leads to a result — as it says in our government reports, many officials today have no faith and just chase money and beautiful women. It is the same thing I am talking about.

Based on modernity as a whole, on security, and on the absence of faith — the threefold results can all produce a kind of anxiety. Since we have identified the problem, there will be different ways of responding and adjusting, so I am not particularly pessimistic about this. Of course, one possibility is that when anxiety accumulates to a certain degree, it becomes a kind of destruction — a destructive force. As far as nature is concerned, it will certainly find a way to balance. It may be war, it may be plague, it may be large-scale disease — in any case it will always find something to balance things out.

So natural law — in the unseen, there is an absolute power balancing this kind of thing; you cannot be sure. So some people end up going mad and may hack others down for no reason; it is all a form of venting. Of course, as a social manager, one must pay attention to these possibilities of negative venting. Didn't Nazi Germany grow out of precisely such widespread anxiety? Hitler was a product of Germany's anxiety. Back then, Mao Zedong's Cultural Revolution was in fact also a kind of anxiety. He hoped to catch up with Britain and America in an extremely short time — only then were there the Great Leap Forward, and, after others criticized his practices (especially Peng Dehuai's criticism), the Cultural Revolution. This, too, is a kind of anxiety. So this is a quite complex thing.

### 7) "Downward mobility" is a perpetual, and the greatest, worry about the future

**Wang Xiaoyu, Associate Professor of Chinese Language and Literature, Tongji University**

> "Wealth doesn't last three generations" shows that personal property is always in a highly unstable condition.

What we face is not only class solidification but also the downward mobility of classes. Even if today you are a so-called member of the middle class or the elite, what class your children — or even you yourself — will be in the future is highly uncertain. You can live in a 10-million-yuan luxury home (soon a 10-million-yuan residence will not count as luxury even in a central city); your children can attend kindergartens with sky-high fees — already belonging to the top 0.1%, even the top 0.01% — yet they may still encounter a fire and fail to get timely rescue, or a child-abuse incident that commands a response only once the whole nation's attention is focused on it. To put it harshly, even some of the most basic rights are not necessarily all that well protected. In such circumstances, anxiety that crosses classes, regions, and age groups arises, to the point that a climate of rancor prevails everywhere. Emigration becoming a hot topic also shows people's universal anxiety.

Closed class solidification is problematic; classes need to be open while also needing a certain stability, so as to give people hope and a sense of security — "those with constant property have constant hearts." There is a saying: "wealth doesn't last three generations." Think carefully and you find this phenomenon conceals many problems and crises. Of course, we should criticize excessive hereditary succession, but "wealth doesn't last three generations" shows that personal property is always in a highly unstable state. If your private rights were protected, and no various catastrophic events occurred, how could wealth fail to last three generations?

"Wealth doesn't last three generations," but "it takes three generations to make an aristocrat" — which means there are no aristocrats. Looking at the history of various countries, the role of the aristocracy or the elite class is very important. For a long time, "aristocrat" was often a pejorative, equated with the privileged class, seemingly irreconcilably opposed to the common people. But toppling the aristocrats did not mean the privileged class ceased to exist; on the contrary, with the privileged class standing vertically above the common people, classes became even more solidified.

Mencius said, "Only a shi (scholar) is capable of having a constant heart without constant property." Judging from historical experience, even for the shi class, "having a constant heart without constant property" is very difficult; the overwhelming majority cannot do it.

Looking around now, it seems that except for the elderly doing square dancing, who feel happy, every class feels anxious. One conspicuous phenomenon is "everyone turning middle-aged, and middle age turning greasy." The word "middle age" had rarely been seen for many years; what replaced it was the ever-lengthening "youth." A claim circulated widely among the public: the WHO defines youth as 18 to 65 years old (and middle age as 66 to 79). The claim spread so widely that on Youth Day (May 4) in 2016, the UN's official Sina Weibo account explicitly debunked it: "The UN's definition of 'youth' is persons between the ages of 15 and 24."

It is only in the last year or two that the word "middle age" has made a comeback. Most unexpectedly, the post-90s generation has hurriedly joined the ranks, often mocking themselves for falling into a "midlife crisis," clutching thermoses with goji berries steeping inside. "Sang" culture (demotivational culture) pervades everywhere — utterly different from the 1980s, when the whole nation was in a kind of adolescence, hoping to "subtract ten years" from their age. There are many reasons for this collective psychological difference; one of them is perhaps related to expectations of the future. When every class faces the possibility of downward mobility, the belief that tomorrow will be better changes, and what naturally replaces it is collective anxiety. Classes should flow into one another, but when every class holds a strong expectation of "downward mobility," a psychological crisis even more serious than the "midlife crisis" will follow.

### 8) Anxiety comes more from the clash of values

**Zhang Bin, Research Fellow, Institute of World Economics and Politics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences**

> During China's high-speed growth, the whole social atmosphere didn't feel like anxiety; it felt a bit delirious. The 1980s didn't feel anxious — back then it felt like this world could hold infinite possibilities.

Actually, the improvement of human capital has a great deal to do with the age structure of the population. The family-planning policy, the two-child and three-child policies — these should all be abolished as soon as possible; from now on, policy should encourage childbearing rather than restrict it. Now, once a family has another child, it is not only the giving birth but the raising, the education (kindergarten, the child's schooling). If urban public services can be improved and the follow-up problems properly solved, I believe more families will be willing to have a second child, willing to give birth. In the current environment, people can't afford it, dare not have it. Even if they have it, they cannot take better responsibility for the child. So it is not just a matter of loosening the two-child policy — what about kindergartens? What about the child's schooling? These must all be solved as a package, so that people don't have so many worries about raising children.

Why anxiety? Look at it this way: when you were desperately poor, were you anxious? During China's period of high-speed growth, the whole social atmosphere didn't feel like anxiety; it felt a bit delirious. The 1980s didn't feel anxious — back then it felt like this world could hold infinite possibilities, that the means of doing things were also infinite possibilities, delirious. Now it feels like there is more anxiety. I think this anxiety comes more from the clash of the two value systems just mentioned. Society's development is increasingly at odds with the values you believe in.

For example, everyone often reads WeChat. On WeChat you can see a lot of emotional expression. Most WeChat content, rather than conveying information, conveys emotion. This emotion is actually an expression of disapproval of certain ideas. Some people worry all the time that a crisis is about to come, always believing that China is bound to collapse, that the yuan is bound to depreciate, that real estate value harbors an enormous bubble. This is deeply ingrained. Because they have never had confidence in economic development, in observing China, in the future; they feel a kind of insecurity. Because these so-called changes are not the order as they imagine it, the order things "should" have. They think the order should be like this, but real development differs from their imagination. Because of this difference, they are very pessimistic about the outcome.

Is the change of rules in reality necessarily wrong? I don't think so. The rules you imagine are not necessarily the best-adapted either. You may have an ultimate goal, but in the process of moving toward that goal, the forms of change are many —

**Hong Tao, Professor, School of International Relations and Public Affairs, Fudan University**

> Hope comes from the belief that effort can change oneself or the world around; hopelessness comes from the feeling that the world around can never be shaken, that one is utterly powerless even over oneself — because "I" can only be an object defined by others. People in the past had more hope, because the world still had some room for freedom; people today feel hopeless, because the world is completely manipulated.

I was born in the 1960s and have lived through China's changes over the past half century. Whether housing, education, or medical care, everything is far better now than before. You may have heard about Shanghainese housing in the old days: three generations living in one room of about ten square meters. Why are people more anxious today? I think people in the past still had hope; people today have more hopelessness. Whether there is hope makes a big difference. With hope, even if the present is bad, you always feel it will get better. Hopelessness means no change is possible — neither to the world nor to the self. "I" cannot decide myself at all; I can only be decided.

How did this change come about? The question is very complex. It may be related to the different stages of economic development. From reform and opening up until the 21st century, the whole of Chinese society was changing at flying speed; after entering the 21st century, everything cooled, congealed, contracted. Economic power, political power, and cultural power became increasingly concentrated, increasingly gathered into the hands of a few. Ordinary people created this world, yet the world they themselves created is hostile to them, growing stronger by the day, hard to shake. Today, apart from a very small number of power elites, most people live under the crushing weight of the "three big mountains": housing, medical care, and education. At my income today, to buy my current home I would have to work at least 25 years without eating or drinking. How many years can a person work in a lifetime? To see a doctor for a minor ailment, queuing three hours for a five-minute consultation is perfectly normal. I have limited personal experience with education, but I know educational resources are unbalanced, and in some places teachers are clamoring for their pay. Generally speaking, ordinary Chinese people don't make much of a fuss anymore — who knows whether it is because the level of civilization has risen, or because they feel that fussing is useless and changes nothing.

The Chinese state is too strong; society is too weak. The state can launch spaceships and build aircraft carriers, but is not very willing to invest in medical care and education; it owes a lot of debt. Recently (I saw online that a well-known professor at Tsinghua said) China's comprehensive national power has already surpassed that of the United States. But the United States — and Japan and Europe, whom we long ago surpassed — what is their social welfare like, their medical care, their education? Have we surpassed them?

China is now very formidable, so people everywhere say "amazing, my country." But what really should be said is "amazing, my people" — otherwise, how does China embody being a socialist country? How does the CPC embody its commitment to Marxism? Unity of word and deed, the unity of knowing and doing — that should not be meant only for ordinary people, at least. When the "people" want to become a bit better, a bit stronger, teeth grind in rage — nothing short of ruining them can assuage the anger; only when the people are weak and corrupt is it acceptable. This is not socialism; this is Legalism.

### 9) The greatest anxiety is the unclear big direction and reform

**Wei Sen, Professor of Economics, Fudan University**

> If we waver even on this — if I go back to a planned economy, like Zhou Xincheng of Renmin University, and talk about abolishing private ownership — then what are we doing today?

The key is that we don't know where the country is heading. This is the big direction. Are you taking the socialist road or the capitalist road? Zhou Xincheng of Renmin University says that remaining true to the original aspiration means abolishing private ownership. Well, if private ownership is abolished and we take the planned-economy road again, what business am I doing? What am I investing in? One day it's back to killing the landlords and dividing the fields again — what is there to develop? This anxiety touches everyone. If we return to the planned economy, assets, property, and enterprises all become (publicly owned). This is more anxiety-inducing than anything. The key is China's big direction; reform is unclear.

The Chinese people's business acumen is extremely strong — no less than Israel's, South Korea's, or Japan's, and no less than any other people's. Chinese entrepreneurship is almost like the pines on Mount Huang: give them a little water and they grow into great trees. Look at the difficulty and high cost of financing, heavy taxes, rising labor costs, the appreciating yuan — business is very hard. Yet these entrepreneurs still struggle on with technological innovation, expanding their enterprises, still moving forward. This is the healthy aspect of China's economy.

When I went to Germany, I found that we Chinese have both the business spirit of Israelis or East Asians and great dexterity in making things. Our phones are made very well, our small home appliances very well — no worse than the Germans'. China possesses both of these. The failure, in the end, lies in the big direction. Look at the 1980s and 1990s: intellectuals were optimistic; everyone was full of enthusiasm, offering ideas, thinking of where to break through — was it reform? Who is like that now? Everyone says: I won't stick my neck out, I won't act proactively; keeping quiet and staying safe is best.

The key sentence: since several thousand years of human history have proven that only a market economy is a system capable of generating economic growth, we should develop the market economy without wavering, build a path of rule of law, and move forward along it. If we waver even on this — if I go back to a planned economy, like Zhou Xincheng of Renmin University, and talk about abolishing private ownership — then what are we doing today?

### Appendix: On the middle-class hypothesis Huntington raised in *The Third Wave*:

Economic development requires a high degree of urbanization, literacy, and education. It also brings changes in the employment structure, leading to a decline in the size and importance of the peasantry, and to the growth of the middle class and the urban working class. These latter two groups will continually demand a voice and influence in the policies that affect them.

As their education rises, they are able to organize unions, political parties, and civic groups to promote their interests.

Second, economic development generates more public and private resources to be distributed among the various groups. Politics becomes increasingly less a zero-sum game of life and death. Compromise and tolerance are therefore both promoted.

Third, economic growth creates a more complex economic system. Such an economy is increasingly difficult for the state to control. As the example of the planned economies shows, state control of the economy can be maintained only at the cost of economic stagnation.

Fourth, the state's relaxation of control over the economy leads to the emergence and growth of independent centers of power. And these centers of power rest precisely on private control of capital, technology, and communications. The bourgeoisie holding this property demands a political system they can influence — one not completely dominated by military juntas, politburos, or dictators and their cronies.

Finally, although economic growth often intensifies income inequality in the short run, in the long run it leads to a more equal distribution of income. Democracy is incompatible with complete economic equality; such equality is possible only under an oppressive dictatorship. But democratic politics is also incompatible with enormous inequality of wealth and income. Economic growth ultimately reduces these inequalities and therefore also promotes the emergence of democracy.
