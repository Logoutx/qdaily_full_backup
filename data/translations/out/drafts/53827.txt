She hopes that "in this era, in this place, I will be neither a mediocre good person nor a mediocre evil one."

In 2016, Qian Jianan quit her job as a teacher at Shanghai World Foreign Language Academy, where she had taught for five years, and went to the University of Iowa in the United States to pursue a master's degree in creative writing.

Iowa City is a small town in the American Midwest. The program known as the ["Iowa Writers' Workshop"](https://writersworkshop.uiowa.edu/) is the oldest and most famous creative writing program in the United States. Over 82 years, it has not only produced a large number of renowned poets, editors, novelists, literary agents and nonfiction writers for the cultural world, but has also rewritten the history of contemporary American literature and established new standards.

Take the Pulitzer Prize: to date, 17 winners have graduated from the program — Michael Cunningham, Paul Harding and Tracy Kidder, among others. There have also been 12 winners on the faculty, including Philip Roth, Marilynne Robinson and John Cheever. In addition, Iowa's creative writing system includes an "International Writing Program," which has hosted famous writers such as Orhan Pamuk, Pai Hsien-yung, Wang Zengqi and Mo Yan.

Unlike most master's degree requirements, the MFA in creative writing asks students to take only one course — the "workshop" — leaving the rest of their time entirely free for reading and writing. The so-called "workshop" works like this: each week a student submits a piece of their own work, and then, under a teacher's guidance, all the classmates and the teacher discuss and critique it together.

"The teacher I've had this semester is a very traditional teacher. He has his rules for writing, and you have to follow his rules. So what you get out of the process is the targeted feedback he gives on your text. For example, he'll tell you that English emphasizes conciseness and force. Language needs rhythm. Language that merely conveys information is useless; language must express conflict. When two people cannot understand each other, that may come through in the language itself. Only then does language have its meaning," Qian Jianan told QDaily.

Before coming to Iowa, Qian Jianan wrote in Chinese and was already a fairly well-known young writer. Born in 1988 into an ordinary Shanghai family of state-owned-enterprise workers, she graduated in 2010 with a bachelor's degree from the Chinese Department of Fudan University. She first spent a year doing copywriting at a venture capital firm, then chose to study at SOAS, University of London. But Qian Jianan found she had no real interest in academia. Under financial pressure, she eventually had to drop out and return to Shanghai, where she became a middle school teacher and wrote on the side.

Qian Jianan's Chinese writing is closely tied to her personal experience, mostly about Shanghai, state-owned-enterprise workers and their children. So far she has published two story collections — 《不吃鸡蛋的人》 (*People Who Don't Eat Eggs*) and *People Only Grow Old, They Never Die* — translated the novel *The Pink Hotel*, won the short-story judges' award of Taiwan's "China Times Literary Award," and twice been a finalist for the Lin Yutang Literary Prize.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180531173007WZYF9ju1y5QAzsJr.jpg-w600)

The writer Jin Yucheng, born in the 1950s, once remarked: "The river of life in Jianan's writing — despondent, plain, authentic, colorful; behind the powerful confrontations between her characters, a compassion often congeals in the heart." The writer A Yi, born in the 1970s, held that "Qian Jianan's fiction and her drawings both prove she is a cold, everyday, impassive God. Only thus do her works achieve their strange brilliance and demonic charm." Her peer Zhang Yueran, a writer of the post-80s generation, even called Qian Jianan "without doubt one of the most courageous young writers I have ever met," with "writing that is restrained and detached."

This semester, Qian Jianan submitted a short story written in English about China's "educated youth."

In the story, two educated youth who have been apart for 20 years reunite in Shanghai and have a meal together. Over dinner, the two reminisce about their years as sent-down youth and feel deeply nostalgic. They feel that young people today lack a sense of responsibility and only think about buying houses and cars, whereas in their day everything was for the country. However, the two disagree over the details of their past life laboring in the countryside in Yunnan — they even disagree about the name of the prettiest girl in the village back then. The two drunk men fuss over this point and want to phone their old team leader to verify it. But being drunk, they dial the wrong number. And in real life, neither of them fared well after returning to the city. Over the meal, the Shanghai educated youth who is playing host keeps touching the money in his pocket and is very careful when ordering dishes — because his wife only gave him 100 yuan to settle the bill, and he doesn't want his old friend to see how straitened he is.

"I only write that meal. All the backstory is explained through that meal. The key to the story is that it moves forward through dialogue; what I want to say is that there's a gap between the role they played in history and the role they play in real life — including their current self-definition, that kind of heroism complex, which falls short of the truth," Qian Jianan said.

Although the story adopts a very Western technique, it could not resonate with Qian Jianan's foreign classmates, who had no way to understand its historical and cultural background. So Qian Jianan feels the story might have been a "success" if written in Chinese, but as a piece of English writing it is an "unsuccessful" attempt.

This is also a question that has troubled Qian Jianan for the past two years. Because she now writes in English, she feels she has become a different person — the rhythm and feel of her language have changed. At the same time, she believes that "60% or 70% of my English writing will fail." "Aesthetically I am an Easterner. That aesthetic cannot be carried over to their side. How can I make them accept it? Or should I sacrifice this aesthetic to conform to theirs? I now feel I cannot abandon myself entirely to conform to their aesthetic, so there is a difficulty of compromise," Qian Jianan said.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180531173129jyzbglFUVCHwKWeI.jpg-w600)

*People Who Don't Eat Eggs* is Qian Jianan's new book published this year, but the novel was completed as early as 2015. "This novel was an accident. More than two years ago, when I was applying to the Iowa writing program I now attend, I had no suitable fiction manuscript to submit, when I suddenly took it into my head to write this novel. I had the tireless body of a young person back then: I worked by day, and as soon as I got home at night I wrote, until two or three in the morning, then slept at most three hours before dragging myself up to catch the subway. Like that, as if my whole body were on fire, I wrote for more than two months," [Qian Jianan wrote in her essay "My Love and Hate for Shanghai" at the end of 2017](https://book.douban.com/review/8997413/).

As for why she chose to publish it this year, Qian Jianan thinks there are two reasons. First, because this novel was a kind of emotionally cathartic writing, and some of the pain was too raw. She wasn't satisfied with it at the time; now she feels the pain is more real. Second, because she feels this novel marks the end of her Shanghai experience. She will continue to try writing in English and will publish no Chinese-language work in the near term.

Qian Jianan loves to mention that she is a Gemini — "curious about everything" — and that she likes to "act on a whim." Applied to writing, she tends to finish a work in one sustained burst, so it often happens that she sleeps only two or three hours a night for several days running, writing through the night. When she can't write, she chooses to copy out novels she likes while thinking.

To promote her new book, Qian Jianan traveled from Iowa City to Hangzhou, Guangzhou, Beijing and other cities to take part in a number of events. We met her at a Beijing bookstore. In the interview, Qian Jianan spoke very fast, full of energy. She finds Iowa City "really very lonely," with its "long winters" and few people — it's easy to become depressed — so the brief bustle and exchange after returning to China made her very happy.

To date, Qian Jianan has published only essays in English, [one of which](https://themillions.com/2018/04/the-moon-is-beautiful-how-and-why-east-asian-stories-generate-plot-without-conflict.html) was even recommended by *The New York Times* morning briefing, but she has not published any fiction, being extremely cautious. She feels fiction needs long polishing, and she doesn't want to travel her old road of "publication" (meaning writing realist fiction) just to get published. Her expectations and demands of herself are high, and she is not satisfied with her two years in Iowa, deciding that she "must first manage to read 10 hours a day," hoping that "in this era, in this place, I will be neither a mediocre good person nor a mediocre evil one."

She also firmly believes in the existence of a world literature that can cross national borders. "That is my faith," Qian Jianan said.

### **Compared with the previous generation, today's young people have more money — so why do they seem to care more about it?**

**Q: From the materials I've seen, *People Who Don't Eat Eggs* actually grew out of your reflection on your own generation. You said: "Our generation seems to have more freedom than our parents'. We live in a relatively stable era, have more contact with the world, broader horizons than theirs, and fewer worries. But what exactly is holding me back? What exactly is tying my hands?" The novel is done now, but looking back rationally, do you have an answer to this question now?**

**Qian:** Not much of an answer — I'm still in the midst of thinking about it. What we just said is actually a bit of a paradox. On the surface our freedom is greater and there should be more choices, but you can see that the whole of society is now turning conservative. Overall, people younger than me should be less short of money than our generation was, yet they seem to care more about money — they may even believe in the power of money more than we do. It's not just China; it's the same globally. Ask young people in any city in the world what they want, and they all tell you: first, make a lot of money; second, become famous.

But my generation, when I was young, really wasn't like this. I didn't believe money could buy everything; I believed many things had more power than money. So I keep wondering: why do young people — who have money, at least more than we had — care more about money instead? I really have no answer. It may have to do with differences in growing up. In my own growing up, at least before high school, everyone's economic level was really about the same; there was no extremely rich class and no extremely poor class. Since everyone was about the same, parents would also teach you not to pursue money but surely to pursue something else. Now, among many friends I can really talk with, we feel we still want something transcendent. I certainly don't think it's wrong for others to pursue money or fame — that's fine. But I feel there should also be something transcendent, that one should become a person with strength. My own expectation of myself is to be "a person who brings strength to others."

But now I see (and I hope I'm being one-sided about this): young people in Shanghai, from birth, have already seen a class divide between rich and poor — stratification begins in kindergarten. Many people flaunt their wealth; the extremely rich class is there to be seen. In that process, they may feel that so-called social inequality concretizes, within the realm of their lives, into inequality between rich and poor. If one wants to pursue equality, one tool becomes money. If that's the case, I would find it a great pity to have no transcendent value. We keep citing the "sophisticated egoist" that Mr. Qian Liqun summed up. For my generation the shoots of it were already appearing; I fear it may be even worse for those who come after.

**Q: Before writing *People Who Don't Eat Eggs*, you also did more than forty interviews, with people who had experiences similar to yours — children whose parents were both workers, whose families had gone through layoffs, your own age — and with their parents. Based on your interviews, did you find any common traits among state-owned-enterprise workers and their children? Or were they all quite different?**

**Qian:** Every one was different; the regions were very different. Take Shanghai. The last story in this book, 《乍浦路往事》 (*Old Days on Zhapu Road*), was written based on that interview experience. In Shanghai, although state-owned enterprises were restructured and parents were hit by layoffs, the Chinese government at the time had many "safety-net" policies. The so-called safety net meant that if both parents were laid off by their factory, one of the couple would be given employment. The safety-net policy was implemented very well in Shanghai, so in that process no children fell into an abyss because of these things; no one was reduced to utter poverty, and life could certainly go on. But in that process, Shanghai's strong regional color brings other problems — the gap between rich and poor is very obvious. Even if it didn't threaten one's survival or reduce one to poverty, because an extremely rich class exists, in that process there would be a psychological gap.

But many other places are not like this. Take the Northeast. Because the Northeast is an old industrial region, everyone was laid off. What happened next for many young people was that they had to leave, to leave the Northeast. In that process you can see their leaving and their nostalgia for the Northeast. The whole Northeast has a different atmosphere. In my conception of the Northeast, for instance, men must care about face. If life goes downhill and hurts his face, then many things cannot be spoken of — there's a part where one's dignity is demoted. This demotion of dignity also works on the child. Because he knows the family has begun to have some content that cannot be spoken of, and some things may trigger his father's anger, plus he has to leave this place — so other forces act on the child too.

**Q: After finishing the novel and the interviews, how do you now view your parents' generation? How do you view China's state-owned-enterprise reform and the layoffs of workers?**

**Qian:** I understand them more. Of course this may also have to do with my being abroad now. I've used this example before: one of my interview subjects said that when he was little he wanted to buy a Wenquxing electronic dictionary, and his parents refused, giving him this reason: "If we buy you a Wenquxing, you'll play games on it." But actually the games on the Wenquxing were silly, and he wouldn't have played them. He was very young at the time and felt his parents didn't understand what a Wenquxing was, so he explained to them that it was for learning English. When he grew older, he came to know that his parents said this because they couldn't afford it at the time, but were too embarrassed to tell him so — so they had to find a grand-sounding reason to give him. After interviewing him, I began to think about what my parents once said to me. Including many of the frictions from when we were growing up — perhaps there really were many things they could not say. So in this process, what's needed is a grown-up me who goes back to embrace them, to understand their hardship. I didn't quite understand it when I was little; I understand it more now.

**Q: 《狗头熊》 (*The Dog-Headed Bear*) is the work you consider your best. Why?**

**Qian:** I like writing personal stories. I hope a personal story can build a connection with a bigger world, with the times. *The Dog-Headed Bear* is an attempt of mine at building that connection that I'm fairly satisfied with. Then, legends and myths — I'm fascinated by these things. Because legends and myths are not necessarily true, but they condense real feelings. *The Dog-Headed Bear* condenses the feelings of people in the Zhejiang region toward the War of Resistance, and fear is in there. I find that thing very interesting. Each generation has its own way of condensing this kind of emotional memory. These things are of course only source material, but I can think based on them every day. Then one day, suddenly, an internal logic forms, a way of narrating emerges — I find that thing wondrous. Because that is what I want to do.

**Q: I'm quite curious: where is the biggest difficulty in attempts like yours to connect personal history with the times and history?**

**Qian:** The difficulty is that if you force it, it can become far-fetched. I think the ideal way is to dig deep into a personal history — you can certainly dig down to a connection with the times. The difficulty is that writers (especially Chinese writers) want to write something big, want to write a grand narrative, but if you keep thinking about writing a grand narrative, it's easy to write shallowly and stiffly. So a problem arises: how do you combine the two? But if you force the combination, it will surely be stiff too. So the best way is still to return to personal history, return to a small story, dig deep into it, dig into that character's psychology — there will surely be such a connection. But where is that connection? Can you find it quickly? Not necessarily. So it may take time to explore.

### **University creative writing rewrote postwar American literary history**

**Q: In 2016 you quit the job you had done for five years and went to the University of Iowa to study creative writing. China actually has no tradition of creative writing programs, and many people still doubt whether a university can train writers. Now that you've studied there for more than a year, do you have any response to this view? I saw you had a line before: "This doubt is a good thing, and deserves the joint vigilance of people inside and outside the circle."**

**Qian:** Of course I still think this doubt is a good thing, but this doubt doesn't exist in the United States. 2016 was the 80th anniversary of the Iowa Writers' Workshop. It was the earliest [writers' workshop] in the world and has trained many famous writers, so this doubt no longer exists in the US. But the US now has a problem: the point of its questioning is not whether it can train good writers, but how the so-called workshop culture reshaped the landscape of contemporary American literature.

Not long ago, the *London Review of Books* published a piece about this problem. First, the US has such a tradition: anyone who wants to be a writer or editor, even a literary agent, goes through this workshop training. Many young American literary people attend, or find a way to attend, a workshop — so you can see that everyone in the literary industry has been trained. Their prose, their subject matter, their style all carry a trained conception, a very strong American style. Of course each writer's style is still different, but contemporary American literature as a whole has a very similar quality, and that similar quality is trained.

For example, the American workshop now has certain principles, established when it was first founded. Such as "Show, don't tell" — you must show, and not tell. And many more. In any case, you must write about personal, concrete things, not abstract, grand things. This thing is not native to America, but in America it was deliberately instilled by the founder of the Iowa Writers' Workshop. Now there's a book — 《创意写作的兴起：战后美国文学的"系统时代"》 (*The Program Era: Postwar Fiction and the Rise of Creative Writing*) — about exactly this. Because they had a Cold War mindset back then. It also rewrote literary history, readjusting the standing of writers in that history: it elevated writers of the "show, don't tell" school (like Fitzgerald) — but Fitzgerald's standing at the time was not like that. Fitzgerald was criticized back then for lacking political engagement, for lacking concern for the broader society.

Why do I say that much reflection in China is good, that this doubt of China's is good? Because we still have controversy now; we have not established a single standard. I would rather not have a single standard established by creative writing programs in universities. That is problematic. I don't think "show, don't tell" is wrong, but when it becomes the sole principle, the sole aesthetic standard, problems arise — you get sameness, and anyway many of the problems you now see in America. But if China keeps having controversies, or if there are other channels through which writers can emerge, I think that is truly a good thing. We will have different literatures emerging; otherwise we may also head toward a narrowing.

**Q: At an event promoting your new book, you said before: "Now that I write in English, I have become a different person." Can you elaborate? What's different?**

**Qian:** Very different. Writing in Chinese — first of all I can confirm I'm a Shanghainese. As soon as I write, I put in a lot of Shanghai dialect, there's a Shanghai flavor, a regional character comes in. But in English, first of all there's no regional character. And another thing: the English aesthetic is different from ours. Chinese characters are square, with their own visual beauty. Chinese can have the beauty of repetition, of ambiguity, of implication. English cannot do this. English is simply very concise, forceful, clear and precise. That is its beauty. In writing English, I feel even the rhythm of my language changes. You must conform to their aesthetic. Because these past two years my English has been trained by my translation-department teacher. What my English has been trained into is entirely that Hemingway kind of style, very spare language. Otherwise they will help you cut, cut, cut. And my language even makes me feel it's not like a woman's — it becomes gender-neutral. Even the voice becomes different. But I feel that in my English writing now, my style isn't settled anyway; it's still in an infant state, and I don't know what it will grow into. That state is quite interesting.

**Q: So in this sense, you would say: "I know that 60% or 70% of my English writing will fail."**

**Qian:** Aesthetically I am an Easterner. I've discussed this with my translation teacher. My favorite aesthetics are still, say, Tang and Song poetry, or the Eastern, Kawabata kind of aesthetic. My teacher said a sentence to me then that I keep turning over: someone who tells me he likes the South should live in the South. So in this process, first, this aesthetic of yours cannot be carried over to their side; they cannot identify with it. Second, how can your aesthetic make them accept it? Or do you sacrifice this aesthetic to conform to theirs? Problems exist in all of this. I now feel I cannot wholly abandon myself to conform to their aesthetic. My difficulty lies here. Because I think it would be a great pity to lose the Eastern aesthetic, but I don't know right now how to make them accept the Eastern aesthetic. I think it's impossible. In this process there must be compromise, so there is a difficulty of compromise. But I don't think now is a time to give up; I believe there will be a way. A good story should be able to cross national borders. Like a good song, like all art. So I'm still racking my brains, pursuing this stage.

### **Contemporary American fiction can be summed up as "the story of my grandmother getting cancer"**

**Q: From the materials, I've read that you've always disliked contemporary American literature, because its literary vision is very narrow. Why do you say this? Can you elaborate?**

**Qian:** Yes, I really don't like it. I've had many discussions with my classmates. One classmate is a Russian-American writer. He sums up contemporary American fiction as "the story of my grandmother getting cancer." Everyone understands the concept. Contemporary American literature is tied to the aesthetic principles of the workshop. It all focuses on very personal things, and it's not quite like the Chinese literary tradition. China has two literary traditions: expressing one's aspirations (yanzhi) and conveying the Way (zaidao). With yanzhi, your personal stuff must relate to the times; you must see the bigger things through the personal. We still value this "big." But in America, at least since the rise of the workshop, attention to the big is very slight. No big things are needed; as long as there's a very small thing, you write it interesting, dramatically strong, eye-catching — that's enough.

Recently America has begun to reflect on this. A few months ago, there was an interview in *The New Yorker* that I found very interesting. He said: in our era, many things are happening. This era is big, but the literature we now see is small. We lack a literary work that can match the size of the world. He wrote from an American angle, an American standpoint. So I feel American stuff now is too small. Of course this isn't necessarily related only to the American aesthetic; maybe it's related to our era as well. Our era is a narcissistic era. Seen through a very narrow thing, it is a very narcissistic thing. American literature originally valued literature's ability to build a connection between people, to let others feel empathy. I think this is the Western concern with literature as a whole. Kazuo Ishiguro has also talked about this. Take Ishiguro — he writes with identity.

America has very many ethnicities. If your story, say, is about a Chinese-American experience, other ethnicities can get the empathetic part from the Chinese-American story, so they can understand you, walk into you through literature, build a connection. But this connection is based on your having many shared parts. Now contemporary American literature treats the shared parts merely as a basic plane of humanity — birth, aging, sickness and death, emotions, that sort of thing. But I feel that because this era is very narcissistic, everyone attends to ever smaller things; if you only attend to ever smaller things, it lacks concern for the whole era, for broader things — morality, responsibility, for example. Because American writers used to write about this. Look at Thoreau, Emerson — of course what they wrote wasn't novels but essays. That generation thought about the moral responsibility our generation bears, what we should do for this era, even reflecting on this era beyond the self. But in American literature today you really seldom see this. I think this is one of their problems.

**Q: Yan Lianke gave a lecture earlier, mentioning ["bitter-coffee literature"](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/48781.html), which is quite similar to what you describe.**

**Qian:** There are many things of Yan Lianke's I disagree with, but on this I agree. (Still,) I don't think the Russian literary tradition is right and the American one wrong; but if it becomes a single, supreme one — say, one literary tradition becomes their mainstream and no other voices resist or supplement that mainstream — it becomes a problem. For instance, America has its own thing, but everyone can also see a Russian tradition existing alongside it; there's no problem at all, and I think that's very good. But now America resists the Russian thing; it only thinks (its own tradition) is right and (the Russian) one is wrong. I think that's where the problem lies.

**Q: Then which tradition do you lean toward?**

**Qian:** I would say both are important. I think it's fine that young people like experimental literature. I would rather have different voices all present. There can be a mainstream voice, but I hope that mainstream opens a gap — not feeling it is entirely right, not with a posture of holding the truth in hand.

**Q: Actually, your writing is still at an exploratory stage — can one say that?**

**Qian:** One can say that.

### **The existence of a world literature that crosses national borders is my faith**

**Q: Last year you wrote an article called ["How the White-Straight-Male Tradition of American Literature Understands Eastern Resonance"](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1623583). Later you said that, rereading that piece, you found you had already changed a great deal. Can you elaborate on what changed? I found many of the topics discussed in it quite interesting.**

**Qian:** That title was chosen by them. Recently I wrote a new article in English, [*The Moon Is Beautiful Tonight: On East Asian Narratives* (《今晚月色真美：论东亚叙事》)](https://themillions.com/2018/04/the-moon-is-beautiful-how-and-why-east-asian-stories-generate-plot-without-conflict.html). First, I discussed what I just told you — the matter of Kawabata's *The Izu Dancer*, the problem of their inability to appreciate digressions. Then I talked about the problem of small and big. Early this year, a story in *The New Yorker* was very hot, called [*Cat Person*](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/12/11/cat-person). Later I wrote them a piece saying *Cat Person* is written very much like Eileen Chang's *Sealed Off*. I said that if *Cat Person* were in China, it might not draw such enormous attention. *Cat Person* is well written, sure, but the story is very small. Take *Sealed Off*: Eileen Chang's stuff is of course also very personal, but *Sealed Off* is well written because the story has an era as its background. It happens to be Shanghai's isolated-island period. You would expect that in the isolated-island period, the environment might interrupt the sweet dreams of Shanghai's petty urbanites and make them care a bit more about the times. But what *Sealed Off* shows is that what they care about is still — after the tram is stopped — flirting with someone, and so on and so forth. Actually there's a very cold undercurrent in it. *Cat Person* has none of these things at all; it's just a contemporary, very ordinary story with no depth to it. I think this is where contemporary American literature has a problem.

**Q: In that article you raised a question many people care about and discuss: does a kind of world literature that can cross national borders exist? You believe it does, right?**

**Qian:** Yes, I believe it exists. This is my faith. I believe art has that kind of transmissibility. I believe, say, good music and good painting should break national borders. Although the angles of appreciation differ, people of all countries and all eras can appreciate them. Literature has the limit of language, but if something is good enough, it too can have this border-crossing power. This is just my faith. Because I believe it, I still want to keep trying. If I didn't believe it, there would be no way.

**Q: Here we may need to make a distinction, because there's a critical voice now — I've seen people discussing it in *The New York Review of Books* too — saying that a kind of "global fiction" is now popular, like Haruki Murakami and Han Kang: the language is simple, easy to understand, with no flavor of the writer's home country, so it spreads easily. This seems to touch on the evaluation of writers like Murakami.**

**Qian:** In any case, personally I would not make that choice. I think that choice has its problems. I'm not targeting Han Kang and Haruki Murakami, but to blindly imitate this — thinking that de-regionalization, or being simple and easy to understand, makes a work spread more easily — is an attempt to shallow-ify and simplify literature. It will have many readers. Because you lower literature's threshold for reading; reading things that carry many cultural labels or cultural content is harder.

Second, the popularity we now see doesn't prove it will still be popular decades from now. Look at the many playwrights contemporary with Shakespeare — some were more popular than Shakespeare, but nobody remembers those people's names now. Time will prove everything.

Third, they also have a big problem: they are easy to imitate. Let me give an example: Carver. Carver really trained a lot of very bad writers. We know there are two Carvers. One is the Carver cut by (the editor) Gordon Lish. This cut Carver's influence is very broad, but the big problem with that broad influence is that the cut Carver is easy to imitate. Seen this way, the de-regionalized, threshold-lowering Haruki Murakami may be easier to imitate than others — but once something is easily imitated and copied, many other people may just be replicas. It won't produce unique voices.

**Q: But actually, when I see this kind of categorization, I also wonder: isn't this itself a very superficial and simplistic summary or misreading of Haruki Murakami?**

**Qian:** Personally I like some of Murakami's works very much. I like his early short story "All God's Children Can Dance" a lot. I also like "A Slow Boat to China," and there's a novel called *South of the Border, West of the Sun*. One thing I like about it is that he switched to a way of making you feel the big things. If you look at *All God's Children Can Dance*, the book is about the Great Hanshin Earthquake, but he doesn't write the earthquake directly; he writes about it in very indirect ways, making it very personal. I feel the Murakami of that time was still writing a big thing; he actually wanted to use that thing to build a connection with the Great Hanshin Earthquake. I saw an interview with Murakami. Murakami very much opposes Kenzaburō Ōe's saying that we should reflect some essence of Japanese literature (note: Ōe also kept criticizing Murakami on this point). Because he thinks that thing is very narrow, and that what he writes is a Japanese tradition that moves forward and opens up. It is also Japanese. I agree with this point very much. I also very much agree with his way of letting you see, within the Great Hanshin Earthquake, something deeper — a kind of influence acting on each person's emotions.

I also like "A Slow Boat to China" very much, because Murakami gave an explanation of this work. Murakami's father had fought in the war in China. He said their family had something like a household shrine, and as a child he kept seeing his father burn three sticks of incense on certain anniversaries and bow. His father felt guilt about the war back then. I feel this sense of guilt appears in "A Slow Boat to China" as a kind of base tone. It includes the parts where the narrator has exchanges with Chinese people — every exchange ends somewhat badly, giving a feeling that between different ethnicities there is a possibility of connection he wants to build, but in the end there are barriers and blockades that cannot be broken through. That thing is very moving. Murakami has his humanistic concern in there. And his technique — he chose a very fresh way of telling, very personal stuff. So that's the part of Murakami I like very much.

But there's a part of Murakami I don't like very much. In his early works you can already see it. What is it? He loves telling gags. For instance, in *All God's Children Can Dance* there's a story called "Thailand." It tells a gag about a polar bear: the polar bear is very lonely, meets another only once a year, can mate only once, and then they leave each other. In it he alludes to the loneliness between people. Next you find that Murakami may know everyone likes his gags — in *1Q84* and *Killing Commendatore* there are lots of gags. He even used many other ways to insert these gags. I feel this is pandering to readers. Because we all know there's an age group of people who especially like reading these gags, excerpting these gags, or posting them to social networks. But I feel that at Murakami's stage, he should be able to establish his own way. He doesn't need to rely on these things. This is the part of him I don't like.

### **The greater a writer's talent, the greater their responsibility**

**Q: [In your previous interview with Marilynne Robinson](http://www.jiemian.com/article/1016754.html), I thought two questions were very well asked, so I'd like to put them to you too — only the country has to change. First: how do you view the chasm between people in Chinese society today? For instance, the chasm between those with higher education and those who never attended university? Between city and countryside? What must we do to listen to each other?**

**Qian:** When I did that interview, what I actually thought of wasn't America — it was that, among the questions I saw Americans discussing, I felt China is already facing, or will soon face, these problems, which is why I put them to her. I deliberated a lot, but I really have no answer; I really don't know.

First, these years in America have made me feel that people really cannot communicate one hundred percent. Second, this happens everywhere. Say, I was born into a working-class family, but I have now left that class and cannot return to it. In this process, some connection between me and them has been lost. Extended a bit: once a college student from the countryside arrives in the city, he probably won't go back either. Then what about the countryside? There's this problem. I don't know how to solve it. This is a big thing.

I translated Flannery O'Connor's literary criticism earlier, and there's a line I like very much. She said: the greater a writer's talent, the greater their responsibility. What you must remember is that when God gives you talent, He gives you responsibility too. Of course I can't say proudly that I have any talent, but if in this process I have gained a lot, I would hope to take what I've gained and do some meaningful things with it. I hope I will become such a person. Everyone probably needs to have some transcendent value; this thing is very important.

**Q: The second question is also related. What do you think a writer's responsibility is at this particular moment?**

**Qian:** Keep writing. This question is hard to talk about. In China's environment, our literary tradition is very good and very important; both yanzhi and zaidao are important. The so-called "keep writing" should, one hopes, be a writing that follows the heart in yanzhi and zaidao. If one can keep holding to that, I think that's very good. Because for a writer, writing is his work, and also his responsibility.

**Q: What do you think of writers speaking out on public issues — like Marilynne Robinson, for instance?**

**Qian:** Writers have this problem: sometimes they feel they understand a lot, (and then) speak out on public issues. I think for things you don't understand, listening is a better choice.

**Q: What questions have you been thinking about a lot recently?**

**Qian:** The problem of omniscient point of view — a very technical question. My teacher likes close third person very much. I previously wrote a story that doesn't work under Western aesthetics. That story, I feel, shouldn't (be written in close third person). Then a problem arose: how do I explain to them the context that China lacks? Later I told my teacher I wanted to create an omniscient point of view. This omniscient point of view, as my teacher and I discussed it, is defined as: a camera with a personality — quite interesting. Next, if you have a voice, it can do many things — it can explain the missing cultural context. Very much like Nabokov. I'm reading Nabokov lately; his voice is very charming. English was Nabokov's second language, but he was a genius. Recently I've been thinking about how to build this voice?
