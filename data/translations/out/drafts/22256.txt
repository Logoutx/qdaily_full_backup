Whether in music or in fashion, it is a cultural expression of anger. So where have all the angry people gone?

On the eve of the National Day holiday in 2008, because aftershocks were still rattling Wenchuan in Sichuan, Avril Lavigne moved her solo concert, originally scheduled for Chengdu, to the Olympic Sports Center in Jiulongpo District, Chongqing.

At the time, the Canadian singer known as the "Punk Princess" had just married the lead singer of another famous pop-punk band, [SUM 41](http://baike.baidu.com/view/71088.htm). On an early-autumn night in the mountain city, the newlywed Avril bounced around the stage still singing: "Hey! Hey! Hey! I want to be your girlfriend!"

That song, "Girlfriend" from *The Best Damn Thing* (《美丽的坏东西》), was a smash hit — a love-themed pop-punk number. In fact, to make it even more popular, Avril even translated the chorus into Chinese, French and several other languages and shot music videos for them.

But you should know this: if punk was really trying to express and talk about something, it was definitely not Avril's lyrics, nor the music videos of SUM 41 posing on rooftops. Punk is a kind of anger — the most direct and simplest venting of the inner rebellion of young people.

You may no longer be young in age, but as long as you can hold on to the youth and anger inside, you are still a punk. Sadly, there are fewer and fewer such people.

## Punk as music: simple in form, not so simple in classification

Musically, punk's form is very simple, and very casual. A simple but catchy main melody, plus three chords, set to fast drumming and strummed riffs — that is punk rock. "We just wanted kids to go to a Ramones show and feel like they could do it too," said Johnny Ramone, the Ramones' guitarist. "Not like in the '60s, when you'd watch Jeff Beck or Jimi Hendrix play and think you'd have to practice for 20 years to play songs like that."

![The Ramones](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021608146CDXl2kRHuZP4wI0.jpg-w600)

Beyond the rhythm of the music itself, though, punk seems impossible to define. It has only one core meaning — angry resistance. Scottish singer Shirley Manson once joked in appraisal: "If rebellion is the defining trait of punk, then Mozart was probably a punk too. He was so mischievous, so out of step with his era."

Avril satisfies neither criterion. The flashy sound effects she used on her records, the pop vocal style and arrangements, the exaggerated publicity — even the hi-fi production — none of it is punk rock. People generally call her pop-punk.

In the early days, punks opposed classifying pop-punk as punk at all. According to punk's definition of itself as non-mainstream, it should not consort with "pop." They insisted that the commercialization of punk would bring disillusionment to the movement.

After the most primitive punk of the 1970s, America in the 1980s produced hardcore rock, known for an extremely fast, crude and simple style; it slowly declined because the style was too violent and too underground. Skate punk, street punk and emo followed.

Pop-punk's rise was a '90s story, generally dated to Nirvana's entry into the mainstream music scene, but the style truly took off after Green Day released *Dookie* in 1994. Centered on California's Bay Area, the wave's representative bands included

Green Day

, Offspring, Bad Religion, Rancid, Pennywise, and others.

In fact, China had punk rock more than a decade ago, too: Beijing's "Bored Army." Back then, the dozens of bands in that army — Brain Failure, Reflector, 69, aboys and so on — played China's earliest punk rock. Today, though, they have either broken up or, like Reflector, turned to playing pop-punk.

![Green Day: *Dookie*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702160815lTj7ut54dev3fRHC.jpg-w600)

Pop-punk merely borrows punk's rhythm, but its spiritual core has changed completely — precious and vain.

## Real punk, though, is a form of cultural expression

Punk emerged in the 1970s, rising among the working class of the United States and Britain — a genuine youth movement. They chose the English word PUNK to draw a clear line from mainstream society — young and stubborn, bored, inferior, worthless. They started with music, expressing their attitudes toward life and their refusal to submit, and later the punk movement spread into broader fields such as clothing, design and comics.

That era truly belonged to the young. It was a time of high global fertility and explosive population growth. This was especially evident in Europe. By the mid-1960s, Europe seemed full of young people everywhere — in France in 1968, the student population aged 16 to 24 exceeded 8 million, reaching 16.1% of the national population. By 1960, in the Netherlands, Ireland and Finland, 30% of the population was under 15.

The young people of that era also ran into problems. In earlier years, the population explosion would have put severe pressure on a nation's food supply; even if everyone could be kept fed, their employment would be a big problem. But in a period of rapid economic growth and great prosperity, the main problem facing teenagers was not food, clothing or housing, not even jobs, but how to get an education.

This was the backdrop against which punk appeared — the world suddenly had a whole new cohort of young people. Before the world had time to instill adults' worldview in them, they had reached the age where they could understand the world for themselves. Middle-class life, in their eyes, was not as satisfying as it had seemed to their parents' generation; instead it was full of the boredom that comes after material comfort. They could not win broad social recognition, and were eager to show themselves different from those who came before. They wanted to voice their own claims and rebel against their fathers' generation. Their thinking fixed on the here and now. They wanted to rebel.

In modern intellectual history, the period from the 1960s to the 1980s is also known as the era of "revolt and reaction." It was during this period that youth student movements broke out on a broad scale in both East and West — all large-scale student unrest and rebellion. The "Red May" student movement in Paris in 1968, Japan's student movement, and so on. Students demanded reform of the social system and went on strike and marched in the streets. During that period, what happened in China also changed that country completely — though of course not only because of the young.

![June 15, 1960: Japan's "Bloody Protest Day"](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021608168tr7en2yLFI15SYc.jpg-w600)

At the same time, the 1970s were the age that came right after the hippie movement. People had just been through the baptism of "love and peace," and rock had just taken up the mantle of blues to become the mainstream music among teenagers. The people who created original punk rock — The Who, The Velvet Underground, Iggy Pop — were themselves representatives of the hippie movement. The doors of perception had now opened for teenagers; self-awareness took root in their hearts. They learned to understand the world in their own way, and to express it in their own way.

But having lived through the failure of the hippie movement, the young people of that time chose not to keep dreaming the "live in love and peace" dream. They no longer built themselves solely out of idealism, but pushed self-expression and catharsis to a higher climax: no talk of ideals — instead, criticizing and resisting with the sharpest sarcasm, the crudest words, and the weirdest get-ups. Life was not flowers everywhere, but "no fun," as Iggy Pop sang; there was no beautiful future as the grown-ups taught. In their most famous song, *God Save the Queen*, the Sex Pistols shouted at the end: "no future, no future for you, no future for me."

![*God Save the Queen* album cover](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702160816ZMxqRAstdeGgS5aQ.jpg-w600)

Against this backdrop, punk was the young people's declaration of war on middle-class life.

## Who becomes a punk

In 2007, a documentary called *Punk's Not Dead* (《朋克不死》), directed by American punk fan Susan Dyn, was released. In it, David Vanian, lead singer of The Damned, said that before playing punk he had worked as a gravedigger for a year. Joe Strummer, lead singer and guitarist of another punk band, the Clash, said: "If there were good jobs, we wouldn't play punk. Maybe we could even sing something sweet."

Joe Strummer also said: "Punk is rebellious rock. It belongs to teenagers. It's not music played by a bunch of old guys for your mom and dad. It's working-class music — it has nothing to do with the middle class or the road riders."

That was the original punk: a means for working-class youth to express anger. Everyone had a band. Even with no experience, you could form a band; with no instruments, as long as you could roar, that was enough. You didn't need a musical gift — if you wanted to do it, you could. Every variety of socialism and anarchism mixed together. Teenagers gathered, bringing their own songs; the format didn't matter, the gist was one — we only listen to ourselves. Joey Ramone, the Ramones' lead singer, described his music this way: "It's about our loneliness and isolation, just some feelings."

That feeling may be more complicated than loneliness and isolation, because once sung, it became "Hey, ho, let's go!" That urgent, furious line comes from the Ramones' *Blitzkrieg Bop*, one of their most famous songs.

Across the ocean in Britain there was a group of young people who may have been even more furious than them: the Sex Pistols. In the summer of 1978, the Sex Pistols released *God Save the Queen* and, on the very day of the 25th anniversary of Queen Elizabeth II's accession to the throne, held a concert on a boat on the Thames, playing that song. It was the sharpest satire of the British dream. With the most couldn't-care-less attitude they did the most fearless thing. Lead singer Johnny Rotten said it was "a rose for the fools."

![The 25th anniversary of Queen Elizabeth II's accession](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702160817RqUSvVG1oFJ6Zjp7.jpg-w600)

But punk rock was not all crude and simple. Patti Smith was one punk-scene misfit. Her music was more like poetry, and her performances were entirely poetic performances. When she closed her eyes, language began to overflow. She seemed like a woman who had walked out of the '60s hippies, only turning the poetry of that era into three-chord punk rock. Some call her punk rock "art punk." In her songs she sang in the most direct realist language: "Jesus died for somebody's sins, but not mine."

## Whether in music or in fashion, punk was extinguished by commercialization

In late 1976, the British media began trying to destroy punk rock. The Sex Pistols appeared on a morning news talk show, where the host kept mocking them, convinced they were just a bunch of hooligans rather than people making music seriously. The Sex Pistols members and their friends first earnestly said they really were serious about the music, then unloaded a volley of profanity at the host on air.

![The Sex Pistols and their friends on the Thames TV show "Today"](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702160818y5IFDwbUgC10KPSk.jpg-w600)

This early punk movement — rebellious and disdainful, like a resurrection of the rock spirit — had not yet been targeted by commercial mainstream companies. In fact, the major record labels knew nothing about it, and no label would sign a punk band. They all treated punk as garbage, as high risk, not as a business opportunity.

Punk bands could not even be bothered to look for those labels to put out their records; DIY was the fashion. The Buzzcocks made the first DIY punk record, *Spiral Scratch*, in Manchester — recording took just half an hour and cost 500 pounds. The Lo-Fi quality caused by the crude recording even became a symbol of punk. The band's member Pete Shelley said of that record: "We didn't do it on purpose, but it fits the punk creed. You can do it yourself, nothing can stop you. It may not be shiny or perfect... I think its value lies in its imperfection — it describes life more vividly."

But in the end, punk was commercialized.

In the late 1980s, grunge emerged in the Pacific Northwest of the United States, its main bands Nirvana and Pearl Jam, and their music achieved commercial success. This led to another punk style succeeding in the mainstream, known as power pop or pop-punk. In 1991, punk exploded. Nirvana sold 5–6 million records, then came Offspring, and then Green Day, who were even nominated for a Grammy. Punk music had now become a commercial phenomenon in America.

In 1996, commerce and punk grew closer, but they were torn — on one side the profits commerce brought to the music itself, on the other the contempt from the punk community.

An important figure to mention here is Kevin Lyman, who founded 4Fini, a company specializing in organizing live music festival events. Its most famous product is the [Warped Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vans_Warped_Tour). This large-scale annual outdoor [music festival](https://www.baidu.com/s?wd=%E9%9F%B3%E4%B9%90%E8%8A%82&tn=44039180_cpr&fenlei=mv6quAkxTZn0IZRqIHckPjm4nH00T1Y1mWIBPyFBrHfLmvfkP1I-0ZwV5Hcvrjm3rH6sPfKWUMw85HfYnjn4nH6sgvPsT6KdThsqpZwYTjCEQLGCpyw9Uz4Bmy-bIi4WUvYETgN-TLwGUv3ErjR3n1bsn1R) performance sometimes also hosted extreme sports events, forming a comprehensive outdoor [music festival](https://www.baidu.com/s?wd=%E9%9F%B3%E4%B9%90%E8%8A%82&tn=44039180_cpr&fenlei=mv6quAkxTZn0IZRqIHckPjm4nH00T1Y1mWIBPyFBrHfLmvfkP1I-0ZwV5Hcvrjm3rH6sPfKWUMw85HfYnjn4nH6sgvPsT6KdThsqpZwYTjCEQLGCpyw9Uz4Bmy-bIi4WUvYETgN-TLwGUv3ErjR3n1bsn1R).

In 1996 the event drew a large crop of punk bands to participate. Vans and the department store Target both came running with sponsorships. At first the punks were wary, but they figured this might also be a way to live better.

"This is our best tour," said Jake Kolatis, lead singer of the Casualties. "Honestly I can't say what's wrong with them. They don't represent everything. Join up, make more money — that's how it should be. Using the industry that capital provides for free is better than starting from scratch. That way doesn't work anymore. This way there's still a chance to make money."

That was roughly the thinking of a later cohort of punk players: they hoped commerce could give them a leg up. In 2006, the department store Selfridges directly staged a punk-themed celebration, even declaring: "Selfridges can represent the punk of the future."

At the same time, punk style became a fashion, and many brands wanted to pan for gold in this subculture. As with the music world, real punks sneered at the fashion world and its imitators.

Fashion magazines even made punk guides, things like "Six Steps to Dress Like a Punk," so that pop culture could spread the punk spirit more conveniently.

Punk's original get-ups — piercing the face, tearing clothing and fastening it with pins or straps, painting and decorating clothes with pigment, wearing black garbage bags as shirts or dresses, using safety pins and razor blades as ornaments — and their deliberate use of leather, rubber and vinyl for clothing, evoking criminality, sexual violence and perversion. In short, the look was extremely exaggerated, wanton and provocative.

These clothes also reflected punk music itself. Singing with angry emotion, punks would rip their jeans — and of course sneakers and leather jackets, along with T-shirts printed with provocative slogans, were also signature elements of punk fashion.

But the people buying these cool-looking clothes may not understand this point; they think that's all there is to it. Again, Patti Smith — that misfit punk singer. The cover of her famous album *Horses* is a photo of her wearing a man's shirt and suspenders, a man's jacket draped over her shoulder, tie loosened, expression resolute — she looks like a woman full of confidence. There are none of those messy accessories, no punched holes and studs. Punks just dressed to look more like their true selves, without caring about other people's eyes.

![Patti Smith's *Horses* album cover](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702160818UZSGxCeibPfFhY56.jpg-w600)

In the history of punk fashion, Vivienne Westwood — punk's "godmother" — is an important figure. Her anger often turned political (but then, whose doesn't? Sometimes this trick works pretty well). Just that past September, at London Fashion Week, Westwood turned the runway show of her Red Label diffusion line into a street protest march, denouncing the ugly faces of politicians.

Her punk history need not be recounted here at length. As the former girlfriend of Malcolm McLaren, the pair's behavior and dress happened to chime with the anarchism then in vogue in 1970s Britain.

Take, for example, that 1974 clothing shop called SEX (later renamed Seditionaries), whose door thoughtfully bore the line "clothes make the man, but truth is happy to go naked." It sold punk-style fashion, much of it Westwood's own design, and the Sex Pistols were regulars.

Rubber garments and bondage pants in punk style sold furiously, and Vivienne Westwood became famous through them. They also successfully created a fashion of T-shirts with provocative logos, printed with words like "Cambridge Rapist" and "Paedophile." Their most famous work was the T-shirt printed with "God Save the Queen," with a safety pin through the Queen's nose.

Although American punk and British punk never unified musically, on the matter of dress they were indistinguishable. American musician Richard Hell started a vogue for facial piercings and pinned-on safety pins. This painful-looking fashion still works time and again — in McQueen's newly released menswear collection, male models wore a big safety pin on the face (really, it looks excruciating).

These get-ups were also used for political expression, and punk fashion evolved into the style that is obvious today, including the Mohawk David Beckham sported in 2000, plus collars and tattoos.

Then Jean Paul Gaultier and Zandra Rhodes also did punk fashion designs, and punk fashion successfully made its way onto the runway. Now more and more brands are doing punk fashion: Too Fast, Living Dead Souls, Cyberdog, Demonia, Tripp, Lip Service, Iron Fist, Dead Threads, Morbid Threads, Spin Doctor, Criminal Damage, Hell Bunny, Visual Kei, Hot Topic, Banned — all of them punk fashion brands (can you tell from their names?).

Yes, punk became a fashion — and that means it lost its original meaning, and the value of the "true self" dimmed. "In the '70s and '80s, punk dress marked identity, proclaimed belief — mainly, it sorted friend from foe. But later it was different," appraised Chris Morris, a Billboard writer and Hollywood reporter.

## Why have all those angry young people disappeared

As at the beginning: the so-called "punk" we hear today is the pop-punk of Avril, Sum 41 and the like; the people on the street who look punk are just fashion camp followers. The anger and rebellion of that time are gone, no longer criticizing the ills of the day, no longer even expressing a true inner life. Because it is a business, it needs to be popular, needs to repeat over and over the things people approve of most.

But analyzing from the deepest cause, the age structure of the population is still the key to the problem. Aging is a problem that countries around the world now commonly face. In 2006, the world's population aged 60 and above reached 688 million, and that number is still growing. The situation is exceptionally severe in Europe, one of punk's birthplaces: in 2004, among the 20 countries with the highest proportion of people over 60 in the world, all but Japan were in Europe.

Low fertility is another of their problems. Today, in many European countries the birth rate is far below the death rate. In Spain, Greece, Poland, Germany and Sweden, each woman has on average fewer than 1.4 children. Extrapolating from this, by 2040 the population of many European countries will shrink by one-fifth, if not more.

In a society growing old like this, where would you find enough angry young people to play punk together, to say no together?

As the proportion of young people declines, the middle class has also become ever more universal. People have gradually accepted middle-class thinking and a middle-class way of life, which is surrounded everywhere by a refined, prudent and settled attitude. But punk wants to smash all existing constraints and rules, and to assert everything it perceives regardless of consequences. In this struggle, the middle class is the ultimate victor: commercialization wore away the punk spirit, and a comfortable, easy life became people's daily reality — or at least their goal.

Young people are no longer angry — or rather, they no longer dare be so angry. Even if someone wants to fight something, they may not find enough people to back them up, and so gradually lose momentum.

Besides, "anger" may have shifted its position. The rise of the internet and social networks has given more subcultures their own hidden corners, and given anger all kinds of variants — it no longer has to take an external form like music and broadcast it loudly. And in some places, speech control can be a great help: some people stay silent out of bewilderment and cynicism, others out of fear.

Still, punk has not disappeared completely. It still exists in some corners of the world. 1990s is a fine punk band; on their 2007 album *Cookies*, they sang out their cynicism in the most classic punk tones.

In 2015, Vogue held a punk-themed party, fine clothes filling the room, everyone politely raising glasses and chatting. Grace Coddington, the creative director at the time, was present. She looked around and said: "Look around us — no one here is a punk."
