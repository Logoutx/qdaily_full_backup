Chinese people are actually all too familiar with so-called plant milk — so why does it still feel fresh to consumers?

"Hmm... it doesn't seem to have that gamey smell of milk." At Barista Specialty in Sanlitun, Beijing, a customer said this after trying the oat milk latte the barista had recommended.

After the flat white, the piccolo latte, and the "dirty" coffee, the oat milk latte has become the new thing to push. On café menus across Beijing and Shanghai you may spot "oat milk +5 yuan" or "New: oat milk latte." But the force behind this new product isn't the cafés — it's a Swedish company that sells oat milk products, Oatly.

Six months after formally entering the Chinese market, Oatly is already in more than 1,300 cafés. Many of them don't even offer soy milk — Seesaw and Barista Specialty, for instance — but they've taken up Oatly oat milk.

![(Image source: Oatly)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030005730ohaMFDG7fJ05Bcpd.jpeg-w600)

"It's because the product quality is good, and it pairs really well with coffee" — CEO Toni Petersson told QDaily when asked why Oatly had been able to enter so many cafés in such a short time — though of course, you can guess he'd say that. Toni once ran a café himself. He had baristas take part in Oatly's R&D, which is how the gray-packaged "coffee master" oat drink series came about.

But the more important reason is word-of-mouth promotion within the barista community. "Many of our staff come from the barista community. Our people know baristas and respect them deeply. Other companies don't have this kind of close relationship with baristas," he said, noting that nearly everyone on Oatly's Hong Kong team has a coffee industry background.

This community-style operation makes it easy for Oatly to talk cooperation with cafés and move into them. Lu Jianxia, owner of Manner Café, told QDaily that a friend in coffee circles had recommended Oatly to her; Liu Yeyan, owner of Barista Specialty in Beijing, said the people doing Oatly's business development have "known him all along."

It took only a year and a half for Oatly to enter 2,500 cafés in the US — mostly small third-wave coffee chains or independent shops like Intelligentsia. In 2017, [Oatly's sales were 640 million Swedish kronor](https://live.retail-week.com/speakers/toni-petersson) (about 500 million yuan); thanks to its expansion in both China and the US, its 2018 sales are expected to reach 1 billion kronor (about 770 million yuan).

"This (expanding through cafés) is a global strategy — we're the same everywhere," Oatly's CEO Toni Petersson said.

It looks like Oatly arrived at the right place at the right time. Coffee is one of the hottest drinks in China's first- and second-tier cities. According to a survey by Meituan and 《咖门》 (*Kamen*), the number of cafés nationwide had exceeded 100,000 by the end of 2016, and the market is still growing at 16%. More mass-market coffee players haven't yet shown any inclination to embrace oat milk, but it's still good to build the atmosphere first — after all, you never know when Starbucks and McDonald's will follow the specialty cafés' lead and add oat milk to their menus as "the right choice."

![Oatly in a coffee shop (Image source: Oatly)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201810292314229j3NJZ6rT4nMqWKy.JPG-w600)

### "The Right Choice"

Oats are [a traditional food in the Nordic region.](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2018/03/09/591957289/the-great-norwegian-porridge-debate-or-tradition-vs-science) In the 1990s, a laboratory at Lund University in Sweden discovered an enzyme that can turn oats into oat milk. Before Oatly was repositioned in 2014, it was an unremarkable drink aimed at people with poor digestion or lactose intolerance.

Toni Petersson and Oatly's creative director John Schoolcraft recast it as a more avant-garde, "challenger"-postured beverage, setting it squarely against milk. "It's like milk, but made for humans," "the post-milk generation" — that was their copy. In the interview, Toni — promoting his own product — also voiced the view that "milk is for calves, not for people."

After the rebrand and the tilt toward the café channel, Oatly caught on first in Sweden, with over [40% of the plant milk market](http://news.cision.com/king-street-pr/r/oatly--sweden-s-fast-growing-lifestyle-brand-of-plant-based-products,c2135800), followed closely by other Nordic and European countries. Before entering the US market in 2016, it was already [Europe's second-largest "alternative milk" brand](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2018/08/06/hey-wheres-my-oat-milk). Because the "plant-based diet movement" was popular in the US, it quickly found a foothold there. Beyond local independent cafés, Oatly also got into mid-to-high-end supermarkets such as Whole Foods and Target.

![Oatly original oat milk (Image source: Oatly)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181029233055z7wVlrq2gTe35nfU.jpeg-w600)

Mintel believes plant milk's ["functional benefits" and "distinctive taste and texture"](http://www.mintel.com/press-centre/food-and-drink/us-non-dairy-milk-sales-grow-61-over-the-last-five-years) are the reasons for its popularity. And a Food Navigator survey of 1,000 people on plant milk found that taste is the most important factor influencing American consumers' decisions, followed by price, health benefits, ingredient sourcing, and whether it is natural.

Wherever it appears, the arrival of oat milk and plant milk comes down, in the end, to people wanting a new flavor. But in post-industrial Europe and America, plant milk's "lower fat" or "more fiber" makes it look like the more correct choice. Plant milk is also endowed with broader social meaning — "good for the environment," "lower carbon emissions" — and it is more ethical when animal welfare is taken into account.

Food Navigator found that millennials and the younger Gen Z, compared with previous generations of consumers, care more about whether plant milk is healthy and environmentally sustainable. Another [Nielsen survey reached the same conclusion.](https://www.nielsen.com/us/en/insights/news/2016/americans-are-nuts-for-almond-milk.html)

Toni will reinforce your perception of his product like this: "Producing oat milk generates 80% less carbon emissions than producing milk." That is obviously one of their selling points. He pledged to cut emissions at the Swedish factory by a further 25% before 2020. Rod Coligado, owner of Butler, a café in Williamsburg, [told The New Yorker](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2018/08/06/hey-wheres-my-oat-milk): "It's environmentally sustainable, requires less water, and the product contains no added sugar."

Given all this, you can pretty much sketch the typical oat milk enthusiast. In the brand's description, they are a group of people who demand something of themselves, of environmental protection, and of their quality of life. Real life is probably more flexible: environmentalists, vegans, or the lactose intolerant may indeed be oat milk customers — but those who are just following the trend and trying something new can't be ruled out either.

### Plant Milk Is Becoming a Trend

According to Mintel's report, US sales of plant milk (or plant-protein beverages) reached [US$2.11 billion](http://www.mintel.com/press-centre/food-and-drink/us-non-dairy-milk-sales-grow-61-over-the-last-five-years) in 2017. From 2012 to 2016, plant milk's compound annual growth rate [hit 20%](http://www.enfasis.com/Presentaciones/FTSMX/2017/Summit_Food_Tech/FRIDA_KAHLO-TOP_TEN_TRENDS-LU_ANN_WILLIAMS.pdf). The plant milk market has opened up, and the category keeps diversifying — beyond the mainstream almond milk, soy milk, and coconut milk, there are now quinoa protein drinks, pecan protein drinks, rice milk, hazelnut milk, flax milk, and more. Starbucks in the US already offers a rich choice of bases: besides cow's milk and soy milk, there are coconut and almond milk.

You could perhaps see the rise of plant milk as part of the "plant-based diet" movement (Plant-based movement). As its name suggests, it advocates eating more plant-based products and fewer animal products.

Plant milk was initially aimed at the lactose-intolerant, launched as a substitute for milk. But the health selling point quickly broadened.

Some consumers have received the message that "plant milk is healthier than milk." Mintel's survey found that one in five Americans is cutting back on milk for health reasons. But according to US Department of Agriculture surveys, people who buy plant milk haven't given up animal milk — 90% still drink milk, just in smaller amounts.

Mintel's beverage analyst Megan Hambleton said: "Milk actually has the things plant milk consumers value — minerals, vitamins, and protein — but consumers are unaware of this."

[Bonnie Wertheim of The New York Times argues](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/01/19/style/oat-milk-coffee-oatly.html) that Oatly is free of dairy, nuts, soy, and any GMOs, and — as the brand claims — relatively high in soluble fiber: all decent selling points. But Oatly has roughly twice the carbohydrate calories of the same amount of almond milk.

Compared with Meiji's Chunyi full-fat milk, original Oatly oat milk has only a third as much protein per serving (100 ml), but half the fat. On calcium, the two are about the same.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030005322htecwJBL36rzgv2y.png-w600)

As with any food or nutrition research — about milk or plant milk — you always hear both sides. Marion Nestle, a professor of nutrition at New York University, said: "From a nutritional standpoint, plant milk has its value, but as with most processed foods, it's better to eat ingredients closer to their original state — almonds, oats." (Soy milk may be the exception: once soybeans are made into tofu, soy milk, and other products, they become more digestible and absorbable.) According to [a comparison by researchers at McGill University](https://www.menshealth.com.sg/health/dairy-vs-non-dairy-cow-milk-better-plant-based-milk-soy-almond-coconut-rice/), almond milk, coconut milk, and rice milk all have less protein than cow's milk; only soy milk comes close.

The plant milk camp's argument is: people don't need that much protein. "We — especially Westerners — consume excess protein in our diets, but lack fiber," Toni told QDaily. This view has had theoretical support before. Two American nutrition experts, Walter Willett and David Ludwig (both from Harvard University), published an article in *JAMA Pediatrics* arguing that for healthy adults whose diets already include enough vegetables, nuts, and protein, drinking milk confers no additional benefit.

"Which is healthier, plant milk or milk?" seems to have no definitive answer. Still, plant milk products have become a rising consumer trend. Corporate investment makes the so-called trend look more like the real thing. People accept mature consumer products and mature merchandising more readily than scientific conclusions.

Oatly's Chinese shareholder is China Resources. In 2016, Oatly received investment from China Resources Weilin Health Investment (the amount was not disclosed), a joint venture between China Resources and the Belgian private equity fund Verlinvest. China Resources quickly brought Oatly into its premium supermarket Ole', as well as more than 600 Pacific Coffee outlets in mainland China and Hong Kong.

![Califia Farms, a fast-rising US almond milk brand](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181029232637vbwiYOyDAIkKGBtS.jpg-w600)

Other big companies had made their moves earlier. General Mills invested twice in the almond milk brand Kite Hill; Coca-Cola acquired Zhonglv Culiangwang in China and invested in AdeS, the top-selling soy milk in Latin America; Danone acquired WhiteWave in 2017 for US$12.5 billion, whose most important brand assets — Silk (sold in China as "Zhipu Mofang") and So Delicious — are both plant-protein beverages.

PepsiCo has also spotted the oat milk market: its Quaker oats brand will launch an oat-based drink in the US next year. Vita Coco, the plant-based diet star company (and brand) that started out making coconut water, has also launched coconut milk. And there are fast-growing small brands like Oatly: Califia Farms, a plant-based beverage brand focused on almond milk, raised US$50 million led by investment bank Ambrosia Investments, and since 2014 it has been the plant milk brand with the highest compound annual growth rate. Good Karma Foods, a company that makes milk and yogurt from flaxseed, is growing sales at 50% this year.

### Plant Milk in China

That's how it is: plant milk builds its concept on a reinterpretation of animal milk nutrition.

"First, we're still going after milk consumers — we won't directly target soy milk consumers," Toni told QDaily. "Soy milk and soy drink are part of the Chinese tradition — why would we try to change that? But drinking milk is not a Chinese tradition. It was learned from the West."

Although the specific communication strategy is still being worked out, Oatly plans to position itself as "the new milk," telling stories along the lines of "we're a good company" and "make the world a better place" — the Swedish factory being carbon neutral, transparent institutions that can be trusted, oat milk's lower carbon emissions than milk, and so on. Though Toni also admits there is no direct evidence that Chinese consumers care about these things as purchase-decision factors. He added: "Although the absolute number of vegetarians in China is small, it's growing very fast."

As Oatly's tagline "Wow, No Cow" puts it, this is a plan to "strike back against milk."

If you count from 1988, when [the Dietary Guidelines for Chinese Residents first](http://www.chinanews.com/cj/2017/02-21/8155971.shtml) began recommending that residents drink milk, it took more than 20 years for milk to become an everyday drink for Chinese people. According to the Dairy Association of China, per-capita dairy consumption in China converts to only 36.3 kg of fresh milk — less than 100 grams a day on average — which is [considered](http://www.chinanews.com/cj/2017/02-21/8155971.shtml) to still have room to grow.

According to data Euromonitor International provided to QDaily, China's packaged milk market (fresh milk and UHT milk combined) has exceeded 122.2 billion yuan — ten times the packaged soy milk drink market. But it's worth noting that freshly made soy milk/soy drink reaches far wider restaurant channels than milk, from KFC and McDonald's to Yonghe King, and even full-service restaurants. Chinese people also have the habit of making soy milk at home. Soy milk machines were once hot sellers, carrying companies like Joyoung to IPO. None of these are captured in the statistics.

Packaged soy milk and soy drink will grow faster in the future. According to Euromonitor International, from 2014 to 2018, China's packaged soy milk drinks (including ready-to-drink soy milk) grew at compound annual rates of 9.3% in volume and 12% in value — far outpacing milk's 1.1% and 4.6%. Over the next five years, soy milk drinks will keep growing more than twice as fast as milk. This is not because of the plant-based diet movement, nor entirely because of the large lactose-intolerant population. It is because of the Chinese tradition of eating soy products; in addition, the 2008 melamine scandal gave soy milk and its surrounding industries (including soy milk machines) a chance to develop.

The Chinese market has no real concept of plant milk yet, even though people have been drinking plant-protein beverages for a while. Lulu almond milk drink, Weiwei soy milk, Yinlu peanut milk, and Coconut Palm coconut juice are all plant-protein brands that were nationally famous in the 1990s or earlier.

Due to aging products or their own management problems, these companies have been rather quiet in recent years.

Chengde Lulu's revenue began to decline in 2015. It fell 6.85% and 16% in 2016 and 2017 respectively. Weiwei Co. has long wanted to develop sidelines like dairy and baijiu, and its core business has shown almost no progress for over a decade — it's still spinning on soy milk powder with the "happy and cheerful" slogan. After Nestlé took control of Yinlu, performance stayed poor; [in 2017 Yinlu achieved growth in both revenue and profit](http://www.kanshangjie.com/article/146165-1.html), but that came from coffee and eight-treasure porridge products, not peanut milk. Coconut Palm coconut juice's output value in 2016 was 4.021 billion yuan, down [430 million yuan](http://money.163.com/17/0210/01/CCSJDDMU002580S6.html) from 2014.

![Doubendou (Photo/Dali Foods)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181030005558VxUSahBr4Zw1I6vN.jpg-w600)

Vitasoy may be one of the companies riding the trend well. It has gradually expanded from its home turf of Guangdong, Hong Kong, and Macau, and the south, toward the north. With the expansion of channels, Vitasoy's performance has grown markedly. Beyond retail, Vitasoy also shows up in restaurant channels. Vitasoy partnered with Manner Café to launch a soy milk latte.

For three consecutive fiscal years from FY13/14 to FY15/16, Vitasoy International's mainland revenue grew more than 25%; growth slowed somewhat to 17% in FY16/17, then surged 39% in the last fiscal year (FY17/18). This past May, it launched a premium line called "Health Plus," said to use micron-level grinding technology — one variety with added calcium, one with added fiber. In short, compared with the original Vitasoy, it's more "healthy," and the retail unit price has risen by about 3 yuan.

In China's plant-protein beverage market, there aren't yet many younger plant milk brands that fit the consumption-upgrade trend. Step into the refrigerated cases of a Lawson or FamilyMart in Shanghai, and the shelves reserved for yogurt and milk have around three to five rows. But plant-based drinks can't even fill one row. Some companies see the "consumption upgrade" gap in the plant milk market. Dali Foods launched "Doubendou" in 2017, and within less than two years it had captured 4.8% of the packaged ready-to-drink soy milk market, ranking second behind Vitasoy.

Oatly has now opened up a bit of new thinking for plant milk — turning cafés into its own promotion channel. In some cafés, Oatly is even placed at the cash register. This move may work better than selling boxed oat milk directly. Like a soy milk-based latte, it mixes in a sense of novelty without being as industrialized as off-the-shelf products.

Without coffee, Oatly has a faint cereal and nutty aroma, like the aftertaste left in your mouth after finishing a bowl of cereal.

That said, if you set aside the labels — "plant-based diet movement," "vegan," "low carbon emissions" — its benefit to ordinary people is probably just this: "Hmm... it doesn't seem to have that gamey smell of milk."
