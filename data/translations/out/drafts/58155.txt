Under Chinese law, smuggling covers everything from buying an iPhone in Hong Kong and failing to declare it and pay duties at customs to transporting drugs or arms across borders. Should we lump all of these together?

**#Debate is a new QDaily column that hopes to explore interesting, controversial issues. We know we stand on the shoulders of those who came before us.**

Is smuggling a crime?

In the eyes of the law, yes. The criminal codes of most countries in the world include smuggling. But if we appeal to people's moral intuition, smuggling does not seem to be an unambiguous evil in the way murder, arson, robbery, and rape are.

This split between law and morality explains the public's confusion over a recent case. In August 2018, the Guangdong Provincial High People's Court found that a 42-year-old woman who had traveled back and forth between the mainland and Hong Kong over the previous three years, buying goods on consignment (daigou) and selling more than 11.4 million yuan worth of clothing within China, had committed the crime of smuggling ordinary goods. She was sentenced to 10 years in prison and fined 5.5 million yuan. Many people were outraged that the punishment was too heavy, but others held that daigou is smuggling, and smuggling is simply wrong.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109134049IQvrZmGHTxwokB2d.JPG-w600)

The reason people disagree about how to understand smuggling morally is that many very different acts all get judged as smuggling. Under Chinese law, smuggling covers everything from buying an iPhone in Hong Kong and failing to declare it and pay duties at customs to transporting drugs or arms across borders—and the moral content of these two acts obviously differs enormously.

For this very reason, the provisions on smuggling in criminal law are usually relatively vague. They merely emphasize that smuggling is the act of moving goods and articles back and forth across a national border in violation of relevant laws and regulations. This usually means evading customs duties, or trafficking in goods prohibited by customs. So both China and the United States, when they need to determine whether an act constitutes smuggling, must draw on regulations and statutes outside the criminal code.

In Chinese law, the Customs Law sets out comparatively detailed provisions on smuggling. And for a charge such as smuggling precious metals, one must also consult the General Rules on Tariffs and the Measures for the Administration of Gold and Gold Products Imports and Exports, jointly issued by the General Administration of Customs and the central bank. Likewise, when the United States determines whether smuggling has occurred, it must consult congressional legislation on the goods in question and the rules of U.S. Customs and Border Protection.

In this sense, what constitutes smuggling depends entirely on the relevant policy. And historically, governments' decisions have shifted constantly. Things that could not previously be exported became exportable; goods that could be imported might suddenly be banned. The quotas of goods permitted for import or export rose and fell. Overnight, the picture could change completely—and America's Prohibition, which lasted less than 15 years, best illustrates how governments waver on this class of question.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109134320ho8FqcPC2lXHZgfm.jpg-w600)

In January 1919, with the dust of World War I barely settled and national leaders still negotiating in Paris's palaces over how to carve up the defeated powers and redistribute the world's interests, the United States passed the Eighteenth Amendment to the Constitution, declaring the production, transportation, and sale of any liquor illegal. The amendment took effect a year later, ushering the United States into the Prohibition era.

Alcohol is one of the most addictive beverages in the world, and Americans have never been able to resist it. In the colonial period, Americans drank liquor as a matter of habit—men, women, old, and young alike. After the Civil War, beer replaced spirits as Americans' favorite drink. In 1848, in a painting depicting the first American president's departure from office, a bottle of spirits sat on the table beside Washington. When Prohibition was abruptly implemented in 1920, demand for alcohol gave birth to a vast liquor-smuggling chain.

Some of those involved were former liquor merchants, for whom this was a means of survival they could not abandon. Others, drawn by the enormous profits of the illicit liquor trade, took the risk and joined in. What they did was exactly the same as what merchants in the liquor import and export trade had done before: ferrying liquor from around the world into the United States. Only, because of a constitutional amendment, what they were doing had become an illegal business.

"Ships laden with rum set out from the Bimini Islands, Belize, and Saint-Pierre, entered American ports under careful disguise, transferred their cargoes to motor speedboats, and carried them to safe coves. Trucks hid spirits smuggled across the Canadian border; whole carloads of whiskey were sometimes smuggled into the United States with cleverly refitted cap seals. These varied forms of smuggling enjoyed great success."

So wrote American historian Frederick Allen in his book *Only Yesterday*. Citing Department of Commerce data from the time, he estimated that in 1924 alone more than US$4 million worth of liquor entered the United States.

But the Prohibition era did not last long; Prohibition was repealed in 1933. The newly installed president, Franklin D. Roosevelt, first loosened restrictions on a small number of alcoholic beverages, after which the U.S. Congress passed the Twenty-first Amendment, repealing the Eighteenth—the Prohibition amendment. What had been called "smuggling" in the Prohibition era became legal once again.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109134336jQou0vckhWmYy5Kb.jpg-w600)

From legal, to illegal, and back to legal: in a mere 15 years, the character of ferrying liquor back and forth across the border changed dramatically—enough to show that so-called smuggling amounts to little more than a government's whim. Interestingly, the reasons the U.S. government used to establish and later repeal Prohibition are the very two arguments contemporary society uses to justify the existence of customs and tariffs.

The first is national security and morality. Throughout the 1910s, Prohibition advocates endowed alcohol with enormous moral significance. They believed people should remain sober, that drinking causes people to lose their reason, and that alcohol itself was an immoral product.

To ban liquor nationwide, you had to strike at the source. Beyond wiping out domestic liquor producers, you also had to guard against people secretly bringing liquor in from other countries—so ferrying liquor into the United States became an act of smuggling. Over the decade from 1920 to 1930, the number of U.S. Prohibition agents grew from just over 1,500 to just over 2,800—and even a force of that size was far too small for a country as vast as the United States.

In the contemporary world, the goods endowed with the same significance are drugs. Nineteenth-century opium, twentieth-century heroin, and the new synthetic drugs of the twenty-first century are all considered highly addictive and gravely harmful to health. To protect citizens and maintain social order, most countries ban the possession and circulation of these drugs. It appears that in the name of national security, governments have the right to ban particular goods, and smuggling emerges as a crime as a byproduct of such bans.

The problem with arguing the moral content of smuggling this way is that smuggling drugs is immoral because of the nature of drugs, not because of the act of smuggling itself. More importantly, as with American Prohibition, how one understands the relationship between drugs and national security also depends on each government's own choices.

Marijuana was once a drug that governments around the world would not touch, but in recent years a global fashion has taken hold: legalization. In 1996, marijuana was already fully legalized in California. By 2014, 20 U.S. states supported legal medical marijuana. In 2018, the Canadian government classified marijuana as a legal drug—and even a recreational product.

Canada has decided to go a step further. It is considering eliminating criminal penalties for drug possession, treating the proliferation of illegal drugs as a public health and social problem rather than a criminal one. Advocates cite the case of Portugal: in the 1990s, Portugal was a center of heroin addiction, with the highest rate of HIV infection caused by injecting drugs in the entire European Union. But today, deaths from drug overdose and HIV cases related to injecting drugs have fallen sharply in Portugal.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109134738GBSOFVf1TkZDpQdi.jpg-w600)

What happened to alcohol in the United States has its parallels. Led by Al Capone, the Chicago gangs controlled the liquor-smuggling business and used its profits to build a vast criminal empire. In addition, many scholars believe that the mass closure of American distilleries held back the U.S. economy in the 1920s.

Although by the time Prohibition was repealed in the 1930s most people agreed it had reduced alcohol consumption, whether its costs and benefits—for society as a whole—can be weighed with a simple yes or no is not an easy question. In this sense, tying national security to the justification for the existence of customs and tariffs is not a particularly sound argument.

When the U.S. government decided to repeal Prohibition in 1933, it may have weighed the social effects of the ban, but the most critical factor was fiscal. Historians' calculations show that before Prohibition, roughly 14 percent of government revenue came from liquor taxes, a share that even accounted for 30 to 40 percent of the budgets of some state and local governments. During the booming 1920s, the lost revenue was perhaps tolerable. But with the arrival of the Great Depression in 1929, the Roosevelt administration launched the New Deal and needed vast sums to fund infrastructure, making liquor taxes newly important—and ultimately prompting the U.S. government to repeal Prohibition through the legislative process.

Economic factors are the second reason governments need a customs and tariff system—and also the reason such systems first appeared.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109135147K6LupOb2IXck1Mz8.jpg-w600)

*World History of Customs and Tariffs* (《世界海关与关税史》), published in 2003, is perhaps the most systematic survey of the history of customs and tariffs. Its author, Hirotaka Asakura, believes that ancient civilizations such as Egypt and Mesopotamia already had customs-like functional departments, but their exact functions and organizational forms cannot be confirmed because so much time has passed. More reliable records come from ancient Greece.

Compared with the ancient civilizations of Egypt and Mesopotamia, Greece was coastal and mountainous, its agricultural civilization underdeveloped, its grain self-sufficiency limited; it depended heavily on trade with the entire Mediterranean region to obtain enough wheat. After the Greeks gradually developed and replaced the Phoenicians as the core of Mediterranean trade, the Phoenicians presented gifts to the Greeks, "hoping to ensure that trade between them and the Greeks would be protected and proceed smoothly."

Later, this tributary-like practice was replaced by relatively systematic import and export duties. The rate is generally believed to have been 2 percent, though records say it could be raised to 5 or even 20 percent depending on the situation. The Greeks appointed dedicated officials to oversee taxation, while collection was contracted out to tax farmers. Like any other tax, import and export duties seem to have served to increase Greek state revenue. The most direct evidence: when Athens became embroiled in the Peloponnesian War against Sparta, it raised the rate from the original 5 percent to 20 percent.

Once import and export duties were levied, they inevitably raised importers' costs, and naturally some importers tried to evade them—and this was the earliest smuggling. Hirotaka Asakura quotes a line from the ancient Greek philosopher Zeno: "In the city of Olophyx live the customs collectors and the smugglers—the misfortune of that city, and of its people."

If ancient Greece levied import and export duties to raise revenue, then the Ptolemaic dynasty, founded in Egypt in 305 BC, invented another use for them. Under the Ptolemies, producing olive oil was a royal prerogative and an important source of royal income. Yet the olive oil they produced was of lower quality than Greek and Syrian oil, while its price was very high. To protect their own interests from harm, they raised the import duty on olive oil to 50 percent, so that in nominal terms domestic olive oil was 25 percent cheaper than imported oil. The same tactic was applied to goods such as honey and wine.

"These are classic cases of the Ptolemaic dynasty's protective tariffs," Asakura writes. "There is no doubt that one function of this practice was to keep foreign goods out of the domestic market." This may also be the earliest case of trade protectionism.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109135317GxTd1W7pRw9IAXoz.jpg-w600)

To increase revenue and protect domestic industry—this is the chief reason rulers of later eras continually carried forward and developed the customs and tariff system. Medieval Europe was fragmented, and the warlords who ruled their own patches levied taxes on passing merchants, sometimes at rates above 50 percent. In 782, Tang dynasty officials, seeking to make up fiscal deficits, stationed themselves in major cities and ports and levied import duties of 2 to 10 percent on goods. During the Hundred Years' War between England and France in the 14th century, the English government raised export duties on wool to fund the war against France.

In the mid-17th century, France ran enormous trade deficits with the Netherlands, England, Italy, and other major countries, totaling nearly 10 million pounds sterling a year. In 1667 France launched a round of tariff reform, sharply raising duty rates, with duties on some goods increasing by more than 120 percent. France ultimately achieved a trade surplus by the early 18th century.

Even today it is the same. In March 2016, the United States and Hong Kong jointly broke a smuggling case. Large quantities of clothing from mainland China, nominally bound for Mexico, instead entered the United States and were sold to buyers across the country. Joseph Macias, a special agent with the Los Angeles office of Homeland Security Investigations, put it most bluntly: "Commercial smuggling is a multibillion-dollar global industry. It deprives governments of revenue and undermines our economy."

Economists, however, do not buy this argument. In the textbook Gregory Mankiw wrote for first-year economics students, he devotes an entire chapter to analyzing the effect of tariffs on trade using the simplest supply-and-demand theory. "From the standpoint of economic efficiency, the best policy is free trade without tariffs." This has also been the mainstream view of economics as a whole since Adam Smith.

From an economic standpoint, smuggling is also easier to understand. When people must pay high tariffs on goods, they naturally choose a cheaper way—transacting around the customs system—and this becomes smuggling in the legal sense.

Smuggling was not punished at first. According to the Border Force National Museum in Liverpool, England, the early English customs service merely collected duties at ports and did not take on the function of suppressing smuggling. In the 17th and 18th centuries, smuggling grew enormously; at times, smuggled goods entering England may have outnumbered goods that had paid their duties. It was not until 1661 that England enacted a statute making smuggling punishable by death. Thereafter, both smugglers and customs officers took up arms, and the two sides sometimes came into serious conflict.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109135429e3K54I8tb2zfapmB.jpg-w600)

This did not curb smuggling. British history textbooks write that many ordinary people considered smuggling harmless and took part in it. "Many people respected in society were involved—sometimes to make money, sometimes simply because they did not consider smuggling a crime at all."

And it is precisely because, in the eyes of many English people, smuggling carried no moral meaning at all that only economic means could curb it. "When the British prime minister William Pitt lowered tariffs in the 1780s, smuggling profits shrank. Further tariff abolition in the 19th century made the fairly open smuggling of the 18th century disappear entirely."

Citizens and governments will plainly keep playing this game against each other. From haitao to daigou, Chinese consumers have devised every imaginable method to avoid the high tariffs and consumption taxes China levies on imported goods—and turned them into a business. [A May 2017 report in The New York Times noted](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/40437.html) that in 2016 international students in Australia brought goods worth US$600 million back to China. [A report from Bain & Company](http://www.bain.cn/news_info.php?id=617) found that even though China's overseas daigou market shrank by one-third year over year in 2015, it was still worth 43 billion yuan.

If we go strictly by the definition in China's Customs Law, all of these 43 billion yuan count as illegal income. But setting aside the legal definition, the act of ferrying goods back and forth across a border carries no moral meaning in itself. As long as we accept that tariffs, as a form of taxation, are part of a government's economic policy, this question should leave room for discussion and room for amendment.
