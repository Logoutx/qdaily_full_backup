On March 8, 2018, 91-year-old Indian architect Balkrishna Doshi was awarded the Pritzker Architecture Prize.

Over a design career spanning more than sixty years, he carried out a remarkably diverse architectural practice in India, ranging from public buildings and public housing for low-income groups to private residences and art museums.

Doshi was born in 1927 in Pune, India, into a family that had worked in the furniture trade for two generations. In 1947 he enrolled at the Sir J.J. School of Architecture in Mumbai. That same year, India formally cast off colonial rule and, eager to modernize in every respect, began to look to the West for architectural design and urban planning.

Like other young people of his generation from India's middle and upper classes, Doshi went to England. There he met the French architect Le Corbusier, who would shape his architectural career and who had been invited to design a new city for India — Chandigarh. Several years later, Doshi returned to India to lead Le Corbusier's projects in Chandigarh and Ahmedabad. He later became a collaborator of another master of modern architecture, Louis Kahn. Even today, the influence of both masters can still be seen in Doshi's early buildings.

Le Corbusier's failure in the urban design of Chandigarh began to prompt Doshi to reflect on what architecture, streets, and cities would truly answer to India's own needs. Studying in the West, returning home to build, and then reflecting on local identity — this was a trajectory shared by many architects, Doshi among them. In the 1930s, the Japanese architect Kunio Maekawa brought the ideas of Le Corbusier and the Bauhaus back to Japan. In China in the 1980s, the Chinese-American architect I. M. Pei similarly wove the architectural vocabulary of Suzhou's classical gardens into his design of the Fragrant Hill Hotel in Beijing.

Of his localized practice, the Pritzker Prize praised him this way: "for his understanding of Indian architectural traditions, combining prefabrication with local craft to develop an architectural language in harmony with India's history, culture, and local traditions."

Notably, the Pritzker's judging criteria have stirred controversy within the industry in recent years. Toyo Ito of Japan (2013), Shigeru Ban (2014), and the South American architect Alejandro Aravena (2016) all ran practices with a strong social orientation — whether appearing on post-disaster reconstruction sites or designing housing for low-income groups. Some architects have bluntly criticized this judging criterion's emphasis on "social concern" for making the Pritzker increasingly "politically correct"; the most prominent [example](http://www.designmena.com/thoughts/schumacher-of-zha-questions-priorities-of-the-pritzker-prize) is Patrick Schumacher, Zaha Hadid's former business partner.

Wang Shu, the architect who this year joined the Pritzker Architecture Prize jury for the first time, [said in an interview](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MTc5MTU3NTYyMQ==&mid=2650650428&idx=1&sn=3fbb604e8891a60a97ecb572b5b60687&chksm=5affa6166d882f0025e34ea47c51891b9d666ae767b4cad3d25478eb69765376d755083492a5&mpshare=1&scene=1&srcid=0307rPX2YNsc8cknOVlcXm9e&key=d86b2ae03ec589d49133685a5770cc269db123c42485bcc063fcc8861ad35bd04467ac0e15a7bed32dd3de1c9ae2f0750d3e239c7af29885f28488fc049a9949a62e134875dd1db28bae97387f9ad862&ascene=0&uin=MTQzNTM1NTMwMQ%3D%3D&devicetype=iMac+MacBookPro12%2C1+OSX+OSX+10.10.4+build(14E46)&version=11020201&lang=zh_CN&pass_ticket=rnyo%2BODlP2lklwIhrZgMmk88DIFP38gk4CRM1gIGBn%2Fh2tN0sN6ETZUCW%2Bj%2FF7Bu) that this is a shift in architecture itself, as people increasingly value diversity and difference across regions and cultural backgrounds.

### Chandigarh, a "Failure" of New-City Planning

In 1950, Doshi — then in London — won the chance to take part in the Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne (CIAM), where the very topic under discussion was the new city of Chandigarh that Le Corbusier was planning for India. Doshi was the only Indian at the meeting. Although people warned him that "Le Corbusier is very difficult to work with," he still joined the Chandigarh project.

This was the meeting of two "newcomers." The 23-year-old Doshi did not know Le Corbusier's architecture and did not even speak French; he often watched curiously as this energetic designer, working like a monk, shut himself in an office 226 centimeters long and wide, drawing, thinking, and writing. Their earliest exchanges were like acting in a silent film. Chandigarh was Le Corbusier's first project in India.

![A hand-drawn sketch by Le Corbusier (Image / Pinterest)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018030804041736HKnMeuUrdW5EBb.jpg-w600)

Doshi recalls a hand-drawn diagram that Le Corbusier once gave to a friend, featuring three characters: Don Quixote, the Trojan Horse, and a donkey. One needs Don Quixote's fighting spirit, an endless struggle against darkness and negativity; for that, one must exhaust every stratagem, even designing a Trojan Horse if need be; and all of it requires unceasing work, like a donkey that never stops turning the millstone.

Le Corbusier once wrote a poem titled "The Acrobatic": "Not a marionette / throwing himself into the acrobatic act / phenomena arise all around / he does everything he can to complete / each difficult feat... Result: not a hair out of place! He does what others cannot." The poem is also read as this master architect and technological optimist's self-portrait.

For the Mill Owners' Association Building in Ahmedabad, India, Le Corbusier designed an exceptionally spacious interior, yet paired it with toilet doors only 70 centimeters wide. Confronted with the client's doubts, Le Corbusier wrote back: "Gentlemen, you will find that a pregnant woman carrying two suitcases can easily pass through a train-carriage aisle no more than 70 centimeters wide, so I am quite certain that, however stout you may be, you can get through this door."

But not all clients were so "docile." He once met a difficult client in Paris who asked him to change the kitchen's height. In Doshi's memory, the master was furious: "I just told them: in 25 years of work I have never changed a thing. I would rather not build this at all — goodbye!" And when Doshi pressed him about what to do next, Le Corbusier said, "One thing I have learned from life: take revenge on failure through double the effort."

In 1950, this confidence met an India that had only recently become independent. After the partition of India and Pakistan, Punjab was split in two by the border between them, and the original provincial capital fell on the Pakistani side. India urgently needed to build a modern new capital for Punjab — one that would announce to the world the arrival of an independent new India. Prime Minister Nehru marked out a tract of land 270 kilometers from New Delhi, at the foot of the Himalayas. "Chandi" is the name of the principal deity of the temple at the mountain's foot — a goddess of power — and "garh" means "fortress."

![Le Corbusier (Image / Pinterest)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180308041207f04QEBvRzXZlLpPN.jpg-w600)

Nehru had originally found an American engineer to design the new city, but the engineer withdrew from the project after his partner died in a plane crash. Soon after, Le Corbusier received the invitation. From leaders to designers to ordinary people, all rushed eagerly toward an unknown destination called "modernization."

In his plan, the northeastern corner of the city held a government plaza of enormous scale, ringed by the parliament building, the courts, the secretariat, and the governor's palace. People had to walk across the plaza and its lawns to reach the different buildings — hardly a comfortable scale in India's scorching heat.

Le Corbusier designed a multi-level traffic network, with the city laid out around streets for fast, medium, and slow traffic. In a 1986 interview, Doshi said this was not a design that belonged to India — that India "has not yet developed to the level of a traffic-dominated civilization." "I think that within twenty years from now (1986), Chandigarh will not even be regarded as an Indian city. What we experience here is Le Corbusier's vision of future living, not Indian life."

For Doshi, another major reason the new city's planning failed was a government that gazed up at Western modernization with boundless admiration, showed blind deference to designers from developed countries, and had yet to grasp the importance of its own soil. He argued that traditional Indian society was organized around groups and communities, with frequent social activity, and that people needed more public spaces shaded by trees for outdoor life. But in Chandigarh, this traditional Indian mode of socializing was never seriously considered. Though the city had streets, broad open areas, and large residential districts, "there is no life here!"

### A Long Localization Practice

In 1954, Doshi "graduated" from Le Corbusier's studio. In 2014, sixty years after that "graduation," the National Gallery of Modern Art in India held a retrospective for him. He described it as a sixty-year-long journey of constantly wandering and questioning himself: what does architecture have to do with society — and in particular, with an independent India?

This farewell carried, in Doshi's memory, the undertone of India's national independence. "At the time, people once doubted that, although the country was independent, Indians could not shake off the servility instilled by colonialism." He privately vowed not to simply go on using Le Corbusier's architectural elements or forms. "He did not want me to merely imitate or endlessly repeat building after building; he wanted me to invent and pursue new forms of expression." Doshi even took a photograph of Le Corbusier with him and hung it on the wall as a reminder for self-reflection.

What Doshi returned to was still that India hungry for modernization, and he received a large volume of commissions from public departments at the central and local levels. The Institute of Indology building, completed in 1966, was his earliest public building. To preserve Jainism's precious manuscripts and paintings, Doshi designed a semi-underground structure whose entire scheme was concentrated in a reinforced-concrete "great ship." For this project, Doshi drew on the Haveli, a traditional form of South Asian residence.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180308042148CahrSKG7jeAPx9W3.jpg-w600)

![Institute of Indology (Photo / Vinay Panjwani)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803080422331Wa2pXh7kC3Fjs9b.jpg-w600)

For CEPT University — its School of Architecture and Centre for Environmental Planning and Technology — Doshi designed a "campus without gates": brick-kiln structures raised on pilotis, the entire campus without walls, where ordinary residents could also enter and take part in activities. Notably, Doshi was the founder of this school. Beyond the traditional curriculum, they set up dedicated research programs on India's environment, cities, and culture. This also laid the groundwork for the later work of Doshi's firm Vastu Shilpa.

In 1980, Doshi designed a studio for his own practice, "Sangath," one of his best-known works internationally. Referencing the base and structure of traditional Indian temples, several long barrel-vaulted buildings enclose an open-air courtyard. Inside the vaulted buildings, the work areas are sunk half a level into the ground. Broken ceramic tiles decorate the roofs and walls, reflecting sunlight to reduce heat absorption. The vaults also collect rainwater and channel it into the interior courtyard to cool the building; the flowing water forms a distinctive landscape.

"We don't need high-powered air conditioning; we can comfortably get through Indian heat waves that regularly top 50°C." Khushnu Hoof is Doshi's granddaughter. In 2017, she curated a retrospective of Doshi's architectural career, exhibited at the Power Station of Art in Shanghai.

"He never repeated a design; every project was an experiment," Hoof says of her grandfather. The Gufa cave gallery (Amdavad ni Gufa) is another example. Unlike his public projects, this gallery came from a private commission. Today it looks something like a community activity center, composed of several interlocking spheres. Doshi's inspiration came from the circular and semi-circular designs inside the Ajanta and Ellora caves of ancient Indian Buddhist art; the shapes also resemble the domed shrine of Shiva in the Hindu tradition. The gallery is a collaboration between Doshi and the Indian painter Maqbool Fida Husain. Husain painted murals in this underground cave, with light streaming through porthole windows into a dim space.

To build this underground gallery, Doshi used computer modeling in 1994, but no local construction crew was willing to take it on; he could only seek out tribal craftsmen who built it by hand, "pinching" it into shape the way one builds a thatched hut.

After the cave gallery succeeded, the client was so satisfied with the design that "it seems to have become a project that will never end," Hoof says — until recently they still received "orders" from this old client, among them designing the landscape and new art spaces in the area around the cave gallery. Her challenge, she says, is how to unify new construction with the existing design while making the smallest possible changes.

### Designing Housing for the Low-Income

Doshi's self-account at his 2014 retrospective was strikingly poetic, titled *Give Time a Break* ([《让时间停歇》](https://architexturez.net/doc/az-cf-167936)). He did not talk about his own designs but began with several traditional Indian temples. Religion is not some lofty fiction; in India, religion means day-to-day life and real space.

He describes an Islamic complex built in 1466 — a summer palace, a mosque, and several tombs arranged around a water reservoir — which has become the most frequently visited public space in the whole city, whether for worship, prayer, or simply to cool off. It is not fenced off as a museum; on the steps by the water, nearby residents go about their daily routines of bathing and washing clothes. Some undefined areas become flexible venues for spontaneous activities such as dancing. Its physical distance from the residential quarters is short, and so is the psychological and social distance.

Building housing for low-income groups is an important thread in Doshi's projects. Aranya Low Cost Housing was a project he fought for himself in 1982. Hoof tells us that Doshi was initially invited to design an upscale neighborhood near the city of Indore; while surveying the site, he noticed an open plot of land across the way and was told it was public land reserved for a government housing project for the "Economically Weaker Sections" (EWS), where people living in slums would eventually move.

"Many people have very romantic ideas about slums." Hoof now works at the Vastu Shilpa Foundation, which conducts research on India's slums every year. The "International Habitat Studio" is made up of half Indian students and half overseas students; they select pilot sites to observe, research, or transform.

In recent years, architects and social scientists have been surprised to find that a slum can become a self-circulating miniature city. "How people use limited money and materials to create physical 'buildings' and lives — that is creativity worthy of admiration." But Hoof also points out that many slums, which provide a foothold for outsiders, offer no sense of security: no land ownership, no basic electricity or sewage facilities, dire sanitation, the spread of disease, even gang power.

![Aranya Low Cost Housing gradually grew into a small town (Photo / John Paniker)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180308045358flv8MrJXHtj6D35b.jpg-w600)

Doshi's Aranya consists of simply structured houses. Each household was given a plot of 30 square meters, with a foundation built of bricks, a toilet, drainage, and electricity.

To prove the design workable, the government first had Doshi build 60 model homes as a pilot. Among these model homes, a central axis forms the main commercial district, flanked by six relatively self-contained blocks, each with its own infrastructure. At the Power Station of Art exhibition, an intricate drawing showed what a mature block looks like: not-so-wide inner streets with houses facing each other, plants growing in the gaps between houses, bicycles shuttling along the inner streets, while cars are confined to the commercial district along the central axis; each block has its own cluster of vendors. Roofs can be turned into gardens, used to dry laundry, or extended further upward.

Doshi calls this a design of "trading time for space," not accomplished in one step. The Indian government-affiliated development bank provided low-interest loans to low-income buyers, who could choose amenities according to their ability to repay. The most basic option was a small hut with a plinth (plith) for future expansion. Optional items included water taps, rainwater-harvesting systems, sewers, streetlights, and so on.

People built their houses in Aranya in stages, starting with structures of the cheapest materials, reinforcing them with brick and cement, and gradually extending them, until the temporary shelter became a more solid building fit for long-term living. The retrospective presented the project's intention the way Lego bricks do: simple structures that can keep growing.

![A model of Aranya that can be freely "built" (Photo / Power Station of Art)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180308045608qSV2mR4jbvy1D6T7.jpg-w600)

"But as we found in our slum research, improving their living conditions is not just a matter of moving them into a new place," Hoof says. "Many government public-housing programs think that is enough, but in the new housing people are cut off from their existing social networks, and there is no new public space where they can interact with their new neighbors."

Doshi envisioned traditional street life. Following the original topography, he designed non-linear streets where people could spontaneously occupy street spaces of varying widths and occasional turns, while motor vehicles were kept to the commercial district along the central axis. Formal commercial storefronts sit close to the main roads, while informal street vendors can do business on the narrow inner streets and in the open areas, keeping them apart from traffic.

By 1988, the small town of Aranya had gradually taken shape. McGill University in Canada once collaborated with Doshi's firm Vastu Shilpa to revisit Aranya. Their criteria for measuring the success of this low-cost housing project included the sturdiness of the houses, the degree to which people used the open spaces, and whether the design fit the residents' way of life.

In Aranya's small — even miniature — public spaces, they found temples and resting platforms, and people's everyday celebrations take place there too. But there were regrets: some large-scale open spaces sat idle, and some were even used to dump garbage.

![Model of the Life Insurance Corporation of India mixed-income housing complex (Photo / Power Station of Art)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180308045709QVAjdpDE8sqNUT1y.jpg-w600)

Many of Doshi's projects came from the government, and like their designer counterparts in China, they too faced all kinds of changes from their clients — such as a change of administration. "If you are unlucky, the entire project gets shelved," Hoof tells us. "But sometimes you have to thank the client for changing, because it allowed Doshi to fix problems discovered in the previous round of plans that had gone unresolved."

Doshi's firm Vastu Shilpa still designs for low-income groups. Hoof took part in reconstruction projects in some of India's earthquake-stricken areas in 2016; she remembers sitting in circles with people, nearly "drowned" by their chatter. They did not understand architecture or the meaning of professional jargon. In the end, Hoof and her colleagues pulled out paper and drew their needs: who was whose neighbor, what facilities needed restoring or adding. She was instantly reminded of the visitors she encountered as a child in her grandfather's studio — neighbors often dropped by to ask Doshi for "small bits of advice" on construction, and they would talk for half a day. Today, it is still the same kind of challenge: how to communicate with ordinary people, "because you need to use the simplest language, not arm yourself with jargon — and of course, before that, you have to figure out what it is you really want to do."
