Hong Kong has an independent bookstore called "MOSSES" — the name means moss, and its location is moss-like too.

The name sounds at first like Moses, and it takes a bit of the biblical Moses's nerve in parting the Red Sea to actually find the shop. On a damp, drizzly day, you turn off Queen's Road Central in Wan Chai into the Sun, Moon and Star Streets area, pass St. Francis Mansion at 14 St. Francis Street, round one more corner before you spot No. 15 tucked into the bend, then head down the steps to declare "touchdown." The bookstore has no street number; the only sign is "MOSSES" painted in white on an orange wall — just like moss, which slips you up if you're not careful, and which you miss if you're not paying attention.

MOSSES was founded by book designer Hu Zhuobin (known as Zhuo) and illustrator Huang Sizhe (known as Zhe). The idea is the same as moss: though tiny and invisible, it can wedge itself into any crack and find a space to grow.

The space is only a hundred-odd, two-hundred-odd square feet. Nothing in the shop is categorized; it's gloriously messy, and customers are meant to hunt for treasure. "This shop is our own taste," Zhuo says. "The space is already this small, so we just put in the things we love most." Among Hong Kong's many independent bookstores, MOSSES is the only one that specializes in expensive books. To the two of them, books are artworks, and the bookstore shapes and merges with the city's aesthetic.

The shop specializes in photobooks, magazines, and hard-to-find books. At first the pair gathered them on their travels from around the world; having bought too many, they would lament that "there's no reason some of these books shouldn't exist in Hong Kong." Over time they turned to running a bookstore, buying three to four hundred books from more than fifty countries, ordering directly from the makers. Many are limited editions, others are consigned by photographers or recommended by fellow independent bookstores — introductions begetting introductions — and so the network slowly took shape.

"I can say without exaggeration: nobody else in Hong Kong has these books." Every title is hand-picked, and each book's provenance has become a delicious story — Zhuo has become the raconteur of books. Take *Sabat*, a witchcraft magazine published in the UK: its first three issues explore the desires of girls, mothers, and old women. "Witchcraft was originally a product of feminism, because most religions in the world don't want women to voice their desires." Or *Shukyu Magazine*, a thematic football publication: the *Identity* issue profiles the strength of national teams that never signed with FIFA, and one article even bears the title *Ten Ways to Get Hurt* — guaranteed to make football fans roar with laughter.

*Science of the Secondary*, published by the Singapore design team Atelier HOKO, has a bright red apple printed on the cover and probes what an apple is, the structure of the hand and its relationship to the apple, how to bite an apple, the various crunching sounds of eating… and the series continues. Hong Kong writer Li Meiqi's new book *Midnight Therapy* 《夜半治疗》 sells for 400 Hong Kong dollars; only just over 200 copies were made, and more than 20 sold at the launch. The most popular, best-selling item is Greg Girard's photobook *HK:PM*, priced at HK$350, which captures Wan Chai's old streetscapes.

The most expensive photobook sells for a staggering 25,000. *Rabbits Abandon their Children* exists in just over seventy copies worldwide, and "the resale prices have gone crazy." It looks at first glance like a children's book: the first half photographs the green hills and clear waters of Japan's "Rabbit Island," then old historical photographs are gradually interleaved, and only at the end is the truth revealed. "During World War II there was a poison-gas plant there — the Chukai Manufacturing Works — which killed huge numbers of Chinese at the time. Using surviving photographs and interviews with former Japanese military personnel still alive, this book is a book of repentance, saying this history must not be forgotten, because the Japanese government no longer mentions it." They love it so much they don't want to sell it, so they priced it to scare people off.

He steps up onto a chair and, from the highest shelf above the cash register, reverently takes down a not-for-sale copy: Junpei Ueda's *Picture of My Life*, the story of a young Japanese man who travels abroad, only for his depression-afflicted parents to take their own lives as a result; after sorting through his parents' belongings, he reviews his memories from within despair and gradually heals and lets go. It is the author's own experience, published once a year, and this book too was printed in just over 250 copies.

Look up, and the shop is looping interview footage from 1985 of their favorite author, Marguerite Duras, on the ceiling, saying: "I believe people will drown in a flood of information" and "There are no more people who read seriously."

The 2008 anti–Express Rail Link movement in Hong Kong was what brought the two together. Both studied design and had been classmates, and they grew close talking about the books they loved. Huang Sizhe remembers a reading-sharing session that invited Zhuo: everyone was chatting away easily about the joy of reading, and the moment he played this short film he had edited himself, the atmosphere turned ice-cold.

Through organizing publishing events, Zhuo got to know a circle of independent publishers and put on several exhibitions, but found they weren't sustainable. In 2013 he opened his first shop in the Wontonmeen guesthouse — a space so small it had only one wall, room for a single bookshelf. Then came Book B, located in Kowloon, which moved several times. Finally in 2016, when the gallery café that had occupied the space went out of business, they rented it and opened MOSSES — the first physical bookstore. It has taken five years to get here.

The St. Francis Street neighborhood is quiet. They chose Hong Kong Island because Hong Kong Islanders don't cross the harbor — yet there are plenty of potential customers. Half the customers are now foreigners and half locals, and you can tell the "aura is strong": art directors, designers, photographers, artists… Zhuo recounts some of the odd customers he has met. One came in, listened to music for a while, then asked him: "What kind of book can I get for five hundred dollars?" Zhuo talks while trying to flip through the art books that customer ended up buying. "In the old days you could copy a vinyl record with latex glue: spread white latex on the record, let it dry, peel the whole thing off, and you could play it once or twice. Whatever record the artist liked, he'd copy with latex, and then make artwork on top of it." The customer took one look, found it delightful, and took it home.

A physical bookstore is really a place of exchange, a place where books and people meet, and it should shoulder the responsibility of getting people back into reading. "If you're going to read the classics, you'll read them. But sometimes you haven't read for so long that you can't get back into a reading state. This shop has so many photobooks precisely because I feel that once you've seriously and attentively looked through one, you can get back into that state. Read here as long as you like, then go on to read other books."

"Some people say our books are so expensive that we look down on the poor. But we open our doors and let anyone read; the prices themselves are simply back at a reasonable level. If a book took the writer years to write, with good design and good production, and you think its price should be lower than a bowl of ramen that costs a hundred-odd dollars — I can't accept that."

Zhuo actually wants to do much more. He once worked for Joint Publishing and quit after three months; for years he has designed book covers for Kubrick, and won awards at the HKDA Global Design Awards for book designs including *The Necessary Silence* 《必要的静默》 and *Slow Wind Street* 《慢风街》. He founded the design studio "Edited."

What drew him into design in the first place was Wong Bik-wan. As a child he loved reading Wong Bik-wan — *Thereafter* 《其后》, for instance — and recommended her to his middle-school classmates. "Nobody was interested. Maybe I pitched it badly, so I blamed it on the cover not being pretty." He bursts out laughing, adding that Wong Bik-wan's covers are all drawn by the author herself. "Of course I'd want her to ask me to design for her, but unfortunately that won't happen."

When he got into book design five years ago, he didn't hesitate to go door-knocking at publishers in person to find work, but at first he earned only HK$6,000 a month — below the minimum wage.

Zhe adds that when he first knew him, Zhuo was much quieter — very different from the eloquent designer sitting before us now. Mainstream bookstores do approach Zhuo for collaboration these days; he admits he charges a lot, so they rarely work together. As if ground down by survival, Zhuo also says that the communication skills needed to convey design ideas to clients are part of the craft too.

The other bookstore is named Book B to remind people that Book A exists. Mainstream bookstores have shaped a book market clustered around the hundred-dollar price point. With the publishing market shrinking and mainstream publishers lacking influence, their books are produced from a canned template. He finds it a pity: many of the talented people around him, good at illustration and writing, end up writing screenplays — doing something else entirely — because publishers can't bring themselves to take a chance on them.

Even a thoroughbred horse needs to meet its Bole; what he wants most is for writers to have a choice.

Huang Sizhe pulls out a well-thumbed copy of the Duras novel *The Little Horses of Tarquinia*. He spent two years on a working holiday in Ireland and carried this book with him the whole time. Caught in a complicated relationship, he would open the novel and seem to find guidance for his life — though "something unfortunate would always happen." That year in late winter, he made a special trip to Montparnasse Cemetery in Paris and spent three days sketching with a red pencil. Like saving the strawberry on a cake for last, he went to Duras's grave last, to converse with Duras — "talking to myself." Some gravestones have train tickets placed on them; on Duras's grave sits a pen holder, and he dropped that short stub of red pencil into it.

Those three days of drawings became the illustration collection *Where She Sleeps*, designed by none other than Zhuo, who chose the typeface from the gravestone and printed it in intaglio. "Making books with him is like being in a band," Zhe pauses. "I don't think mainstream bookstores could ever do that."

"People often assume the soul of a book is something I give it. It isn't — the soul of a book is written by the author. Without a body, the soul can never be seen. So the designer must make a good body to hold the soul. Good design is when you feel it simply had to be this way."

*Decade Novels: Remembering Hong Kong*, designed by Zhuo, gathers seven writers — including Chen Hui, Wang Lianghe, Huang Renkui, and Han Lizhu — each writing stories beginning from the 1950s onward. On the cover, each writer's name is paired with bands of deep shadow shifting from white to gray, representing the times gradually darkening. "I often hear people say that all the good things about Hong Kong are gone." The title on the spine is deliberately printed in gold, its strokes flickering in and out of sight; he tuned the palette and typeface to imitate each era's printing technology — the older the era, the bigger and bolder the type.

"As a kid I'd wonder: why hasn't a Hong Kong literary writer like Wong Bik-wan won the Nobel Prize in Literature?" Zhuo has visited book fairs around the world, including Tokyo Art Book Fair 2017, and has never once seen a Hong Kong publisher. He often chats with foreign publishers, and they always ask what books Hong Kong has — further proof that mainstream bookstores do zero promotion of Hong Kong books. Hong Kong publishers mostly won't invest resources in new writers, in making covers more distinctive, in attracting people to buy.

"I feel I've run out of road. As a cover designer my influence is tiny; books hardly reach foreign shores, and others have no inclination to learn about Hong Kong's literary writers — that in itself is a problem." As for the work of literary giants like Haruki Murakami and Bohumil Hrabal, the traditional Chinese editions are all translated via Taiwan.

Hong Kong was once jokingly called a cultural desert; the most-borrowed books in its libraries are mostly travel guides — a city that doesn't read. Zhuo runs through the grievances: today's Hong Kong Book Fair draws a million visits, yet what it sells is the same as what's in storefronts — no vision, none of the element of an exhibition; publishers only want to sell books, and every advertisement is a spring sale, an autumn sale, a winter sale. "After thirty or forty years of this, who buys books at full price? Hong Kong publishing used to be formidable — Joint Publishing's books on Ming-style furniture once sold in hundreds of editions worldwide. Publishing a book really was like printing money, and they amassed huge resources — but did they ever support local writers and publishers?"

He believes that for why the publishing market has declined, the big publishers must take responsibility — and must also examine themselves. In conversation the two mention the "Three Zhong-Shang," a collective label for Joint Publishing, Chung Hwa Book Company, and The Commercial Press. Owned by Guangdong New Culture Enterprise Development Co., Ltd., which is wholly held by the Liaison Office of the Central People's Government in Hong Kong, it is the largest bookstore distribution network by market share, with 52 outlets in total — one-stop publishing, distribution, printing, and retail — monopolizing 80% of the publishing market by store count and revenue.

By some counts, Hong Kong's independent bookstores number 67 in total. When the large English-language bookstore Page One folded in 2016, many independent bookstores quietly and rapidly vanished. Hong Kong's Census and Statistics Department doesn't count bookstores as a separate category, but retailers of books, newspapers, and stationery fell from 1,540 in 2014 to 1,240 in 2017.

Should the niche go boutique? "I think the mass market is gone now. We're always branded as non-mainstream, but lately I've started changing my tune, because a print run these days is only 800 to 1,000 copies — I print 500 to 750, a difference of a hundred-odd copies, but I can sell them all." He dares say that if a mainstream bookstore's writer came to him, it might sell not just two or three thousand copies — maybe ten thousand, in bilingual or translated editions, sold around the world.

In the future the two will open a shop in the Nan Fung Mills, a revitalized heritage textile site in Tsuen Wan, running publishing workshops that teach ordinary people how to proofread, edit, do basic design, choose materials, and promote a book after publication… Their goal is to provide more publishing resources, and MOSSES also hosts publishing and music events. The proofreading profession is aging and fading, with no one entering the trade, its priorities more on catching typos than on textual verification. He wants to revive the industry, doing whatever he can however small the step, hoping to lead others to go one step further too.

The humanities bookstore Hong Kong Reader has stood for ten years; its owner once said that reading is a luxury in Hong Kong. Hong Kong's space is an even greater luxury — yet Zhuo has just renewed the lease for four more years.

"Since I started doing independent publishing events, the money I've poured in is beyond counting. I haven't earned a single cent — it's impossible to earn anything — but the most important thing is—" he sighs, "the losses cut deep." He subsidizes it with a monthly five-figure income from brand design. "The point isn't profit or loss. Many people think you open a shop to do business, but for me, beyond business, if my life's calling is to make books, then I must run this bookstore."

"I want a bookstore that represents this city." He travels to visit independent bookstores in various countries, goes book hunting, and takes inspiration from interesting books. A bookstore is, to a degree, like a shopfront for a city. When foreign designers, experts, and professors come to Hong Kong looking for distinctive publications that cross language barriers, he believes MOSSES is no worse than bookstores abroad.

True to its name — stealing into the night with the wind, moistening all things in silence — this bookstore has become an oasis in the cultural desert amid the melancholy, humid weather, persisting in one quiet corner.

Duras is still playing on the ceiling, saying: "People no longer travel. When the world can be circled in eight to fifteen days, there's no longer any need to travel — why? Travel takes the time of travel; it is not rushing through the sights, but living and seeing at the same time. To live by way of travel is no longer possible… In any case, a stretch of sea remains. Those oceans. And reading. People will rediscover it. A person, one day, will read again. And then everything begins once more."
