Judged on its own terms, the Paramount Decree has indeed become outdated; if the re-evaluation can bring new forms of monopoly and new threats into consideration, it may well be a good thing for Hollywood.

A landmark ruling is about to be re-evaluated because times have changed.

The Paramount Decree handed down 70 years ago may be one of the most consequential events in film history. Most people working in film today cannot help but live under the rules it established — it created the very concepts of commercial cinema and independent film; helped genres like horror and gore shake off the B-movie label; gave rise to the art-house cinema; made room for large chains like AMC, Regal, and Cinemark to survive; broke up the studio system; and laid the foundation for today's Hollywood media-empire model.

The Paramount Decree itself was not complicated. It stipulated that film studios could not own or control theater circuits, and it banned a series of studio practices that suppressed independent exhibitors. That such a simple set of rules could have an enormous impact was only because it shattered Hollywood's monopoly over the entire film industry.

In August 2018, the U.S. Department of Justice announced it would re-evaluate the Paramount Decree. [According to the Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/atr/paramount-decree-review), the film industry has changed in many ways over the 70 years since the decree: none of the companies named in the Paramount case still owns a significant number of theaters; most large cities now have more than one cinema; the situation in the 1930s and 1940s, when a theater had only a single screen and could show only a particular film for a given stretch of time, has given way to multiplexes where multiple screens can show different films at once; and theaters are no longer the only channel through which audiences watch movies — after a film's theatrical run, viewers can also watch it on television, DVD, and the internet.

"Based on these many changes, and the Paramount Decree having been in existence for 70 years, the Department of Justice has decided to institute a review of the Paramount Decree to determine whether it should be modified or terminated."

The Department of Justice's decision sparked heated debate. Judging from the many public comment letters, the worry was that if the Paramount Decree were abolished, studio dominance over theater circuits and a monopolistic landscape would re-emerge.

That said, the Department of Justice is not wrong about how the landscape has shifted, and it has also mentioned that modifying the decree is one possibility. From the decree's own vantage point, it does look somewhat behind the times, because new, unconstrained forms of monopolistic momentum have emerged — such as synergy-driven consolidation and internet streaming, the latter of which, while the DOJ sees it as broadening the audience for films, also carries potential monopolistic threats.

If the Department of Justice can take the new forms of monopoly in this reshaped landscape into account during the re-evaluation and make corresponding modifications, that may well turn out to be a good thing for Hollywood.

### How the Paramount Decree Came to Be, and the Effects of Antitrust

Go back 70 years, and Hollywood was enjoying a golden age built on monopoly. The form of monopoly it practiced was called vertical integration — control of every link in an entire industry chain. For the film business, that meant production, distribution, and exhibition.

Paramount Pictures was the first to achieve vertical integration: by acquiring theater circuits, the studio came to control every stage of a film's life. The other studios followed suit, and Hollywood settled into a structure of the "Big Five" (Paramount, Warner Bros., Loew's/MGM, 20th Century Fox, and RKO) and the "Little Three" (Universal, Columbia, and United Artists). The Little Three owned no theaters of their own, but by cooperating with the Big Five they kept newcomers out of the film business. The studio system that defined Hollywood's structure and film style thus took shape.

![Image: [Wiki Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Paramount_Theater._Omaha_-_NARA_-_283720.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181227123610GemSjfoQcdnRY8zB.jpg-w600)

This put the studios in an overwhelmingly strong position when negotiating with independent theaters. A model called block booking emerged — if a circuit wanted to screen films audiences were eager to see, it also had to screen other bundled titles. Studios could thus sell off low-budget pictures packaged together with star-studded films to theater chains. In other words, theaters could not choose for themselves what to show, and the studios could never lose money.

Although vertically integrated companies accounted for only a small fraction of the country's 15,000 theaters — in the mid-1920s Paramount owned 500 and MGM a mere 200 — the major studio-controlled chains included many large first-run houses, enough to generate handsome profits. By the late 1920s, three-quarters of box-office revenue came from these big theaters. Cinemas in small towns or on farms had to wait for films after their first runs.

Just as Hollywood's vertical integration reached its peak, the Department of Justice — which had been watching Hollywood's every move and remained ever vigilant against monopoly — filed the "Paramount" suit in 1938, charging that the Big Five and the Little Three had conspired to monopolize the film industry in violation of antitrust law.

After a series of trials, the Supreme Court handed down its final decision in 1948, ruling that both the Big Five and the Little Three had engaged in monopolistic conduct. The Big Five held a monopoly through their theater ownership; the Little Three were charged with shutting other companies out of the market through their cooperation with the Big Five.

The court ordered the studios to divest their theater circuits and banned block booking along with other practices harmful to independent exhibitors, such as setting minimum ticket prices. The studios complied: the Big Five kept their production and distribution companies and sold off their theaters.

The Paramount Decree stimulated Hollywood's creativity. The end of block booking allowed exhibitors to freely fill their open screening slots with independent films, and the result was that independent films improved in production cost, output, and creative ambition.

According to David Bordwell's *Film History: An Introduction*, between 1946 and 1956 the annual output of independent films more than doubled, reaching 150 pictures. United Artists, one of the Little Three, was a company that distributed only independent films, and it alone released as many as 50 independent films a year. By comparison, A24 — which in recent years has released many critically acclaimed titles — put out only 15 films in 2017.

The producer-centric system and the star system of the studio era gave producers and stars enormous say. As the Paramount Decree and the rise of postwar television pushed the studio system toward its end, film directors gained greater power. Young filmmakers of the era — George Lucas, Martin Scorsese, Steven Spielberg, and others — seized the opportunity and made landmark works such as *Star Wars*, *Taxi Driver*, and *Jaws*.

### What Are the New Monopolistic Threats? Synergy-Driven Consolidation

Yet capital always tends toward monopoly. The profit-seeking nature of companies keeps them searching for opportunities to maximize returns. Clearly, even after the U.S. Supreme Court put an end to vertical integration, Hollywood would not give up on monopoly — and this time the form it took was synergy.

In 2018, 41 years after the first *Star Wars* was released, George Lucas topped the Forbes list of America's wealthiest celebrities, with a net worth of $5.4 billion — well above second-place Steven Spielberg's $3.7 billion. On films alone, however, Spielberg comes out well ahead: [he is the first director in history whose box-office grosses surpassed $10 billion](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52244.html), while Lucas doesn't even crack the global top ten.

What made Lucas his fortune was not the Star Wars films themselves, but the Star Wars brand. In today's entertainment industry, a film is no longer a standalone entity; it has become one link in an IP-and-brand chain. A film's box office is no longer the end goal but a measure of the IP's value. Hence we see Star Wars toys and Star Wars lands in theme parks — merchandise can earn even more than the movies: the Star Wars films grossed $9.5 billion at the box office, while licensed merchandise brought in more than $40 billion.

In this environment, synergy-driven consolidation rather than vertical integration has become the most lucrative model. Disney (which was not named in the Paramount case, though it and the later-arriving studios also abided by the decree's rules) is the exemplar of this kind of consolidation. When we speak of Disney today, we mean not just a film studio but a media empire spanning film, television, and theme parks, weaving the IP business into every link. Its acquisitions of Marvel, Pixar, Lucasfilm, and 21st Century Fox have steadily expanded its IP inventory.

![Image: [pixabay](https://pixabay.com/zh/%E4%B9%90%E9%AB%98%E4%B8%BB%E9%A2%98%E5%85%AC%E5%9B%AD-%E4%B9%90%E9%AB%98-%E6%98%9F%E7%90%83%E5%A4%A7%E6%88%98-%E9%98%85%E5%85%B5-g%C3%BCnzburg-%E4%B8%BB%E9%A2%98%E5%85%AC%E5%9B%AD-3481634/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181227123750ECViYjbMdsxGleBU.jpg-w600)

[Take Disney's $71.3 billion acquisition of 21st Century Fox's assets](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/54496.html): after bringing 20th Century Fox's film studio into its camp, Disney intends to fold characters like the X-Men, Deadpool, and the Fantastic Four into the Marvel Cinematic Universe. For the MCU — which after *Avengers 4* enters a new phase and has already grossed $17.5 billion worldwide — Disney naturally welcomes the prospect of characters with untapped potential.

The Department of Justice expressed monopoly concerns during the Disney acquisition, but they were not about Disney buying a film studio — they were about sports channels. Disney paid a higher price for 21st Century Fox's 22 regional sports channels than for 20th Century Fox itself. Disney hoped the regional channels' content would complement its cable channel ESPN, and that holding them would give it leverage to drive down broadcast-rights fees when dealing with sports leagues.

That hope, however, ultimately went unrealized. When it approved Disney's acquisition of 21st Century Fox, the Department of Justice attached a condition requiring Disney to sell those 22 sports channels to comply with antitrust rules. The DOJ worried that holding regional channels would let the company charge higher fees in local markets.

After the DOJ announced its re-evaluation of the Paramount Decree, some broke into a sweat for the future of movie theaters, fearing the return of block booking. But in fact, antitrust enforcement in the United States had already loosened in the 1980s — practices that would once have been judged clear violations were even permitted, such as TriStar Pictures (founded by CBS, HBO, and Columbia Pictures) buying the Loew's theater chain, Warner Bros. acquiring multiple circuits, and Paramount spending $300 million to buy more than 100 theaters (into which Warner later injected further capital).

And on reflection, the media empires' current dominance over movie theaters probably has little to do with vertical mergers. Consider that the three major U.S. circuits — AMC, Regal, and Cinemark — are not owned by any media group, yet the media empires remain dominant; that tells you this leverage comes precisely from synergy-driven consolidation.

[A Wall Street Journal report, citing Disney as an example](https://www.wsj.com/articles/disney-lays-down-the-law-for-theaters-on-star-wars-the-last-jedi-1509528603), pointed out that in distributing *Star Wars: The Last Jedi*, Disney demanded a 65% share of ticket revenue — a new record; most studios take around 55%, and some blockbusters 60%.

Disney's terms with theaters also required that *Star Wars: The Last Jedi* play in a cinema's largest auditorium for at least four weeks and that scheduled screenings not be reduced. If a theater failed to honor the agreement, Disney would claim an additional 5% of ticket sales. Although *Star Wars: The Last Jedi* grossed $620 million, the highest of the year, some smaller theaters passed on the film for fear they could not meet the conditions.

### Streaming: Another Monopoly to Watch

In its statement, the Department of Justice treats streaming as a remedy for monopoly, but the reality is likely the opposite: Netflix is taking on a monopolistic posture, and it is beyond the reach of the Paramount Decree as written 70 years ago.

Hollywood has already felt the pressure from Netflix.

In 2016, The Hollywood Reporter published [an article on Hollywood's fear that Netflix was monopolizing content](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/features/netflix-backlash-why-hollywood-fears-928428). It quoted FX chairman John Landgraf: "I think it's bad for the creators of stories when one company is controlling 40, 50 or even 60 percent of the content in the marketplace."

That year, Netflix spent $6 billion on content — far outpacing HBO's $2 billion. Cable-industry insiders told The Hollywood Reporter that Netflix was no longer giving creators full creative freedom: Beau Willimon, the showrunner of the first four seasons of *House of Cards*, left over creative differences with the platform. (Willimon himself declined to comment.)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181227124302ahd8GHCNgzPExvF0.jpg-w600)

Netflix has not slowed its content spending: in 2018 its officially announced content budget reached $8 billion, with reports putting the real figure at $13 billion. Among the filmmakers who work with Netflix, many have spoken of enjoying full creative freedom — a claim that contradicts The Hollywood Reporter's earlier account. Still, creative suppression driven by the leverage that comes with monopoly is a real phenomenon worth noting and guarding against.

The streaming market has more players than Netflix alone, but calling it the dominant player is no exaggeration. Among streaming users in the United States, Netflix accounts for 77%, 21 percentage points ahead of second-place Amazon (users may subscribe to multiple services). By contrast, some niche streamers withered for lack of an audience, among them [FilmStruck, the service that carried many of the Criterion Collection's masterworks](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/57780.html).

News that Netflix and Amazon had each expressed interest in acquiring Landmark Theatres also easily evokes the studios' vertical integration. The Paramount Decree might constrain such moves when it comes to vertical integration, but when it comes to streamers exercising synergy like media groups, the decree has no purchase.

In July 2017, Netflix completed the first acquisition in its 20-year history, buying a comic-book company called Millarworld. Millarworld has created 18 comic-book properties, including the already well-known IPs *Kingsman*, *Kick-Ass*, and *Wanted*. Does this move invite comparisons with Disney's acquisition of Marvel? From Netflix's perspective, at least, it presumably welcomes the comparison. In an external statement, Netflix's chief content officer Ted Sarandos called Millarworld founder Mark Millar a modern-day Stan Lee.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812271238509E53PVghJ6fCStrl.jpg-w600)

In this sense, looking back at the Paramount Decree of 70 years ago makes plain that it has become an outdated law. In the 1940s, film was one of the few forms of entertainment and could only be watched in theaters, so whoever controlled the theaters could monopolize the market. Today, the entire annual U.S. box office doesn't even match Netflix's yearly revenue. In an era of ever more fragmented channels, splitting studios from theaters can no longer achieve the goal of curbing monopoly.

And yet the Paramount Decree's meaning for today lies in its reminder that the threat of monopoly is ever-present. The key question has always been to identify monopoly where it exists and find new ways to protect the innovation and vitality of the market as a whole. This time, as the Department of Justice re-evaluates the Paramount Decree, will it level its sights at Disney — or at Netflix?
