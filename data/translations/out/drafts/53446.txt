Real Estate Developers, Hotels, and Brokerage Firms Are All Getting Into the Long-Term Rental Apartment Business — but What They're After Isn't the Rent

@@QD_BODY@@
Real estate financial innovation.

Chinese people find it very hard to stop buying homes.

Last year China sold a cumulative 13 trillion yuan worth of commercial housing, [equivalent to half of all household savings](http://www.sohu.com/a/222263281_313480). China's three largest developers — Country Garden, Vanke, and Evergrande — each sold more than 500 billion yuan worth of homes last year.

By comparison, renting looks more like a transitional choice for consumers, [a sideline to selling homes](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/35296.html) for brokerages.

But all of a sudden, the big developers that once looked down on the rental business have charged into this market. Country Garden has completed an apartment project in Shanghai; Vanke has rented out 80% of its apartments in Beijing at 1.8 million yuan for ten-year leases. Evergrande, meanwhile, is preparing to raise 10 billion yuan for the business.

Beyond these three largest Chinese developers, companies with strong sales such as China Merchants Shekou and Longfor have also converted portions of their unsold inventory into long-term rental apartments. Brokerages such as Lianjia, World Union, and 5i5j have brands like Ziroom Apartments, Hongpu Apartments, and Xiangyu. Even hotel groups like Home Inn and Huazhu have launched long-term rental apartments.

And indeed, more people are renting.

According to estimates by Orient Securities Research Institute, China's rental housing market reached 1.14 trillion yuan in 2017, with 191 million people renting nationwide — rental demand is real. As many Chinese cities roll out talent-attraction policies, population will [concentrate further in first- and second-tier cities](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/53592.html), where rental demand may be even stronger.

Preferential policies from the state are likely a major driver. In 2016 the NDRC proposed establishing a housing system that gives equal weight to renting and buying; the State Council followed with preferential policies allowing commercial properties to be converted to rental use, with water, electricity, and gas charged at residential rates. Local governments including Zhejiang's also pledged, under the principle of controllable risk, to increase credit support for rental housing projects.

In this process, whether a long-term rental apartment company acquires land, builds, and rents out properties itself or acts as a second landlord, the essence is the same: provide tenants with better service, then charge higher rent.

### Long-term rental apartments offer a better experience, but almost none of them make money

The advantage of long-term rental apartments lies in standardized service and relatively more stable lease relationships among landlords, operators, and tenants. Uniform renovation and management help build a rental brand.

For landlords, they sign a long-term contract with a brokerage such as Ziroom or World Union, hand the property over for renovation, and take it back when the lease ends; during the term they deal only with the brokerage, rent collection is orderly, and problems that arise during occupancy are generally solved by the brokerage. Tenants get a similar kind of service.

![The Ziroom mobile app.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180523233048UihafWeEzomXHZIY.jpg-w600)

But for companies operating long-term rental apartments, the operating risks and pressure come from how to source properties, and then — once sourced — from advance rent payments, renovation, and other operating costs, all of which consume large amounts of cash flow.

Vanke's Port Apartment, for example, leases clusters of disused factories, office buildings, or residential properties from state-owned enterprises, governments, and village collectives on long-term contracts, typically for 10–15 years, then converts them into apartments for rent. China Merchants Shekou's apartment renovation spending was 276 million yuan, 374 million yuan, and 469 million yuan respectively from 2015 to 2017.

The initial outlay is enormous. If a company converts already-built inventory housing into long-term rental apartments, the cost accounting must also include the earlier land and construction costs.

For developers, though, the rent-to-price ratio in prime locations is too low, while in cheap, poorly located areas few people are willing to live. In China's first-tier cities it routinely takes 60–80 years of rent to recoup the purchase cost of a home. By the time you break even, you may have passed the 70-year property-rights limit. If someone rents out a home at age 30, there's a good chance they will never recoup the cost in their lifetime.

Rising rents might shorten the payback period, but developers still have to put up a huge sum of money to build first. Compared with the returns and payback speed of selling homes, renting is both lower and slower. Some cities, when approving land parcels, even specifically require that only rental housing be built, not commercial housing.

Han Shi, vice president and CEO of Longfor Group, said flatly in an interview with *Securities Daily* last year that [Goyoo would not consider profitability for the first three years](http://money.163.com/17/0905/05/CTI2RHF4002580S6.html). Vanke board chairman Yu Liang said at the 2017 interim results briefing that he would already be quite satisfied if long-term rental apartments achieved a return rate of 1%–2%.

Lianjia, the brokerage with the highest transaction volume in the industry, incubated the long-term rental apartment brand Ziroom internally and operates it as an independent business. Ziroom's [CEO Xiong Lin said back in 2017](http://www.sohu.com/a/190993187_637431) that its long-term rental apartments could only achieve stable cash flow at a 95% occupancy rate — and that's before mentioning profitability.

Brokerages don't hold properties directly, so they can choose locations as needed. Colliers International says about 80% of Shanghai's long-term rental apartment supply is distributed in the main urban area near the inner and middle ring roads, mostly along rail transit lines, with convenient transportation.

But factoring in recruitment, leasing, renovation, and other costs, every day a rented unit sits vacant adds a day of operating costs. The pressure is equally enormous. And when home prices are rising, faced with a resale gain of at least several hundred thousand yuan — or even over a million — landlords have a much lower cost of breaching contracts, and Ziroom finds them hard to restrain.

World Union, which has been developing long-term rental apartments for several years, reported in its 2017 annual report that it was operating 35,000 units in core first- and second-tier cities with an 81% occupancy rate; in 2017 it posted revenue of 208 million yuan but a loss of 184 million yuan. Among its many projects, only the Beijing Hongpu project was profitable. The main reason for the losses was high operating costs, of which property acquisition/base rent accounted for about 55–60% and renovation about 20%.

According to CITIC Securities' estimates, if a long-term rental apartment brand is subject to a 6% VAT rate and a 20% markup on subleased rent, its net profit margin is about -10.3%. Even when the sublease rent markup widens to 40%, net profit is only 3.4%. Of all expenses, rent accounts for 70%, renovation for 10%, and the rest goes to labor, marketing, and taxes.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180528231637FU6IJgRKj0Gx2cWV.png-w600)

Under such operating conditions, few companies can guarantee that money comes in faster than it goes out, let alone turn a profit. For real estate companies with hundreds of billions of yuan in assets as well as startups valued in the tens of billions, this will undoubtedly add to their [already crushing debt](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/44925.html).

That's because the real estate industry carries relatively high leverage — the average at the end of the first quarter of this year was close to 80%. Intensifying inventory pressure has made capital turnover even harder. And since 2016, tightening regulation has arrived one policy after another, further restricting developers' financing. Data from 10jqka's iFinD shows that the growth rate of China's real estate development investment, after peaking at 35% in 2010–2011, fell all the way to about 8% in 2017.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180528191028B8IGDtsmdlJH4UZR.png-w600)

### Rent is a dead end; long-term rental apartments are counting on two kinds of financial products

Real estate financial products have existed for years. But this year China's Central Economic Work Conference emphasized support for the rental market and widening direct financing channels. Specifically, this includes increasing the number of banks participating in rental housing development loans, developing asset-backed securities (ABS) products, and drafting plans for the development of real estate investment trusts (REITs).

These financial products can provide long-term rental apartments with the credit support necessary to keep cash flow running.

By the end of the first quarter of this year, China had approved the issuance of 7 real estate ABS deals, raising 25.12 billion yuan. In March, Ziroom obtained a quota to issue 2 billion yuan in ABS.

Specifically: when tenants sign a lease, they then pay the rental company monthly or quarterly for the next year — that is a form of debt. The rental company packages these debts into securities products and sells them to investors — that is ABS (asset-backed securitization).

Investors who buy ABS earn interest (rental income); it is essentially not much different from buying wealth-management products. The rental company, meanwhile, doesn't have to wait for the rent every month and can recoup its investment in advance to put into new projects.

Ziroom successfully issued a 500-million-yuan ABS last year, backed by the rental contracts of individual apartments.

Mofang Apartment's 350-million-yuan ABS, meanwhile, packaged and sold the next three years of rental income from its 4,014 apartments, regardless of whether those buildings were occupied. Its underlying assets were microloan claims from rent-installment lending — namely, the rent-installment microloans of Ziroom's Youjia and whole-rental business lines.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524004446P0eWtTb3Rm6JELAQ.png-w600)

At the end of April, the CSRC and the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development jointly issued [a new policy to promote the securitization of rental housing](http://www.csrc.gov.cn/pub/newsite/zjhxwfb/xwdd/201804/t20180425_337315.html), encouraging companies to securitize rental housing assets.

Beyond ABS, another, larger financial product — the real estate investment trust (REIT) — has also been liberalized in China. Long-term rental apartment operators raise funds from investors by issuing shares or beneficiary certificates, then carry out real estate investment and management, and distribute the aggregate returns to investors proportionally.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524004525Wl9etHqSfsXuG2Tp.png-w600)

Usually, most of the money REITs raise is used to buy and hold income-producing properties such as office buildings, hotels, apartments, and industrial real estate. Returns come mainly from rental income and property appreciation, with 90% of returns paid out to investors as dividends.

Compared with ABS, whose underlying assets are future rental income, REITs — whose underlying assets are rental income or property ownership — are much larger in scale.

According to CICC's calculations, China's total real estate value at the end of 2017 was about 200 trillion yuan; under a neutral assumption that REITs account for 2% of the real estate market, China's total REITs market works out to roughly 4 trillion yuan.

Whether ABS or REITs, the essence is the same: the issuer mortgages the future income a property may generate in exchange for financing. Developers, brokerages, and hotel groups are all participating — for example, CIFI's 3-billion-yuan Lingyu long-term rental apartment shelf-registered equity REITs, and Country Garden's 10-billion-yuan rental housing REITs. Greentown also received a no-objection letter for a 1.48-billion-yuan hotel-asset CMBS. Xinpai Apartment, backed by Huazhu Group investment, is preparing to issue 270 million yuan in ABS. Evergrande's 10-billion-yuan rental ABS quota has already been approved.

This way, a company that builds an apartment no longer has to wait fifty or sixty years — or even longer — to recoup its investment; it can use financial products to get the money back in advance and put it into new investments.

### Real estate finance started in the United States, where there are some success stories

American REITs began in the early 1950s. In 1960, the U.S. Congress's Internal Revenue Code of 1960 first established the industry's norms and legal status, permitting investment in offices, residential, industrial, retail, warehouse, and hotel properties.

REITs can widen funding sources for real estate companies and speed up capital turnover, playing a significant role in accelerating their expansion and improving profitability. The two hotel groups Starwood and Marriott both expanded rapidly by mortgaging their real estate through REITs. And from very early on, U.S. regulators made REITs a financial product similar to stock trading, making it convenient for investors to buy and sell.

The best example of the past two decades of a company using REITs to raise funds and then make serial acquisitions to become an industry leader is probably Equity Residential (EQR), one of the largest long-term rental apartment companies in the United States.

EQR was the first listed long-term rental apartment company in the United States. Its operating model focuses on developing, acquiring, and subsequently managing high-quality apartments. After expanding through acquisitions, it contracted geographically, continually exiting non-core regions.

From its IPO in 1993 through 2000, EQR issued REITs 43 times, raising US$3.219 billion, funds mainly used to acquire properties and then rent them out. By the end of 2017, EQR was operating about 78,000 apartments with total revenue of US$2.471 billion.

EQR's success also has to do with the high rent-to-price ratio in the United States, where the national average payback period is about 19 years of rent. The local rental market was profitable to begin with.

99% of EQR's revenue is rent, and its operating profit reached US$847 million. In addition, it made US$4.065 billion by selling properties in 2016. Whether from rental income or property appreciation, it has delivered solid, stable returns to investors. Its current market cap is about 140 billion yuan in RMB terms. Vanke, the highest-valued real estate company in China, has more than 40 times EQR's annual revenue and a market cap of about 310 billion yuan.

Beyond the United States, REITs markets in the rest of the world have also been gradually developing in recent years. By the end of 2017, more than 30 countries and regions had issued REITs, and global REITs had a net market value of about US$1.69 trillion. U.S. REITs products alone account for 43.8% of the global total, Japan for 9.6%, and Hong Kong, China for 7.64%.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805290205103kHa7ObdX8MWGRrU.png-w600)

However, according to CICC, China's REITs are constrained by problems including an unclear domestic legal and regulatory authority, yield suppression from double taxation, a shortage of quality properties that can be securitized, and a lack of professional REITs management institutions and talent. The REITs already issued only approximate the product form — they are not true REITs — and operate inefficiently with low yields.

By its estimates, China's current REITs yield of around 3% is basically on par with the yields of most existing asset plans such as ABS, and far below the roughly 12% yield in the United States — which will affect their financing capacity.

### But as long as investors all believe home prices won't fall, real estate finance will bring new risks

Land acquisition and construction in the real estate industry both demand large amounts of capital, so a core competitive strength of domestic real estate companies is, in fact, the ability to borrow money.

Only by borrowing can they keep operating and speed up expansion. This has also left several real estate companies with very high net debt ratios.

Encouraging the real estate and long-term rental apartment industries to issue ABS and REITs now adds a new method of financing expansion through financial products.

But in this process, if China's rent-to-price ratio remains so high that it still takes seventy or eighty years to recoup costs, then ABS and REITs merely let companies pull seventy or eighty years of income forward — without solving the problem these companies worried about in the first place: that the rental business doesn't make money.

New financial products bring more money, which will stimulate real estate companies to expand — buying more land, building more homes. But if what lies behind that money is a business that doesn't make money, the risks keep growing.

Developers' use of the cash raised through long-term rental apartments is restricted, but as long as the current state of unprofitable renting doesn't change, the participating companies still have the incentive to channel money through complex financial products into the land market — acquiring new land, building new homes — and consumers will likewise keep taking out mortgages to buy homes. It amounts to securitization extending credit based on banks and the government to a wider range of borrowers; risk is accumulating.

Former Fed chairman Ben Bernanke once said that in a world with perfect markets, bankruptcies would not happen.

Against the backdrop of China's land finance, both homebuilders and homebuyers believe economic growth cannot do without real estate. And since the government wants to maintain economic growth, there is limited room for home prices to fall. This is the shared expectation of developers and homebuyers, and the money earned keeps being plowed back into this market. Chinese real estate looks like a perfect market.

But the thing is, nothing is really perfect.
