Amid the anxiety of a turbulent, divided year, Samuel West believes that the attitude of learning from failure and chaos matters more than ever.

To catch the frozen-food wave, Colgate launched beef lasagna in 1920;

To monetize its brand as quickly as possible, Harley-Davidson — a company famed for its wild, free spirit — launched Harley perfume in 1990;

To grab a share of the premium coffee market, Coca-Cola launched a coffee cola in 2006;

...

Besides all coming from big companies, these products share one thing in common — they all ended in failure. Many people never had the chance to see these products or learn the stories behind them.

But now there is a "Museum of Failure" in Sweden devoted to displaying more than 70 failed products from the 1940s to the present day, spanning over 60 well-known companies worldwide.

The museum was founded by Samuel West, a Swedish psychology professor tired of success stories. In his view, "tedious successes are all alike, while every distinctive failure is different in its own way."

"Everyone knows that 80 to 90 percent of innovation fails — it's no secret," he said. "So what about all these failed products? Why do we only ever hear about the successes?"

Three years ago, as part of his research on innovation, Samuel began collecting failed products, which had to come from what he defines as innovative companies — companies that consistently launch successful products or services. Along the way, he made a special trip to the Museum of Broken Relationships in Croatia, a museum that collects gifts abandoned by couples after their breakups. That visit gave Samuel the idea that failed products, too, could fill a museum full of ceremony.

![A coffee cola](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504231716kpcijBGKANlaMC7E.jpg-w600)

Beyond making people aware that failure is inevitable in the innovation process, Samuel has a higher aspiration — that both companies and governments can admit their mistakes, discuss current problems openly, and learn from failure and chaos. Amid the anxiety of a turbulent, divided year, this attitude matters more than ever.

The Museum of Failure officially launched its publicity two weeks ago. In the 14 days since, Samuel has received four or five phone interview requests a day, and the museum has drawn the attention of more than 20 media outlets around the world.

A week ago, the museum also appeared on the famous American talk show *Late Night with Seth Meyers*, where the host said: "The Museum of Failure in Sweden opens to the public in June. If you can't wait to see it, you can take a look at this one in Washington, D.C. first (the screen showed a photo of the White House)."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504214037jdGvsq4FmNf6Eeit.jpeg-w600)

In January, the Museum of Failure appeared in pop-up form at an innovation conference held in London by the digital marketing company Mirum. Since then, Samuel has been receiving pop-up invitations from Germany, the Netherlands, the United States, Turkey, Singapore and elsewhere; he says the pop-up schedule is now booked into 2018.

The massive global attention the museum has drawn in such a short time has far exceeded Samuel's expectations. His schedule is now almost entirely filled by pop-up museums — "but it's quite simple: my pop-up museum fits in my four suitcases. I just take them to the airport, check them in, and show up at the pop-up location."

Though he can talk about it lightly now, collecting these products took Samuel a great deal of effort. He had hoped companies would voluntarily donate or share some of their failed products of the past, but he clearly underestimated how tight-lipped companies are about them.

Samuel lives in Sweden and had assumed that IKEA, headquartered in Sweden, would be a sure thing. This American from California has lived in Sweden for 18 years and was convinced it is an open, transparent country — "people are happy to share, there are no secrets here," he said — but he still never expected that "the moment you mention failure, everyone falls silent." ... Samuel contacted at least 15 people who work at IKEA, across the innovation department, public relations, social media and so on, and his request was politely worded: "As a hugely successful international company, could you donate some failed products to my museum?" "Oh, that's a great idea, I love your museum, hahaha, very interesting. Let's stay in touch, bye." Then the phone was hung up.

And through his repeated contacts with IKEA, he gradually realized that "failure" is an extremely sensitive topic for companies.

![Bic For Her, the pen for women](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504230827Fd732TngUAkXwbve.jpeg-w600)

![Apple's Newton handheld computer](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705042319086oTCOmiALFZwE910.jpeg-w600)

After that he changed strategy: he stopped casting a wide net, and stopped messaging companies' official email addresses or calling their front desks, since operators could never connect him to the right person. Samuel's new strategy was to use social media, sending messages to companies' official LinkedIn and Facebook accounts. Companies now attach ever more importance to this more transparent and active platform, assigning people to manage their social media accounts around the clock.

For instance, he messaged Volvo — the Swedish car brand, acquired by China's Geely in 2010 — on Facebook: "Volvo is a highly innovative car company, but along the way it has also made some mistakes. Could you share some such products, along with your reflections on them?"

Samuel's hope came to nothing in the end — "They simply ignored me." "I'm disappointed, but not surprised. I know companies keep secret what they're working on and their future ambitions, and I understand that."

![Colgate beef lasagna](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504214119IUaMCvgVn5i6KxqH.jpeg-w600)

So all of the more than 70 failed products in Samuel's collection ultimately came from interviews with consumers and from research materials.

In these interviews, Samuel learned that one reason companies keep silent about failed products is to protect their image. Revealing, say, that a company once invested 2 million euros in a failed product would send a negative signal to its investors and to the market as a whole, directly affecting the company's market value. And if recent failure cases leaked, competitors could quickly learn the direction and progress of its research.

As a psychology professor, Samuel understands this — people are the same, after all. Few people make their failures public; what they show is mostly the glossy fragments. "We're very much like these companies: in front of friends, on Facebook, we present an image of ourselves. It's an image — not always real, but it's the one we want others to see."

Although Samuel ultimately received no support from companies, the positive feedback the museum has received around the world seems to have softened that disappointment.

![A Monopoly-like Trump board game — though if it were released today, its fate might be different](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504214336gFxUiztACVOwI6pm.jpg-w600)

Samuel returned to San Francisco in 2014, and he remembers that at every restaurant or café he entered, there was always a sign at the door that read: "Google Glass wearers are not allowed in."

Google Glass, of course, made it into Samuel's museum.

The museum's exhibits span nearly 80 years, but the failed products of the last decade share a striking resemblance: products like Google Glass reveal the technological trap of this particular era — in the rush to enter and grab the market first, more and more products come out while still mere prototypes, some without any practical function at all.

They enter consumers' field of vision at a stunning speed, and vanish just as quickly.

![Google Glass](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705042145205CPonrQNGDBh89VR.jpg-w600)

When Google Glass launched in 2012, priced at $1,500, Google initially — generously — gave the glasses to internet celebrities, bloggers and tech journalists among others, to try out. But these tech pioneers found Google Glass was indeed cool, and that was about it. "Google completely botched it. It was a prototype with incomplete technology and a battery that didn't work. It had no functions — just a lens," Samuel said.

Worse still, with its microphone and camera, Google Glass raised issues of integrity and privacy; nobody wants to be watched 24 hours a day.

It was a failure by any measure — both as a product and in moral terms.

Google Glass ceased production on January 15, 2015.

Silicon Valley's other giant, Twitter, fell into the same trap, and is also represented among the exhibits in Samuel's museum. In 2008, Twitter teamed up with Peek to launch the Twitter Peek phone, priced at $200. The phone's main — and only — function was tweeting; it couldn't make calls or send emails.

Setting aside the thought that "a phone that can't even make calls can hardly be called a phone," its screen was so small that it couldn't even display a full 140-character tweet; the number of tweets you could receive was capped at 25, and you could only have one Twitter account ... All these design flaws made the product utterly uncompetitive. "By the time this phone came out, there were already plenty of phones with mobile internet," Samuel said.

![The Twitter phone](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504214548CbpBV8MtqrgLx36f.jpeg-w600)

What the Twitter phone and Google Glass have in common is that the newness and supposed breakthrough of these products were questionable; the democratization of technology never happened, and the mainstream public gained nothing from them.

In recent years, intensifying competition brought by globalization explains why some companies sacrifice product quality to keep up with speed. But Samuel has found one trap that neither companies of the past nor those of the present can escape — obsessing over launching new products or new services without asking why.

The new products neither address customers' real needs nor carry any latent value. And this tragedy has repeated itself throughout history.

In Britain's Victorian era (1837–1901), once people discovered the potential uses of electricity, they couldn't wait to experiment. "They used this exciting, magical new thing to create all kinds of new products, some of them downright dangerous!" Samuel said. There was, for example, the electric corset billed as "a must-wear for every married woman," said to radiate healthy energy and cure women's back ailments, colds and rheumatism; and the electric comb that treated constipation and malaria ...

It sounds utterly absurd today, but the era's passion for creating for creation's sake made it happen.

![The electric corset](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504215420ahz2Uj80ksdLnx41.jpeg-w600)

Among Samuel's exhibits is an extreme and complicated tragedy of failure — Kodak. In 1996, Kodak ranked fourth among the most valuable American companies, yet it could never shake its complacent, inward-looking culture.

As early as 1975, Kodak had built a digital camera, and management's reaction was: "That's cute, but don't tell anyone." They worried digital cameras would hurt the company's hugely profitable film-processing business, and so refused to make any decision that would threaten their existing interests.

Even after the company launched the DC40 digital camera in 1995, it remained committed to getting people to buy cameras along with the matching printers and paper, pinning its hopes on the traditional profit model.

In Samuel's view, Kodak's boldest move was launching Ofoto, a photo-sharing website. Sadly, they treated online sharing as an offshoot of the existing business rather than an entirely new direction. And when people turned to their phones to take photos, Kodak still refused to change its business model. "They were too stubborn," Samuel said.

Leica, by contrast, didn't launch a digital camera until 2006, yet the company now has annual revenue of $360 million.

![Kodak's DC40 digital camera](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504214654S0FoNWsUdQef1DVM.jpg-w600)

Kodak also once sought outside help — Harvard professor Clayton Christensen, dubbed the "innovation czar" by The New York Times and author of the bestsellers *The Innovator's Dilemma* and *The Innovator's Solution*. In an interview with Harvard Business Review, Christensen mentioned that he had once spent three days in discussion with Kodak, and that Kodak's conclusion was: the innovator's dilemma was exactly Kodak's dilemma at the time — having done everything right was the biggest mistake.

To break free of dependence on the parent organization, Kodak followed Clayton Christensen's advice and set up an independent product division to develop and sell a low-cost, low-megapixel product, EasyShare, which at one point claimed one-third of the US camera market.

And the rest of the story is a familiar one: after Kodak changed CEOs, the new chief decided the independent division was no different from any other traditional division — thin margins, added cost. The division was folded into the company's existing structure, its camera share promptly fell to 12 percent, and it was eventually sold to Flextronics, the Singapore-headquartered electronics manufacturer.

Samuel chose to exhibit Kodak's cameras in the museum not to show that its products failed, but that its strategy failed: "Kodak invented the digital camera and put it into production, but in the end, the digital camera destroyed Kodak."

In 2012, Kodak declared bankruptcy and 16,000 people immediately lost their jobs. That same year, Instagram was sold to Facebook for $1 billion.

![In 1999, actress Linda Evans was the spokesperson for the electric face mask](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705042314497qDB0UaV9cfp5xCz.jpeg-w600)

![An electric face mask that claimed a few zaps a month could make you more beautiful](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504214753rtGhudajUzikYKF5.jpeg-w600)

When we asked Samuel who should be held responsible for these failed products, if anyone had to be, he said it often points to the marketing department. This is the unit inside a company that gathers the latest market intelligence and is regarded as the listening post for consumer demand; precisely because of this, its advice is hard to ignore.

Peter Thiel and Blake Masters, authors of *Zero to One*, wrote that "as long as a company keeps innovating, the startup isn't over; once innovation stops, the startup is over."

Precisely because the word "innovation" is so fashionable, it is sometimes narrowly understood as launching a new product or a new service — and the marketing department is the creator of this kind of "innovation."

They always have an endless stream of exciting ideas, and even when the R&D department says a product isn't finished yet, marketing can persuade them that the opportunity must not be missed and they must act at once.

![Sony video cassettes](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504231014rnpoTDcht9sUIwSL.jpg-w600)

By comparison, bad decision-making at the leadership level can be even more catastrophic. And it is worse in "dictatorial" cultures. Samuel points out that in the more hierarchical, bureaucratic companies of Asian countries such as China and Japan, employees are used to obeying the boss unquestioningly — the worst possible condition for innovation.

Stephen Sass, a Cornell professor who once taught in China, once said: ["I don't believe China will soon gain the upper hand in innovation — at least not until China's institutional culture shifts from suppressing dissidents toward supporting free speech and encouraging critical thinking."](https://cn.nytimes.com/opinion/20140123/c23sass/)

He believes innovation cannot do without skepticism and asking critical questions.

On this front, many Asian companies have made progress. And to get closer to Silicon Valley, the global center of innovation, many companies have chosen in recent years to set up R&D departments or research institutes there — Baidu, JD.com, Suning and the like; this past March, Didi also announced plans to establish a research institute in Silicon Valley.

Silicon Valley has always been regarded as the place most willing to accept failure and to talk about it. The principle it lives by, Samuel says, is: "Create a company culture that accepts failure, so that you can make small mistakes and thereby avoid catastrophic big ones."

![Harley perfume](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504215022cygqG7U1otTB8sFM.jpg-w600)

Kodak's bankruptcy may be one of the most tragic episodes in business history; behind it lies the transformation dilemma that thousands upon thousands of companies face. In Christensen's view, transformation is never easy, especially for mature companies. However talented the people they hire, those people must follow established workflows and business models. Those processes succeeded in the past and are the safest choice — but they also doom innovation to failure.

Looking back at the hundreds of American department store companies, only Dayton-Hudson (the predecessor of Target) successfully transformed itself into a discount retailer; and every single minicomputer company was routed in the personal computer era.

"When disruption arrives, an organization's strengths become its obstacles," Christensen writes in *The Innovator's Solution*.

![Blockbuster](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504231647wbDisrVOl5RTIeSv.jpg-w600)

Despite having given countless media interviews by now, Samuel still seemed caught off guard when asked about his own failures. He says he always has plenty of novel ideas, but they always fizzle out because of personal finances. Preparing the Museum of Failure forced him to realize: "If I don't figure out a way to make money from it this time, the Museum of Failure will die young too."

At present, the Museum of Failure relies in small part on research funding from Lund University in Sweden; 90 percent comes out of Samuel's own pocket, including the pay for his assistant and two museum designers.

Despite the tight finances, he refuses to actively seek sponsors. Samuel wants this to be an independent museum, controlled by no company. "If a company is a sponsor, they still don't get to decide what can and can't be shown ... I don't want to sacrifice my freedom for corporate money."

For now, the way he has come up with to make money is to recoup some costs through pop-up museums around the world. Most of those inviting him are innovation and technology conferences or forums, where he shows the museum and gives related talks.

At these conferences, Samuel inevitably runs into his "nemeses." At the innovation conference in London in January, the booths next to Samuel's belonged to Adobe and Microsoft, but "everyone came over to look at this rather strange museum. It was a huge success."

![Nokia's N-Gage](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170504215140ZUYls6i2TagAFOrq.jpeg-w600)

One visitor stood thoughtfully in front of the Nokia N-Gage, Nokia's first dedicated mobile gaming device, launched in 2004, which combined a phone with a game console. But its operating system and design were criticized for having too many flaws; to change games, users had to disassemble the device — power it off, open the back cover, take out the battery ... And "they had almost no games — basically only two worth playing," Samuel said.

In the week after the N-Gage launched, total sales across Britain's 6,000 game retailers came to fewer than 500 units; production was discontinued in November 2005.

And the person standing in front of that machine at that moment was none other than Nokia's current head of innovation.
