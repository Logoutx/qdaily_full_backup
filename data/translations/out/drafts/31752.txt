Shanghai Disneyland Has Been Open for Nearly Three Months — What Are the People Doing Business Off It Worried About?
@@QD_EXCERPT@@

@@QD_BODY@@
We spoke with merchants in Disneytown, shop assistants at Shanghai Village, and homestay owners in the surrounding area. Here's what they told us.

Zhaohang Village, in the Huanglou community of Chuansha, Shanghai, lies about 30 kilometers from the city center.

It is surrounded on all sides by artificial waterways, and Metro Line 11 runs right underneath, delivering tourists directly into the heart of this land. About 600 meters from the metro station, visitors can see the bronze statue of Mickey Mouse at the helm of a steamboat. Farther off, the castle's spires loom faintly into view.

Most of the visitors now on this land have never heard of "Zhaohang Village"; they all simply call it "Disney." That is the new name this place acquired starting in 2009, and it will be the name used for a long time to come.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902142342szroqQNXFRvKcTZH.jpeg-w600)

Only the Shanghai municipal government won't call it Disney, but it no longer mentions Zhaohang Village either. It calls the area the Shanghai International Tourism and Resorts Zone. The name signals the hope that Disney's influence will not stay confined to the park itself, but will extend several kilometers — even tens of kilometers — into the surrounding area.

From the early planning stages, the Shanghai municipal government laid out five distinct zones in the four cardinal directions around the core area where Shanghai Disneyland sits, serving functions including a cluster of arts and culture academies, a modern entertainment and commercial complex, the ancient town of Hengmian, a wedding services base, and high-end corporate leisure facilities.

Even inside the core area, it placed two tourism and shopping projects entirely independent of Shanghai Disneyland: the Shendi Eco-Park and Shanghai Village.

In the government's plan, all of these surrounding developments would benefit from the huge flows of visitors drawn by Shanghai Disneyland.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902142413zydq2XAn3fpP1U7B.jpg-w600)

It has now been nearly three months since Shanghai Disneyland's grand opening on June 16. According to Ma Xuejie, deputy director of the Pudong New Area Commerce Commission, the park averaged about 27,000 visitors per day in June. On August 9, at an analyst conference following the earnings release, Disney chairman Bob Iger also said: "Since June 16, we have welcomed well over 1 million guests." Disney, however, has yet to express positive expectations for this area's performance.

According to the 《上海迪士尼人群洞察报告》(Shanghai Disneyland Visitor Insights Report) from the independent third-party data platform TalkingData, visitor numbers rose continuously from June 16 to July 17. Beyond that, though, there is little independent data to demonstrate how it is performing.

So how is this highly anticipated Disneyland doing?

### Disneytown, 50 Meters Away

"This Starbucks is expected to become the busiest store in the world."

Two months ago, when Starbucks opened a store in Disneytown, just a stone's throw from Shanghai Disneyland, Belinda Wong, CEO of Starbucks China, said as much.

The store is Starbucks' first flagship in Shanghai and its fourth in China; the design alone took Starbucks a year and a half. To cope with the potential flood of customers, it staffed this 437-square-meter store with 110 employees, a considerable number of whom move around the store floor helping customers place orders.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902142524H8W70YUV6d3ac9Tg.jpg-w600)

Starbucks is just one of the many brands in Disneytown, an open-air shopping center covering 46,000 square meters, designed with reference to the layout of traditional American small towns and outlet malls. It is now home to nearly 50 brands, including Starbucks, Xiao Nan Guo, Chow Tai Fook, Superdry, UGG, and Sephora.

The idea of pairing such a shopping center with a Disneyland dates to 1975. Four years after the opening of the Disneyland in Florida, Disney opened a shopping center called the Lake Buena Vista Shopping Village, next to the golf courses of the resort.

In 1984, then-Disney CEO Michael Eisner wanted to keep visitors — and even locals — inside the entire Disney resort, so the Lake Buena Vista Shopping Village received more attention and investment. Over the following 30 years, after repeated expansions and remodels, it became Disney Springs, spanning 100,000 square meters.

There are no hard figures for how much revenue Disney Springs brings Disney, or how much profit the brands inside it make. But the fact that speaks to what a lucrative business this is: in 2001 Disney brought Disneytown to the area near the Disneyland in Los Angeles, and with each successive expansion of Disney Springs, more and more brands moved in.

![Concept rendering of Disney Springs](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902143125av9FbcuZoiepq5ET.jpg-w600)

Most of the merchants in Shanghai's Disneytown were expecting the same.

Xiao Nan Guo took four storefronts here, introducing a different brand into each. Citing the approaching earnings season, Xiao Nan Guo declined our interview request, but according to [an earlier report](http://cbnweek.com/v/article?id=22455), the entry fee alone cost Xiao Nan Guo nearly 8 million yuan — not counting rent higher than that of Raffles City, the high-end shopping center in downtown Shanghai.

British streetwear brand Superdry also opened its first store in China in Disneytown. Hu Xiangzhou, general manager of Superdry China, told QDaily that what they value is Disneytown's heavy foot traffic, and the fact that the crowds are highly diverse — a chance for Superdry to showcase its brand.

For some other brands, what they invested was more than money.

The Hot Toys store in Disneytown is the first shop with in-stock sales opened by this Hong Kong luxury figurine brand, and for a long time to come it will be the only one. Li Xue'er, Hot Toys' marketing communications manager, told QDaily in an interview that this is because after touring Shanghai Disneyland, visitors prefer to buy some merchandise to take straight home.

But when we came to Disneytown on the afternoon of August 14, a summer day, we saw only scattered groups of people walking down the somewhat empty main street. The open-format restaurants like Food Republic and Starbucks had some patrons, while brands like Chow Tai Fook, Aape, Swatch, and Samsonite sat nearly deserted.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902145324RN98VahcYdfXPZ5A.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609021454333n8Q0YCtGX1zfIPD.jpg-w600)

Lego, though, had brought in a master builder and set up a monkey twice human height outside its store, which drew some attention. Lego staff put up queue barriers at the entrance, signaling that visitors would have to wait a moment before entering. A person close to Lego said that apart from the World of Disney, the park's own merchandise store, Lego had the highest foot traffic and sales among Disneytown's merchants.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902145134FhqGfxWLjCPgB0M9.jpg-w600)

And inside Shanghai Disneyland, just one wall away, the queue for its most popular ride, Soaring Over the Horizon, still exceeded three hours.

"All I can say is it's too hot, and there aren't many people. Whether business is good or not, you can see for yourself with a walk around outside." One shop assistant was reluctant to say much about business, casually attributing it to the weather. An Adidas store clerk not far away, meanwhile, said: "In the evening, the crowds pick up."

The challenge for merchants may be more than just the weather. "You spend 499 yuan on a ticket to get in and play, and the queues are so long — you're not that likely to come back out into Disneytown," a clerk at the Aikemi Art Gallery, which specializes in paintings and sits beside the Walt Disney Grand Theatre, told QDaily.

Disney CEO Bob Iger confirmed this at the analyst conference in early August: "Guests going to Shanghai Disneyland are staying in the park two hours longer than we expected." Bob Iger believes this is because Chinese visitors embrace the experience the park offers — but for Disneytown's merchants, it simply means the odds of a Shanghai Disneyland visitor becoming a Disneytown customer are even lower.

"The whole of Disneytown doesn't have many people, and the ones who do come fall into three types: those who buy a metro ticket just to come take photos, those who just checked into the Disney hotel and are taking a stroll, and those here to see *The Lion King*," the Aikemi Art Gallery clerk analyzed.

*The Lion King* is a musical produced by Disney, performed every day except Monday at the 1,200-seat Walt Disney Grand Theatre, with an additional show added on weekends. A look at ticket sales on the official website shows that even on weekdays, performance tickets under 600 yuan always sell out early.

Mr. Yang, resting on a flower bed, had just seen *The Lion King* with his family and was about to get something to eat before heading home. Asked about the merchants in Disneytown, he said only that he wasn't very interested in shopping.

![A scene from The Lion King](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902145903qDkrd8fuI6JME3Hj.jpeg-w600)

"I'd venture to say that not a single store in Disneytown is profitable right now," the Aikemi Art Gallery clerk said.

As the sun slanted westward and the summer heat faded, Disneytown began to feel like a public square; after nightfall, nearby residents strolled leisurely here with their families in tow. A warm breeze blew, and children ran and played freely.

The Starbucks entrance faces this scene directly. In an email reply to QDaily, Winnie Ma, general manager of the Disneytown store, wrote: "Unlike a regular store, our flagship has to handle thousands of guests every day."

But when we walked into the store around 6:30, only four of the six ordering windows at the counter were open. A clerk stood to one side telling entering customers which register to order at. In Starbucks' original vision, this person was supposed to channel the flow of people — but there was no flow to channel.

Shanghai Village, 1,500 Meters Away

The P3 parking lot of the Shanghai International Tourism and Resorts Zone is the parking lot closest to Shanghai Village, and it is also the endpoint of the driving route Shanghai Village recommends. At 2 p.m. on August 13, the lot stood nearly empty; the number on the electronic sign at the entrance showed 605 spaces still available.

![The P3 parking lot](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609021502033w59gHY7eNtuJPjq.jpg-w600)

Less than four months ago, Shanghai Village officially opened. The outlet-style development was jointly built by Britain's Value Retail and Shanghai Shendi Group. It sits inside the core area, about 1,500 meters from the Disney Resort metro station.

To get to Shanghai Village from the Disney Resort station, you can walk or take the shuttle bus inside the zone. In fact, the buses are always empty, and there is hardly a pedestrian on the walking route either. Nobody travels from Disney to Shanghai Village.

Shanghai Village may be the best-serviced outlet mall in recent years. Benches for resting are everywhere, complete with elegant cushions. Even on entering, a security guard reminds you that the sun is strong today and that you can take an umbrella at the gate for shade.

Nike's factory store sits in a corner of Shanghai Village. The two-floor store was buzzing. One customer was at the register counting the shoes he had bought, and seven or eight large paper bags sat piled at the door — all his spoils.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902182556TN5tJIVX4DHcwCr0.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902182556R7fMpP1GiZ8A2ndE.jpg-w600)

This was already the most crowded spot in all of Shanghai Village; apart from Coach, no other brand could rival Nike. Most stores stood empty — even one or two groups of customers made a store feel lively.

Even so, Nike was still not satisfied. "Business here is much worse than at the store near Pudong Airport," one clerk said. The store he referred to is in Florentia Village, an outlet mall that opened in January 2015, just two metro stops from Pudong Airport.

Some clerks see Florentia Village's presence as the reason Shanghai Village lacks buzz. "Their publicity side has started pushing hard — the metro is full of Florentia Village ads," said a clerk at the Armani Outlets store.

That may be right. Because the outlet format sells itself on aggregating large numbers of brands with low discounted prices, its locations tend to be relatively remote, so publicity and marketing are especially important for a place like Shanghai Village.

In theory, Shanghai Village should not have to worry about this. When Value Retail reached its cooperation agreement with Shendi in 2014, Scott Malkin, chairman of Value Retail, said that benefiting from the Shanghai Disney Resort's location and its convenient, diverse transport connections, the Shanghai Village project would become Asia's most important tourism shopping outlet.

Moreover, La Vallée Village, operated by Value Retail outside Paris, is less than a 10-minute drive from the local Disneyland. That village receives over 14 million visitors a year, so Value Retail ought to know how to run an outlet mall near a Disneyland.

![La Vallée Village](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902151047iZLM9pObqmThYQXn.jpg-w600)

In a sense, the problem Shanghai Village faces is very similar to Disneytown's.

"A Disney ticket costs 499 yuan after all — if it were me, I'd want to stay inside a while longer too," said a staffer at Zanadu, an online high-end travel agency. Because it has a partnership with Shanghai Village, it opened its first offline experience store here, offering travel shorts shot in virtual reality for visitors to try. After 3 p.m., the VR queue had only reached number 60-something — not many people. The staffer summed it up as "a bad location."

Data from TalkingData also bears this out. The customer traffic concentration index for July 2016 (the customer volume of a specific area divided by the overall customer volume in the same period) shows that the traffic of Shanghai Disneyland itself was four times that of the shopping and dining district.

If we calculate Shanghai Disneyland's daily attendance at 27,000, then Shanghai Village's daily traffic is a mere 6,750, less than 2.5 million a year — roughly one-sixth of La Vallée Village, operated by Value Retail.

A tourism industry insider who asked to remain anonymous said travel agencies may consider packaging Shanghai Village together with Shanghai Disneyland: Disneyland as an optional paid activity, with those unwilling to join being dropped off at Shanghai Village instead.

QDaily sent an interview request to Shanghai Village but had not received a response by publication time.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902151308V5BHgkqMwEFQfXo1.jpg-w600)

### Oubo Shengbaoyuan, More Than 5,000 Meters Away — Where Even Is This?

Lanxue Road is a narrow two-lane street lined with all kinds of grocery shops and snack stalls; there's even a garment factory called Yidong.

Directly opposite the factory is a residential compound called Oubo Shengbaoyuan, with more than 100 villas. Built in 2005, the compound finally got a new lease on life in 2016 because it is less than five kilometers from Shanghai Disneyland.

![Oubo Shengbaoyuan](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902151701v9D64GxIEKpbNYrk.jpg-w600)

Mars and his three friends came to this compound in May this year. He took over the detached villa at No. 89 and began renovating it; the whole project cost more than 1.2 million yuan.

"I used to work at a hotel nearby during the World Expo, and the hotel was full every day. Disney's foot traffic should be about the same as the Expo's, and it's a project with long-term development — unlike the Expo, which was over after five months," Mars said, describing his original intention of starting a homestay near Disney. "Even if visitor numbers are small, it's still enough for us to make a living."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902152232RdDSAUoIyBTvsutY.jpg-w600)

![The homestay run by Mars](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902152053ABkX0uoyZ5IvhKf2.jpg-w600)

In college, Mars studied exhibition design, and later did various design-related work. "Design is a pretty broad concept anyway," Mars said. Those skills were also put to use in the homestay renovation. "Soft furnishings, color matching — I can manage all that."

Before taking over the villa at Oubo Shengbaoyuan, Mars and his friends had also set their sights on a place on Huaxia East Road. Although it was nearly 10 kilometers from the Shanghai International Tourism and Resorts Zone and cellphone reception in the compound was not great, Mars was still ready to sign the lease.

But the day before they were due to sign, someone snatched the place with an offer of 23,000 yuan a month. "That guy is in the renovation business — he's pretty good at that line of work; he used to be a plumbing-and-electrical foreman," Mars said.

According to Kae's observation, landlords like Mars mostly came to the area near the Disney resort around March or April this year. Some had been watching the market for a long time, but only signed leases at that point because renting a property that sits unoccupied is a huge expense for these landlords.

Kae is Mars' neighbor. She works for an online furniture brand called Jiangwu, which took over No. 85 and converted it into a homestay.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902152521GCPSM0Uqd3asj8Fo.jpg-w600)

![The homestay run by Jiangwu](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902152531opwhVLud7ir91qe5.jpg-w600)

For Jiangwu, running a homestay was partly a matter of personal interest. The company's founder, Cai Minghang, was once Airbnb's first engineer from China. Of course, from Jiangwu's perspective, it also happened to want an offline space to showcase some of the furniture products it sells. So hiring a designer to turn a villa into a homestay, furnished with its own furniture, became a natural choice for Jiangwu.

"Since it's a company product, it would be even better if it could turn a profit. Disney was about to open anyway, so we came to this area to find a house," Kae said.

At the end of last September, Kae began house-hunting around Disney, spending a month at it. Because she was relatively early — and the villa was still a bare shell at the time — she took the place for under 10,000 yuan a month in rent. Then she hired a designer, and construction began; the renovation alone cost over 3 million yuan, while the total price of the house was only just over 10 million yuan.

"The hard renovation was very costly — central air conditioning and so on — and we even altered the layout; the yard wasn't level originally," Kae said.

Individuals like Mars and companies like Jiangwu are far from alone in hoping to do business off Shanghai Disneyland. Mars told QDaily that there are now more than 1,600 homestays in the Disney area alone, and the number keeps rising.

But not everyone gets what they hoped for. "Business here — how should I put it — is actually not very good," Kae said, doing the math: electricity alone costs more than 2,000 yuan a month; the toiletries Jiangwu provides are all sourced from Muji; there's the cleaning lady's fee, the rent for the house itself, and the salaries of the operations staff — yet occupancy is only about a third of the month.

"It's not profitable; as for recouping the renovation costs, that's basically impossible."

Kae listed many reasons for the lack of profitability. First, Jiangwu's homestay is positioned relatively high-end, and all the rooms are quite large, so it ended up with only three rooms — unlike Mars, who divided his place into seven or eight rooms.

Second, Jiangwu prefers to rent out the whole villa. A single family obviously has no need for a house this big and isn't willing to pay a price in the thousands of yuan for it. By comparison, groups of friends traveling together are more likely to rent the place, but there are far fewer of them.

Part of the blame was pinned on Shanghai Disneyland itself. "The original assumption was that Disney would be so hot that its hotels would be overbooked, and some guests who couldn't get a Disney hotel room would come to us. But Disney isn't that hot — hotel rooms are still fairly easy to book; you can get a room at the in-park hotel even two or three days in advance," Kae said.

![Shanghai Disneyland Hotel](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902152943Tv3aU0xflWrFNutK.jpg-w600)

Huang Zijie, marketing director of Keys, a homestay services company, confirmed this view. "Disney is an emerging market, and it's not far off from what we predicted. After the brief spike during the trial operation, its ability to keep setting records isn't that strong."

Keys is a company that provides services for homestays, including daily cleaning and maintenance of rooms, design and renovation advice for hosts, and private chefs and transportation for guests. It has signed full-management agreements with more than 100 homestays around Disney, helping hosts operate them. In addition, some hotels and homestays list their rooms on Keys' booking platform.

"At present, occupancy can be guaranteed at 60 to 70 percent — and that's with the peak season nearing its end," Huang said. This occupancy figure includes the 100 homestays under contract with Keys as well as hotels for which Keys only provides booking services; together they supply roughly 500 room-nights a day.

Measured against the industry average, that number might be barely acceptable. But this is Disney — a place that in theory can aggregate huge crowds, where hotel rooms should be in short supply. Although in fact, expectations may have been too optimistic.

"For this Disney, 10,000 rooms a night around the park is the absolute ceiling — that's already very, very big," Huang said. "Those 10,000 rooms span all price levels; for a tourist attraction that's a very small quantity. Some large tourist attractions have 30,000 to 50,000 rooms."

Mars told a different story: "On Chuanzhou Highway there's a hotel — the building itself is quite nice — but it has never opened. If it had opened, hotel rooms definitely wouldn't be in short supply." A hotel that has remained unopened for so long may mean that around Disney, the supply of rooms has already exceeded demand.

![The Alice in Wonderland Maze in Shanghai Disneyland](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160902153205LESQtyjIRiPlzUOC.jpg-w600)

So the question facing homestay operators like Mars and Kae is how to stand out when traffic is insufficient and competition is fierce.

Mars' experience: "Traffic is a bit below what we imagined, so we try to make up for it with service."

In the homestay Mars runs, they turned the first-floor space that was originally the kitchen into his bedroom. Mars lives there, responding to guests' needs 24 hours a day — from providing Shanghai Disneyland tips to picking up guests in his own car when they return from the park at midnight unable to find a taxi.

Then there's promotion. "Mainly we cooperate with hotel platforms and homestay platforms, trying to get more positive reviews, push our ranking up, and become a preferred merchant," Mars said — but he stresses that "these don't require paying the platforms; they're earned entirely by ourselves, through guests' good reviews." He is also considering finding some big WeChat accounts to promote the place when the off-season arrives.

These measures have worked fairly well. We ran into the auntie who cleans Mars' villa in the compound. "It's full every single day," she said, involuntarily raising her voice.

But this is still not an easy business. "A batch of homestay brands like those in Moganshan may emerge near Disney, and a batch may also be eliminated — after all, there isn't that much demand to go around," Huang said.

Mars put it this way: "After the shakeout, maybe only 30 percent will survive."

The summer holiday is over, which means the tourism off-season is coming. Mars is already worrying about his business. What he may not know is that on August 5, the "Chuansha Town Lianmin Village Characteristic Homestays" project, led by the Chuansha town government, was officially launched; the homestay renovation work has begun, and the first batch of 50 characteristic homestays will open around July next year.
