What the artist seeks to provoke is not only the viewer's definition of painting and the relationship between painting and space, but a challenge to every conventional definition and rule.

What is painting? As contemporary art moves toward ever more diverse materials, forms, and concepts, can the ancient medium of painting be redefined?

German artist Katharina Grosse's Shanghai exhibition "Mumbling Mud" attempts to offer viewers a different kind of viewing experience.

"Mumbling Mud" consists of 5 large-scale works, dividing the 1,500-square-meter space of chi K11 Art Museum into 5 sections. The large work in each section was created and completed almost entirely on site. From initial conception, the entire creative process took about a year; the installation in Shanghai took 3 weeks. The 5 works, in exhibition order, are *Underground* (《地下》), *Ghost* (《鬼魂》), *Silk Studio* (《丝绸工作室》), *In the Belly* (《腹中》), and *Showroom* (《陈列室》).

*Underground* (《地下》) is the first work visitors encounter on entering the gallery. It is composed of mounds of earth and construction materials sourced from local Shanghai markets. Visitors can walk inside the structure built from mud, steel wire, cabinets, hollow cylindrical tubes, gravel, and the like, and the route is not a single fixed path. The sprayed paint covering the materials does not follow the boundaries of the objects; it evokes a primal chaos, or a post-apocalyptic landscape — a bit like walking through construction debris, except that color gives these materials an entirely different feeling. It is named "Underground" because chi K11 Art Museum sits next to a subway entrance and occupies an underground floor of the mall.

*Ghost* is a large sculpture of intricate form, made of styrofoam, which the artist cut on site with a hot-wire cutter. Walking around the work, the viewer has no way to piece together its complete image.

*Silk Studio* consists of giant silk curtains hung on both sides of the gallery, printed with images of the walls of Katharina's Berlin studio — a full view of the studio space where the spray-gun works are created, along with reproductions of the other spaces used to make oversized or sculptural works. Katharina believes these different sections present the different conditions that bring a painting into being. "How does a painting appear in our lives? There are differences between its different modes of realization: you must leap across that gap." These images reveal traces of something that once existed in another place, perhaps trying to evoke a connection across the dimension of time.

*In the Belly* is true to its name: the viewer enters a space formed by hundreds of meters of fabric folds hanging from the museum ceiling. It is a bit like a maze, though of course there is more than one exit. The folds formed by the fabric are unevenly distributed throughout the work, like the walls of a stomach; a person inside is as if inside a belly, and painting here becomes a state of being that surrounds the viewer. Situated among the highly saturated folds of color, the viewing experience breaks the habitual mode of looking at a two-dimensional "plane on the wall."

The final work, *Showroom*, is an interior furniture arrangement sprayed with paint; as the last exhibit it refers to the shopping-arts center that houses the exhibition space. Through the paint's "re-creation" of the furniture, it undermines the high-priced objects that inspire consumer desire. *Showroom* examines the rise of the "ideal home environment" and of "model homes" in China, while hinting at people's pursuit of material things and their ideas about consumption.

Katharina Grosse was born in Germany in 1961 and began using the spray gun as her primary painting tool in 1998. She is known for large-scale three-dimensional works characterized by bright, unmixed, sprayed colors. She also brings out the potential of painting as a medium, seeking to extend painting beyond the canvas.

Her painting is not limited to canvas; it can also be eggs, train platforms, entire houses, or piles of sand, the vegetation along roadsides, and beaches. She crosses the boundaries of painting's support and changes the way pigment is used, replacing the hand's drawing with the spray gun's wide-ranging jets. ["I believe a painting can appear anywhere; it is not bound to any fixed setting. It can also be a projection and response to architecture or sculpture, and viewers can appreciate them from external space."](http://art.china.cn/talk/2017-06/14/content_39025209.htm)

But Katharina believes her painting is different from graffiti. "Graffiti usually makes a demand through writing or painting — marking a boundary, for instance. What I do is paint the boundary away; I open up space rather than enclosing it."

Katharina says she enjoys this sense of conflict between material and color. "I spray it onto the material, and the material itself is rough and sharp, so there is a sense of conflict with the color. At the same point there is a sense of contradiction. This very uncomfortable, stimulated feeling is a stimulation of the body's senses."

Most of Katharina's works have a specific relationship with the exhibition venue, and more than half of them are dismantled after the exhibition. For Katharina, the works "live in thinking and speaking" — their fate of disappearance is foretold from the moment of their creation.

In 2016, for MoMA PS1's <Rockaway!> project in New York, Katharina created the work *Rockaway!*, a surrealistic reworking of buildings destroyed by a hurricane. The piece is brightly colored and merges into its surroundings; the paint seeped into the seaside landscape around the buildings. "Rockaway!" is an outdoor art-installation biennial series launched by the Museum of Modern Art in response to Hurricane Sandy.

![*Rockaway!*, [from the MoMA website](https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/35/530)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181108004251BhjaUDivlmIrMpdt.png-w600)

Painting large-scale works requires the artist to mobilize more of her body. In video footage, Katharina can be seen fully equipped and wearing a mask, painting within a large space. "The spray gun extends my body's reach in space and makes the whole painting process more dynamic." By enlarging the amplitude and the movements of painting, she hopes "to inspire a different kind of experience of knowledge." Ulrich Loock, the curator who has collaborated with her over the long term, believes that "Katharina opens the door of our imagination and confronts our understanding of the world."

Katharina's use of color is bold and rich. She tries to leave color unrestricted and uses color to erase the boundaries between objects; this, she believes, "is the potential of color to make us think." In the video [*Painting With Color*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HBfPMGS7XPo), she describes the relationship between painting and color this way: "Since the seventeenth century, art academies in Paris and elsewhere regarded color as a feminine, unstable, unclear, incomprehensible element of painting. The concept of painting — line, drawing — was the more masculine, clear, thoughtful, comprehensible part of art. I think I am engaging with this tradition in an interesting way, because color is a very important spatial feature in my work. It is closely tied to the solid, embodied material world, and that is part of my painting in space."

Conceptually, what the artist seeks to provoke is not only the viewer's definition of painting and the relationship between painting and space, but a challenge to every conventional definition and rule.

Her exhibition in Shanghai this time is titled "Mumbling Mud." Both "mumbling" and "mud" are words for in-between states. Ulrich Loock believes this is not a description but "points to the idea that the works can actually escape clear definition." Liu Xiuyi, artistic director of KAF, says the name was inspired by the Cantonese saying "ghost eating mud," which means speaking indistinctly — an in-between state between hearing and not hearing.

In fact, as early as 1981, Katharina followed her father to Shanghai and lived here for a period. For various reasons, the two-person exhibition originally planned with the painter Ding Yi never materialized. More than 30 years later, her first exhibition in China opens on November 10 at chi K11 Art Museum Shanghai.

On November 5, Katharina Grosse held a conversation at chi K11 Art Museum with the following people.

K: Katharina Grosse

Liu: Liu Xiuyi, Artistic Director of K11 Art Foundation (KAF), curator of "Mumbling Mud"

Zheng: Zheng Guogu, artist.

Cui: Cui Jie, contemporary Chinese artist.

U: Ulrich Loock, independent curator, art critic, and lecturer; has collaborated with Katharina Grosse on multiple projects since 2007.

### On the Connection Between Space, Time, and Place

**K**: My work stretches back more than 20 years, and this time the works were all completed locally in Shanghai. I would also like to hear what my fellow artists think about the connection between space, time, and place.

**Zheng**: She could be called the "Queen of the Spray Gun" — using a spray gun to transform a space and its environment, working with the spray gun.

**Cui**: I had seen Katharina's works in images before, but never experienced them on site. Just now, seeing the exhibition in person, my first feeling was that the live experience is very different. Since I myself am primarily a painter, I have always paid attention to the relationship between painting and space, so I am interested in how to combine space and the language of painting into a new experience. There are many details on site that you cannot experience through images. In the last work, *Showroom*, I noticed a shoe print on the carpet next to the sofa. K11 said a pair of shoes had been placed there earlier and later removed (leaving the print behind). It made me think that beyond painting and space, there is yet another layer here — time.

**Liu**: This is very natural, very autonomous artistic creation. The shoe print is a home-like setup; it is intentional. Without a macro-level creative perspective, one's understanding of a work may be partial. I would like to ask Katharina: what is your way of working?

**K**: For me, texture and painting, or texture and color, are very important. We now speak of painting and color as separate things, but in German, painting and coloring are the same word. Painting is an act tied to the body; color is imagination. The relationship between the painted image and the body is an important relationship in my work. Many of our images arise in the flowing movement of color. Once we cross this threshold, there is a completely different experience. For that experience to become real, the body has to walk through it. This relates to the several layers of this exhibition, which is divided into 5 blocks — there are creations on the ground as well as installations hanging from above.

**Zheng**: I made a comparison, if everyone is interested in space and environment. When I face a space, I ask which magnetic fields disturb the human body, and I remove the part that makes the body uncomfortable. I want people to enter a space without magnetic-field disturbance. When magnetic interference equals zero, it is very comfortable. Katharina works with many kinds of colors: white relates to energy, red may stir your sensitivity — the various colors mobilize the body. Your inner energy gathering points are actually related to the external color space.

**Cui**: I mostly paint architecture. Architecture itself has always been closely tied to music. As a painter, you constantly have to consider: on a plane, what is the greatest sensory possibility you can bring to others? So I wondered whether sound and smell could bring something to the audience looking at my paintings. At that time I would turn the texture of canvas into the texture of metal, because metal also carries memories of hearing, touch, and smell.

Just now, seeing Katharina's exhibition, I could sense sound, though there was no sound on site. You see the different colors and rhythms on site and imagine how the body might move within them. I could even sense smell; as the body moves in the field, these things interweave, forming a kind of musical feeling. As for the layers of different pigments on the canvas — when you look at one point rather than the whole, you feel there is a hole behind it; you imagine whether there is green beneath the red, and whether beneath the final layer there is blankness.

### I Choose to Confront and Question My Own Imagination

**Liu**: Many reporters asked Katharina how she chooses colors. She said it is a very natural choice — "I don't apply the colors one by one; they come out as a color band, a color family." How do different senses and different ideas come together?

**K**: I see the relationship between painting and life as a rhythm, a method that lets us consider different options. What I choose is to confront and question my own imagination. I look at how many possibilities there are, what the definition of space is, and then I can challenge and question it. The space of painting is hard to define and measure; we paint with color, we define with color, and every time is different. The combination of yellow and blue, for example, is infinite and can trigger different bodily reactions. This immeasurability has to be felt by our senses.

The feeling of our body and our intelligence are a measure of reality; we constantly probe the truth with our bodily senses. My choice of a color is not improvised either — it is my feeling in that moment. The basis of my choices changes every day.

My field is very open; I feel that all the factors within it are at work. Everyone I have encountered has an influence on me and on this system.

**U**: To take one step further, I find a particularly interesting point in the wholeness — the overall sensory experience — that Zheng and Liu talked about; I feel I can sense it completely. From another angle, this kind of painting questions previously preset definitions. For example, in our gallery, if I want to know something — bookshelves, plants, chairs — we see many things. But these paintings seem to cross over one another; this painting does not follow our definitions and ideas about objects at all. Katharina opens the door of our imagination and confronts our understanding of the world — our understanding of the world is challenged. I redefine possibility through painting, because for me, we have no obligation to accept conventional definitions and rules. We should learn about them and understand them, but in the end, we have no obligation to accept things that others have defined. For me, this is a very good example of opening a door of possibility. I now have more options; as a viewer, I have freedom to move within these possibilities.

### Not Following Conventional Definitions — That Is the Meaning of "Mumbling"

**Liu**: Within the exhibition's 5 areas, we often forget where we are. That is possibility. This is her exhibition about space, her experience. We often look at her layout plan, every day, but sometimes we ourselves get lost in it. Being undefined — not defining, not following conventional definitions — that is the meaning of "mumbling." Mumbling is the act of speaking without actually saying anything.

Katharina came to China as early as the 1980s, but the earlier exhibition plan never came to pass. Only 30 years later was the exhibition realized. Our exchange is also mumbling and continuous — a blurred state that cannot be fully defined.

Shanghai is a place where culture gathers. For me, it is an interesting platform of opportunity — how different things coexist and blend. "Mumbling Mud" is a bit of a tongue-twister in Chinese. What impression do you have of this title? What kind of feeling does it give you?

**Zheng**: What confuses me is not possibility or indeterminacy, but how to appreciate something from abroad. Many years ago there was an Eastern standard, which is outdated today; now a Western standard has been added. But I think both Eastern and Western standards will become outdated. Later there may well be a Northern standard. Artists who remain in history — to use today's phrase — are the ones who "speak to the heart"; you see their work and you are moved. External phenomena and standards become outdated. When confusion arises, the artist should return to their own heart, and then they are no longer confused.

This is Katharina's discovery — she discovered the spray gun — and it gave rise to this exhibition. Once you return to the collective body, it produces a resonance with this era.

**Cui**: Mumbling — a whispering feeling. I see that the colors Katharina uses, such as pure lemon yellow and emerald green, are very hard to find in nature; they are refined, very chemical colors, yet they are re-sprayed onto mud, construction materials, and everyday materials. My experience is one of conflict.

**K**: I find this sense of opposition and conflict very interesting. My paintings don't use watercolor; I spray onto the material, and the material itself is rough and sharp, so there is a sense of conflict with the color. At the same point there is a sense of contradiction. I am very interested in this very uncomfortable, stimulated feeling — it is a stimulation of the body's senses.

I think this sense of conflict, and of harmony, is an acceptance of different things. We can see it and at the same time see the contradiction. Attaching different materials and conflicting things to the same surface — that is what makes painting interesting. One of my thoughts is the hope to purify painting, so that its appearance and the signs of its emergence feel different from before. I hope for a sense of leaping, as if jumping into an unknown world, like a quantum leap. This is my understanding of the sense of contradiction.

At the same time, I think when you make that leap, there is a boundary — between things previously fixed in impression and feelings previously unfixed — and a process of redefinition and re-stabilization takes place.

**U**: "Mumbling" and "mud" are both in-between states; we have no very clear way to define them. This title is attractive not because it describes something, but because she points out that the works can actually escape clear definition. Perhaps we don't like mud, and we would even avoid the state of mumbling. Although these two words carry negative associations, they also open up more possibilities for interpretation; they are an embodiment of our mind's obsessions. Sometimes, in a life framed by formulaic definitions, we are obliged to define. But the more we are in such an environment, the more we must break through, the more freedom we need — we must throw off all kinds of definitions and delimitations. This is a positive escape. The exhibition's 5 sections also contain this state. A solo exhibition is an artist's use of this opportunity to combine their own ideas with space, gathering the sense of conflict with their own strength.

**K**: We came up with this title together in Berlin. As for what Zheng mentioned about Eastern and Western cultural influences and following one's own heart — this is not a theoretical discussion but an exploration in other ways. Our cultural influences are often not fixed; they are variable and can be constantly explored and pondered.

**Zheng**: Katharina speaks of jumping out — how to jump out of the Eastern and Western phenomena. Sometimes jumping out is easy. For example, in the early 1990s I also used a spray gun — a very small one — to render design sketches, but I only used it to solve problems in commercial work. Seeing Katharina's work, I think she has probably broken free of the constraints the previous generation imposed on her.
