FIFA is mired in scandal and the advertising landscape has been turned upside down. Following the Olympics, the World Cup now faces enormous pressure as well.

FIFA finds itself in an awkward position.

Less than a month before the Russia World Cup kicks off, at least 16 sponsor slots are still unfilled.

It can either slash prices to dump the unsold slots, or simply leave them empty to protect the interests and face of existing partners — but pile an even heavier burden on a FIFA that has been losing money for years.

Either option seems awkward.

FIFA's sponsorship recruitment plan has three tiers:

- Tier one: 6-8 global partners;
- Tier two: 6-8 World Cup sponsors;
- Tier three: 20 regional sponsors.

At the moment, only five of the tier-two slots have been sold, and 15 tier-three slots remain empty. In Asia, for instance, Yadea is the only tier-three regional sponsor.

According to a price list circulating outside FIFA, the sponsorship fees for a World Cup cycle are roughly £100 million, £50 million, and £8 million by tier. FIFA now faces a shortfall of about 20% against its sponsorship target.

For any World Cup in history, this would be unthinkable. For the last World Cup in Brazil, the recruitment target had been met six months before kickoff.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180518131640hFebkExtrXATdc9R.png-w600)

The prediction of [Patrick Nally, the father of modern sports marketing, is coming true](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/media-and-marketing/fifa-toxic-brand-to-cost-it-25-of-world-cup-sponsor-target-expert-says-1.3136003): corruption scandals have tarnished the FIFA brand, and 25% of the target sponsorship revenue will evaporate into thin air. As the architect of FIFA's sponsorship system, Nally brought Coca-Cola to the World Cup stage in the 1970s.

[FIFA's 2016 financial report estimated](https://resources.fifa.com/mm/document/affederation/footballgovernance/02/87/89/44/fr2016digitalen_neutral.pdf) that the Russia World Cup would need to bring in $4 billion to turn a surplus. Based on data from the 2014 Brazil World Cup, advertising sponsorship accounted for 25%-30% of revenue, broadcasting rights sales for 60%-65%, and the remaining 10% came from ticket and merchandise sales.

Broadcasting rights for this World Cup have been a hard sell too. Russian TV stations were for a time unwilling to accept FIFA's asking price.

The absurd scene of the host nation's people being unable to watch the World Cup did not come to pass: at the end of last year FIFA reached a broadcast deal with Russia's 2SPORT2 consortium. The whole process was kept extremely low-key — in the end, they wouldn't even disclose the signing price.

For reference, FIFA's asking price a year earlier was $120 million, [which directly led to the collapse of negotiations](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/39881.html); the Russian side refused because the price was nearly four times the 2014 level.

This is a completely different picture from 2011, when US broadcaster FOX signed a whopping $425 million broadcast deal with FIFA seven years in advance (covering the 2018 and 2022 World Cups). Of course, with the US team's unexpected elimination this time around, FOX has already been jokingly called one of the biggest losers of this World Cup.

In today's TV market, it is already no small feat that FIFA's lifeline — broadcasting rights — can still fetch such high prices, but asking for more is probably not an option.

### Nine years on, Russia's relationship with the rest of the world has changed considerably

Five months ago, [we analyzed the major dilemmas facing the Russia World Cup](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/47937.html): FIFA mired in scandal, over-inflated brand premiums, the decline of TV channels, the absence of popular teams and of the United States, a major advertising spender… And with the next host, Qatar, looking even less reliable, brands that had maintained long-term partnerships also hesitated when it came to renewing.

None of these problems has improved over the past half year, but surprisingly, World Cup sponsorship recruitment has not made further progress as the date draws near — in fact, with kickoff now impossibly close, no one can say how it will all end, or whether regional sponsors will really take the field largely "streaking" with no backing.

Some solid signs indicate that FIFA's plan to expand sponsorship recruitment has failed to hit its targets — and that this will only get harder going forward.

Since 2011, not a single American company, nor any European company outside Russia, has joined the ranks of World Cup sponsors. The new sponsors are all Middle Eastern, Chinese, and Russian companies. Germany's Continental, Britain's Castrol, and America's Johnson & Johnson all chose not to renew. The new tier-one and tier-two sponsors are Qatar Airways, Gazprom, China's Wanda, vivo, Hisense, and Mengniu.

The Russia World Cup, seen through a geopolitical lens, also forces American and European brands to weigh the risks beyond the pitch. One CEO, who asked not to be named, said: "Even if the World Cup can attract 1 billion viewers, faced with a nine-figure price tag and all sorts of risks, brands do not consider FIFA and Russia trustworthy."

As for the current domestic atmosphere in Russia, it truly is nothing like when it bid for the World Cup nine years ago.

In 2009, the year before the bid, the Russian stock market was at an all-time high, and US-Russia relations were in a stable period. But afterward, Russia's posture in the wars in Ukraine and Syria drew constant condemnation from Europe and America; EU economic sanctions on Russia run until July 31, 2018. American politics is being torn apart by Trump, but whether his supporters or his opponents, whether or not they believe Russia influenced the election, everyone has come to dislike the country Putin rules even more.

Oleh Lyashko, leader of Ukraine's Radical Party, even proposed recently that legislation be passed to ban domestic broadcasts of the World Cup, describing the Russia World Cup broadcast as "propaganda by the aggressor."

Compared with the Medvedev era, the domestic environment in Russia is also tenser. Dozhd, the country's last independent TV station, vanished from public view in 2014. Putin was elected president once again, websites and apps have been blocked en masse, and protests inside Russia have hardly let up this year.

At the same time, [a rights group released a report](https://www.rferl.org/a/ahead-of-world-cup-rights-watchdog-points-to-repression-discrimination-in-russia/29227578.html) fiercely attacking Russia's gay propaganda ban, labor abuses, and crackdown on rights activists. An Argentine TV station even produced a satirical ad mocking Russia's attitude toward LGBT people.

Dan Wiersema works for a sports tourism company; he said Russia's gay propaganda ban has indeed drawn customers' attention, and that British customers traveling to Russia for the World Cup have dropped 50% compared with the last edition. Although Russian officials keep stressing that LGBT people will not be targeted during the World Cup, [a survey by a football betting site showed](https://bonuscodebets.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/World-Cup-Report_01.02.18.pdf) that nearly 40% of respondents worry that LGBT people will not enjoy freedom and equal rights while in Russia.

Thirty-one days before the Russia World Cup opening, Russia denied entry to a German journalist who had exposed Russia's doping scandal. The German government reacted strongly, hoping FIFA would step in, so that "a carnival for everyone" would not become an empty phrase.

All in all, type Chinese or English keywords for the Russia World Cup into a search engine and you will see two completely different opinion environments. Doping, discrimination, football hooliganism — such words fill the headlines of English-language media, while Chinese media is all harmony. Given that this is the World Cup with the highest Chinese participation since the 2002 Korea-Japan World Cup, this is easy to understand.

### Changes in media are casting more doubt on the value of World Cup sponsorship

We are not talking only about eight- or nine-figure sponsorship fees here. To make that money burn with value, brands have to pour in more media resources afterward — not just perimeter boards and TV ads, but also mobile, out-of-home, roadshows, anywhere it can catch your eye.

In 2014, WeChat had only just begun selling Moments ads, Weibo's ad sales were still at a very rudimentary stage, and the booming short-video apps Douyin and Kuaishou did not yet exist. The US was the same: Facebook openly poached ads from primetime TV slots, arguing that viewers would be scrolling social networks during the ad breaks anyway.

All of this is very different from four years ago; capturing consumers' attention is now much harder.

Take the media-buying data Hisense has published: beyond the advertising rights granted by FIFA, Hisense also placed products in two CCTV programs, 《我爱世界杯》(*I Love the World Cup*) and 《豪门盛宴》(*A Grand Feast*); in out-of-home advertising, it placed 268 media units across 13 airports and 17 high-speed rail stations, 240,000 elevator-frame ads in 45 cities, plus inflight-magazine ads on the four airlines Air China, China Eastern, China Southern, and Shandong Airlines — a dense bombing campaign over the month-long tournament.

Beyond that, Hisense also set up more than 50 offline "Road to Russia" theme events at home and overseas, showing fans Hisense's products and brand strength.

All of this is beyond FIFA's control. Put simply, they now want to sell the World Cup at a higher price, but audience attention is slipping away like sand through their fingers.

During Euro 2016, [a report by the marketing firm RadiumOne pointed out](http://www.campaignlive.co.uk/article/people-associate-nike-euro-2016-official-sponsors-carlsberg-mcdonalds/1395469) that many brands frequently associated with the Euros are not actually sponsors at all.

The same is true of the World Cup: Nike, a master of "ambush marketing," does not need to be an official sponsor — yet every World Cup year it manages to fight Adidas to a draw on advertising creativity while cleverly sidestepping FIFA's restrictions.

Fully protecting the rights of official sponsors and shutting down other brands' "ambush marketing" is now nearly impossible; neither the IOC nor FIFA has a good answer.

In this changed environment, FIFA chose instead to create new sponsorship tiers and pull in more advertising money.

This is a last resort. Management corruption and years of lawsuits have hollowed out FIFA, and the new president's "appeasement" of his backers cost another large sum (for details on FIFA's financial crisis, [see our earlier report](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/39650.html)). FIFA operates on a four-year cycle, and from 2015 to 2017 it has already lost more than $1 billion — all of it counting on the 2018 World Cup to win it back in one go.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018051815421760XvhpFcknJWjoGw.jpg-w600)

The tier-three regional sponsorship rights — where recruitment has struggled the most — are the new trick this World Cup dreamed up to generate revenue. In the past this tier was open only to companies from the host country; this year it was expanded to five regions worldwide, with slots growing from 4 to 20.

In terms of on-site visibility, which sponsors value most, regional supporters get relatively few rights: in the perimeter LED boards at the World Cup, they appear as a joint advertisement with the other regional supporters from the same region, for one minute per match.

Honestly, how many *Idol Producer* trainees can you sign for $10 million? There is no shortage of traffic-drawing stars to choose from at home and abroad. Why squeeze in with a pile of logos for a fleeting flash during the live broadcast? Do the math and the answer is obvious.

The value for money of this sponsorship is so poor that even Chinese companies — notoriously big spenders on marketing — are hesitating. Yadea is currently the only announced regional sponsor for Asia.

### The big spenders are all Chinese sponsors, but their goals are quite different

"No one except a Chinese company would think it unimportant that FIFA faces corruption investigations in a New York courtroom." Patrick Nally was not mincing words.

But for Chinese companies with clear goals, the World Cup is the world's top-tier sports asset, and also their fastest, most effective route.

The Chinese companies sponsoring the World Cup are mostly mass consumer brands. For ordinary fans, the focus is the sport itself. "Big IP like this isn't found everywhere; we first look at what it can bring, not go looking for flaws," said Zhu Shuqin, director of the brand department at Hisense Group.

Hisense currently has branches in 20 regions around the world, and Hisense vice president Lin Lan has repeatedly stressed in speeches that "the bulk of Hisense's future growth lies overseas." Last year Hisense spent 800 million yuan to acquire Toshiba's TV business; riding the World Cup, Hisense entered Russia, an emerging market, while Australia, Japan, South Africa, and many European and American countries are its key overseas markets — among them, South Africa has a 23.7% volume share and Australia 19.9%, both ranking first.

[According to data from this past February](https://news.znds.com/article/30318.html), Hisense's shipments rank fourth globally. Hisense's financial reports show that its main business is still the domestic market, with overseas markets its huge profit growth point at present.

"In sports marketing, going early doesn't work — no one sees your stuff; going late doesn't work either — you miss the golden window." Lin Lan believes Hisense is fully prepared in channels and products. The effect of its Euro 2016 sponsorship also left Hisense highly satisfied and, to some extent, spurred the later World Cup sponsorship.

According to CSM data, across the 51 matches of Euro 2016 in France, Hisense's ads received 415 seconds of exposure per match, for a cumulative 21,165 seconds. Calculated at the unit price of a 15-second ad in the same time slots before and after kickoff and at halftime, the advertising value equaled 570 million yuan. By this accounting, Hisense believes its sponsorship had already paid for itself.

A consumer electronics company's brand equity is built through long-term accumulation — the same was true of Samsung's and Sony's investments in the Olympics and the World Cup. "We believe the World Cup sponsorship path is proven, and for us the experience of our peers is well worth learning from," said Zhu Shuqin.

World Cup sponsorship follows a same-industry exclusion principle. It was precisely because former home-appliance giant Sony withdrew that Hisense joined the sponsor ranks. Zhu Shuqin revealed that when FIFA screened suppliers, it conducted due diligence on Hisense for more than a month — "at minimum comparing it with Sony, whether it could fill the gap."

![Vivo hopes the World Cup will help promote its products](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180518154514KCHwYd7AU3Rje5X8.jpg-w600)

vivo, which likewise hopes to break through in overseas markets, is less optimistic. According to people familiar with the matter, vivo's efforts to win coverage from foreign media did not go smoothly: apart from trade media and PR consultancy sites, few foreign mainstream outlets reported vivo's World Cup sponsorship. QDaily separately asked Hisense and Mengniu, and in neither case did foreign media actively seek to report on it.

"We focus on communication to the domestic market and domestic consumers. The World Cup has no direct impact on our overseas markets," said a person from Mengniu Group's corporate affairs department. Mengniu's overseas sales markets are currently Mongolia, Singapore, Myanmar, and Cambodia — "mainly along the Belt and Road. This time our products can't be sold directly into Russia; it's not that we don't want to. We made some efforts in this area last year, but there are still some restrictions."

On December 20 last year, Mengniu announced that it had become an official sponsor of the 2018 Russia World Cup — the first dairy brand sponsor in World Cup history. You will notice that Chinese companies love stressing this in their press releases: the "first dairy brand sponsor," the "first two-wheeled electric vehicle category sponsor" (Yadea is currently FIFA's only regional sponsor in Asia). Hisense was a latecomer in consumer electronics sponsorship, but add "Chinese" and it too became the "first Chinese consumer electronics brand sponsor."

The Messi World Cup ad Mengniu is pushing

Within a month of announcing the sponsorship, Mengniu switched to brand-new packaging as a FIFA sponsor; in February it signed Messi as brand spokesperson — reportedly landing this top star's endorsement at a relatively bargain price thanks to the sponsorship rights; in March it launched a scan-the-code campaign for World Cup products. A brand that has sponsored NBA China for more than a decade, Mengniu has long invested in sports marketing and fights fiercely with rival Yili, which won the dairy sponsor slot for the Beijing Olympics.

"Whether we will invest long-term is a decision for our leadership; personally, I think it definitely depends on the results," said the person from Mengniu Group's corporate affairs department. "Within our means, the World Cup is a resource that an ordinary company would not give up."

As for Wanda, which landed a tier-one sponsorship, it comes closest to the "politicized sponsorship" that foreign media speculate about — Chinese companies spending money to build influence within the FIFA system in order to help China host a World Cup. See Wang Jianlin's earlier remarks in public: he divides the sports industry into A, B, and C ends, with the top A end being international organizations in the sports industry — organizations like FIFA.

"On the surface I'm buying the sports industry, but at a deeper level, we want to use the sports industry to make related industries like tourism bigger," [Wang Jianlin said at a forum in 2016](http://www.eeo.com.cn/2016/0101/282225.shtml). "For example, if we bring two major international events to China, then only our own travel companies would have the right to operate those events, and only our own travel websites would have the right to sell the tickets."

Only with FIFA sponsorship rights in hand can you get a say. Wanda hosts the only FIFA A-level tournament named the "China Cup," recognized by FIFA's ranking system. Although the inaugural cup ended in a crushing defeat for the Chinese team, it demonstrates the intent behind Wanda's FIFA sponsorship — its designs go much deeper.

But all of this was Wanda's plan before 2017. In 2017, under the influence of various factors, Wanda became a completely different company, [and probably no longer needs to think that far ahead](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/46055.html).

### If sponsorship falls short, it will ultimately affect the tournament itself

Football remains the world's number one sport, and the World Cup remains the world's most influential sporting event. [Its average daily revenue is slightly below the Olympics', but the tournament runs a third longer](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/37171.html). In ratings, comparing the 2014 World Cup with the 2016 Rio Olympics: China is not a football power, yet [CCTV's Olympics ratings still don't match the World Cup's](https://www.zhihu.com/question/24409954).

But even a sports company like Adidas hopes to downplay the image of football as a single sport, hoping to expand the tournament's reach in a World Cup year to more consumers beyond football fans.

This strategy was already proposed at the Brazil World Cup four years ago; this year, in the ad to which Adidas devoted the most media resources, it assembled more than 50 signed stars from around the world — not only athletes, but also arts celebrities and fashion opinion leaders.

Adidas produced over a hundred different versions, ensuring that the ad viewers see in different markets is full of familiar faces. Overseas, ad delivery with Google's help automatically generates a customized version based on user behavior; but because of Google limitations in China, Adidas pre-edited more than a dozen versions for the Chinese market.

Adidas's World Cup marketing timeline is keyed mainly to the TANGO grassroots league, which kicks off in April. "We don't want to fight local Chinese companies for ad time after the World Cup starts," an Adidas representative said.

![The Adidas Tango grassroots league](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180518154430HQVTvR58xnZKtW3y.jpg-w600)

Sponsors like Adidas, which has backed the World Cup for decades, are starting to look toward more sports. And FIFA, under revenue pressure, loosening the threshold for sponsoring companies may also hurt the commercial value of the tournament.

The nature of World Cup sponsorship is shifting: long-term partnerships may decrease, while short-term, goal-specific partners increase.

Whether or not it hits its $4 billion revenue target this year, FIFA, losing more and more money, cannot stop expanding its business.

To hit that target, it is not only event-sponsor thresholds that must be lowered.

The 2026 World Cup has already been confirmed to expand, with the final-tournament field growing from the current 32 teams to 48. More countries qualifying means more countries' companies may consider paying a sponsorship fee. But this will inevitably let in somewhat weaker teams.

FIFA will also soon vote on a $25 billion proposal — the first time it has considered selling ownership of its own tournaments. Investment funds from the Middle East and Asia are willing to pay $25 billion for an expanded version of FIFA's Club World Cup, plus a national-team league still in the proposal stage.

What will the future World Cup look like? FIFA seems to no longer have the bandwidth to worry about what comes next.
