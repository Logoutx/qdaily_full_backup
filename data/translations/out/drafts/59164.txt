"It sounds like I'm too kindhearted a person — I'm not that kindhearted. I'd rather he said this is someone who created a fantasy world."

In 2010, Kevin Tsai published his first practical book, 《蔡康永的说话之道》 (*Kevin Tsai's Way with Words*), an attempt to teach people how to "talk." Four years later, he published the sequel, *Way with Words 2*. Combined sales of the two books approached 4 million copies, and the man past fifty became a bestselling author of practical books — the very thing he had despised as a child.

Compared with when he was young, he now feels that writing books is a process of "probing himself." If a book he writes happens to let the people who read it take it and probe themselves in turn, then the book was worth writing. So practical books have their reason to exist — at minimum they can help people solve a problem or two. The same holds, for him, for doing shows.

Starting with 《蔡康永的说话之道》, he also stopped wanting to assert the existence of "I," hoping instead to write from other people's standpoints. Before that, he had published eight books, including 《痛快日记》 (*Catharsis Diaries*), 《LA流浪记》 (*LA Drifting*), and 《那些男孩教我的事》 (*What Those Boys Taught Me*). Most of those books carried a strong personal imprint — about his growth, schooling, social life, and ideas.

People who like Tsai's writing mostly find his books comforting, able to help resolve some of life's problems; those who dislike them find them shallow — chicken soup for the soul that doesn't really solve anything. [He himself responded to this](http://news.ifeng.com/gundong/detail_2013_01/11/21089934_0.shtml): "I don't think my Weibo is 'chicken soup for the soul.' What I write is not all optimistic — there is sadness and confusion too. I never write things like 'don't hate others' or 'hard work always pays off' that I don't believe in myself. I write what I believe."

More than as a writer, Tsai is known to the public as a host. He has won Best Variety Show Host at the Golden Bell Awards (Taiwan's top television prize) and has hosted the Golden Horse Awards (Taiwan's top film prize) ceremony seven times. His best-known shows are the Taiwanese TV variety show 《康熙来了》 (*Kang Xi Lai Le*) and the mainland online variety show 《奇葩说》 (*Qipa Shuo*). Above all, *Kang Xi Lai Le*, which he co-hosted with Little S (Dee Hsu) for 12 years, had wide influence across the Chinese-speaking world and a huge fan base.

But before he began hosting the entertainment show 《康熙来了》 in 2004, Tsai was actually a cultural-program host — he hosted the celebrity interview show 《真情指数》 (*The Sincerity Index*) and the reading program 《今天不读书》 (*No Reading Today*), among others.

[His friend, the journalist Sisy Chen, once said](https://yuedu.163.com/book_reader/9b26ae9bc95e4acab0701b130518f968_4/96a1e91b6d0a47cca6a6b7ba216a5da5_5): "I think Kang-yung has a certain ability. First, empathy for others; second, a very small ego; third, although you studied English literature and film, when necessary you can understand — and are willing to stoop to doing — *Kang Xi Lai Le*."

But he disagreed with Sisy Chen's view that he had stooped to do *Kang Xi Lai Le*, [responding](https://yuedu.163.com/book_reader/9b26ae9bc95e4acab0701b130518f968_4/96a1e91b6d0a47cca6a6b7ba216a5da5_5): "In the Analects, Confucius goes to chat with farmers and gardeners, saying that when it comes to the techniques of farming, I am not as good as an old farmer; when it comes to the techniques of growing flowers, I am not as good as an old gardener. Confucius also said: do not promote a man on account of his words, and do not dismiss a man on account of his words. You shouldn't revere someone's character just because he said one fine thing, nor refuse to believe what someone says just because he's a jerk. I'm someone who really likes talking with people outside the academy. I don't think you necessarily have to learn something from everyone — just getting to see what there is to see is fun enough."

Regarding Tsai's hosting style, some find him gentle, refined, clever, and witty; others find him ill-fitting and listless. In fact, both sides exist, and they are largely tied to his life experience.

Tsai was born into an eminent family, well-off. His grandfather ran the Shanghai Waterworks; his father, Cai Tianduo, was a famous lawyer and, before 1949, the owner of China's largest shipping company, the Shanghai Zhonglian Steamship Company. The luxury passenger liner Taiping, which sank in 1949, belonged to that company.

In an interview with Sisy Chen, [Tsai recalled the family's important influence on him](https://yuedu.163.com/book_reader/9b26ae9bc95e4acab0701b130518f968_4/96a1e91b6d0a47cca6a6b7ba216a5da5_5): "An old-fashioned Shanghai family, arriving in Taiwan — the atmosphere I grew up in made me feel as if I were living a secondhand life, and left me unable to muster interest in many things. As a child, whenever my father entertained guests at home, if a yellow croaker was served, my father would take one bite and say it was roughly three times worse than the yellow croaker he had eaten in Shanghai. If we went to see Peking opera and the White Snake somersaulted down from atop two tables perfectly, and we applauded and cheered, my father would sigh and say that in Shanghai the White Snake could somersault down from three tables. Once my father took me out for ice cream, and we ran into a hunchbacked old lady; my father greeted her, and after she left he told me she had been the most beautiful woman in Qingdao. I said she was now a person like a shrimp — and she had once been the most beautiful woman in Qingdao. That I found truly unbearable."

[According to Southern Weekly](http://www.infzm.com/content/127328), Cai Tianduo, a Shanghainese, was famously hospitable and drilled his son in the art of receiving guests from an early age. When the family entertained, Tsai's father would keep watch for any guest being neglected. Later, when Tsai became a host, he likewise treated everyone who came to "Kang Xi" as a guest: "On TV it's like throwing a PARTY and having a great time — a lot of the time it's because I care very much whether the guests are happy."

But like many children of wealthy families, Tsai was rebellious from a young age, hoping to break free of the predetermined world his family had given him. In 2012, [in an interview with the mainland edition of GQ](http://www.gq.com.cn/celebrity/news_1714c3ec6b3c25e0-4.html), he also named "rebellion" his "most distinctive personal style, or personal marker." "Rebellion is an important source of vitality for me. The thought of being a docile person depresses me. I like everything new; sameness depresses me," he told GQ.

For example, in high school a classmate introduced him to the works of Yin Haiguang and Hayek. At the time, he learned about the February 28 Incident and discovered that Taiwan, too, had poor people and laborers — a discovery that shocked, stimulated, and shamed him. He subsequently published articles in the school journal about the truth of the February 28 Incident and *The Communist Manifesto*, and took part in radical activities, earning repeated disciplinary punishments. But perhaps out of deference to his father, the school never expelled him outright.

So it is not hard to understand why, after graduating in 1990 with a master's degree from the film and television program at the University of California, Los Angeles, he went on to hold all kinds of jobs — college teacher, columnist, film screenwriter and director, film-festival juror, creative director at a radio station, founding editor-in-chief of the Taiwan edition of GQ, clothing and women's-shoe designer, collector, host, and maker of performance and installation artworks, among others. And he often says he has no sense of mission or responsibility, doesn't like lecturing people or influencing them, and is a person who loves ease and hates hard work. Sisy Chen had wanted to draw some conclusion about his life but in the end didn't say it; he guesses it would have been "go with the flow."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812102314257eiMFNIbx5g2aj6v.jpg-w600)

The official end of *Kang Xi Lai Le* in 2016 was seen by many as a marker of Taiwanese variety TV's decline, but long before the show ended, the mainland entertainment industry had been steadily rising while the Hong Kong and Taiwan entertainment industries slowly declined. The reasons are complex: the mainland's advantages in capital, market, and talent; creative exhaustion in Hong Kong and Taiwan; differences in advertising restrictions; changes in media forms. One factor many love to cite is the huge gap in production budgets — shows like *I Am a Singer* and *The Voice of China* have a budget of 10 million yuan per episode, while *Kang Xi Lai Le* had a per-episode budget of only about 100,000 yuan (excluding Tsai's and Little S's fees) — and even that was the highest production budget of any talk show in Taiwan.

So starting in 2014, Tsai mainly shifted his career to the mainland in search of new changes — appearing in online variety shows such as *Qipa Shuo*, 《真相吧！花花万物》 (*Go Ahead! Flowers and Everything*), and 《男子甜点俱乐部》 (*Men's Dessert Club*), and releasing his first film, 《"吃吃"的爱》 (*Didi's Dreams*).

But apart from *Qipa Shuo*, the other projects performed poorly. 《真相吧！花花万物》 (2018), 《男子甜点俱乐部》 (2017), and 《"吃吃"的爱》 (2017) scored 4.2, 5.6, and 5.3 respectively on Douban; 《"吃吃"的爱》, with a budget of 250 million New Taiwan dollars (about 56 million yuan), earned only 185 million New Taiwan dollars (about 41 million yuan) at the box office — failing both commercially and with critics.

Unlike *Kang Xi Lai Le*, many longtime viewers feel they see a different Kevin Tsai in *Qipa Shuo*. On *Kang Xi Lai Le*, the role Tsai played was interviewer — what it required was asking questions and listening, with little need to reveal himself — but on *Qipa Shuo*, he has to express himself, persuade others, and wield his influence. For instance, on the 2015 season of *Qipa Shuo*, Tsai uncharacteristically spoke on the show about the subject of his own coming out, weeping uncontrollably, which moved many viewers, who found him gentle yet powerful.

"Whenever anyone in show business is thinking of coming out, they come ask me first. Out of rational consideration, I usually stop them. Because they haven't been through what I've been through, I can't be sure they would hold up the way I did when they encountered those difficulties. But from the standpoint of loneliness, I really wish more people would keep me company. The more people come out, the harder it is for that 'closet' to exist at all. The one thing I can do is prove to mothers and fathers that we are not monsters — that we can live well in this world," he said.

![A screenshot of the variety show Kang Xi Lai Le, with Little S (left) and Kevin Tsai (right). Source: [Douban](https://img3.doubanio.com/view/photo/raw/public/p2310153093.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812102317491F0CmP4du375hvIs.jpg-w600)

Though he often says he has no sense of mission or responsibility, Tsai — regarded as a literatus within the entertainment world — still conveys his values through his shows and books at the right moments, and recommends books he loves. Reading and writing are also the two things he has always loved. At the same time, he keeps a distance from entertainment and reflects on it, hoping people will watch more good films, read more good books, and learn to be alone.

In November 2018, Tsai published his third practical book, 《蔡康永的情商课》 (*Kevin Tsai's EQ Class*). "The process of exploring EQ is the process of exploring yourself. The ability to be alone is the key ability that makes us different from other people. Why is a good book or a good film often praised as 'thought-provoking'? Because when our minds are impoverished and cannot generate energy on their own, the rich mind hidden in the book or the film keeps catalyzing us, letting imagination be born toward life," he writes in the new book.

Compared with all the things he does, Tsai says what he really wants to do is write — and to write a good, long novel. One of his plans is a long fantasy novel; he wants to create a fantasy world closely tied to the real one. But he has been writing this fantasy novel for 12 years, has started it over five times, and still hasn't finished it.

"If my tombstone were to carry one line, I wouldn't need to be introduced as: this is a man who talked about EQ. I'd rather he said this is a person who created a fantasy world. You're right about the weakness — how am I to set all this aside and go write that novel? Or should I write both? I don't think a person can manage both. So maybe the next time you interview me, I'll be starting my fantasy novel for the eighth time," the 56-year-old Tsai told QDaily.

**Q = QDaily**

**Tsai = Kevin Tsai**

**Q: *The EQ Class* is your third practical book. How do you see its connection to and difference from the first two? Has your thinking changed? Because I saw that in a 2012 interview you said that although *Way with Words* sold well, you told yourself not to write *Way with Words 2* or *Way with Words 3* — that you wanted to know and do more other things.**

**Tsai:** That's right. With this EQ book, they told me eight years had already passed since *Way with Words* — I was genuinely startled; I hadn't realized it had been that long. As a child I really didn't want to become a practical-book author. Going into a bookstore and seeing the bestseller list on the wall, I would feel angry — I thought these practical books occupied the best positions in the store and squeezed the books by the serious literary writers I revered into the corners. Later I tried reading some serious literature myself, and found that when they kept confirming to me things I already knew, I began to think: I don't actually need to be endlessly reminded of things I already know.

I watch a lot of films that won prizes at Cannes or Venice, films that keep shooting the ugliness of human nature and the emptiness of life. When I was about seventeen or eighteen, I was grateful for those films. Because back then I felt: why had no one told me these truths about life? Everyone pretended to paper things over, as if living were so interesting, so full of joy. So watching those painful works back then was especially exciting — I felt someone had enlightened me, that someone stood on my side and was going to expose this hypocritical world.

Now when I watch them, I think: fine, I know all that already — can you stop bothering me with it? Those works still suit people of seventeen or eighteen who are just beginning to struggle and wrestle with life. But now, instead, I feel that if what you write can help people solve a problem or two, that is more merit than telling people "life has problems."

I don't think everyone who writes practical books gets readers' support — many write things readers may not like. When I wrote *Way with Words*, I didn't know whether I was suited to do this. After *Way with Words* was recognized, I was very surprised. I had actually written *Way with Words* in a very funny frame of mind; when I wrote many things in it, the tone I used was one of joking around. So when it was accepted, I thought: so there really are this many people troubled by the matter of talking. I truly hadn't expected it.

Four years after *Way with Words* came out, I wrote *Way with Words 2*. I thought: this is something that could be written endlessly. But I didn't want to keep writing it. I think talking is a matter of building a relationship with other people, but what I care about more is building a relationship with myself. Because I've met many smart people who are all very good at building relationships with others, and not very good at building relationships with themselves. So I thought: if I dare to write this book, it will test me far more than *Way with Words* did. Because when I wrote *Way with Words* I knew very well what I was doing — I had been doing that for a long time. But facing myself — even I am uneasy about whether I can write a good enough book.

First I have to solve my own problems, so that I feel more certain when facing myself; only then do I have the face to show the book to others. So having written this book this time, I breathed a sigh of relief — it turns out there is a way out of this. Because as a child, while I didn't believe in practical books, I also didn't believe in this thing called EQ. How could it be that easy? I had read so many literary works about the darkness of human nature and the cruelty of the world, and then you want to tell me it can be resolved by a single turn of one's own mind? As a kid I would definitely have been furious — wanted to blow up the whole world, let humanity perish, stop bothering the animals and let them live in peace; I thought that would feel great. But now you know: we're not trying to become a great demon king and blow up the world. You just have to deal with a single target — yourself. So let's deal with it and see. And then I found that EQ is about this — not, as we mistakenly thought, being very good at dealing with people and erasing all emotion. It's not that. And I was delighted, feeling that this is the EQ worth introducing.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210232311zMxEGn4iDZqPSuOe.jpg-w600)

**Q: From what I've read, what you really want to do is write — and to write good, long novels, one of the plans being a long fantasy novel. I'm curious: how far along is this fantasy novel? Roughly what kind of story is it? And why do you want to write it?**

**Tsai:** It's actually always in progress. Each time I write about 10,000 to 20,000 characters, I think of an even better opening and overturn it, so this has already happened five times. Right now I'm in the fifth version. I genuinely like this opening a lot this time, and I hope to keep writing from it.

My idea for a fantasy story is that I want it to create a world. Going into that world, we would feel more things to look forward to, more surprises. A fantasy world has to be closely tied to our real world, not detached from it — only then will readers happily feel that the fantasy world is not far from us. That's the current direction.

**Q: When did you first start writing it?**

**Tsai:** About 12 years ago. In between, books like *Way with Words* and *The EQ Class* keep coming up, and I feel those are what people need more urgently, so I write those first. Writing those books also takes time, so the other book gets delayed.

**Q: Are you willing to reveal now what the book is about?**

**Tsai:** I have to be sure myself what I'm going to write first (laughs). Right now the opening keeps getting overturned — I don't have the face to tell anyone.

**Q: Which suggests there may yet be a sixth version.**

**Tsai:** Please don't say that — the fifth is very good (laughs).

**Q: Recently on *Qipa Shuo* you mentioned the science-fiction novel *Fahrenheit 451*. In the novel, the author imagines a future day when humanity will not tolerate books. If books are found in your home, they are burned. At the end of the novel, everyone who rejects the policy of burning books takes responsibility for memorizing one book, preserving its contents for humankind. I'm curious: if you had to memorize one book, which would you keep?**

**Tsai:** I'd probably choose my current one, *The EQ Class*.

**Q: Really?**

**Tsai:** Mm. I couldn't bear to let that book disappear. Because if you asked me to pick something else — my favorite, say Miyuki Miyabe — it's hard to decide which of her novels to choose. And I'm quite certain that if I, as a living person, carried that book inside my body, anyone listening would fall asleep 20 minutes in. Her books cannot be transmitted through speech, so I think Miyuki Miyabe, or García Márquez, are both books that don't transmit well orally.

*Fahrenheit 451* mentions a man who carries the *Tao Te Ching* by Laozi inside him. I don't believe anyone could recite the *Tao Te Ching* aloud and have others stay with it. If a copy of the *Three Hundred Tang Poems* were walking down the street, that would be nice — but I don't want to become the transmitter of Tang poetry. I think in a desolate, end-of-world scenario, EQ would be extremely important. Because then people couldn't earn money from a prosperous world, and there would be no entertainment shows to watch — so in the end, one still has to face oneself.

**Q: You said writing is a process of "probing yourself," and that if a book you write happens to let its readers take it and probe themselves in turn, it was worth writing. May I take that as the reason and meaning of your writing?**

**Tsai:** I actually think this way even about doing shows. I think what a show radiates is not its concrete content but your attitude. If your show radiates an attitude that lets viewers feel that you can live life this way — that you don't have to carry so many burdensome labels on your back, and that everything must follow the rules, must be as it "should" be — then the show will bring people vitality. It's not about what is said in the show; it's about what the atmosphere the show radiates gets you to believe. So a show without that quality is, to me, more like just a job. Only when there's a radiating atmosphere do I feel it's more like my work.

So when *Kang Xi Lai Le* or *Qipa Shuo* radiate that atmosphere, I feel more that they are my works. And I firmly believe they plant some seeds in other people's hearts, so that when they run into difficulty, they can grow a flower from those seeds and get through it. When we were growing up, we too relied on some film or some book to do that for us, so I believe this does happen.

As for the books I wrote willfully — for instance, I used to write a lot that showed off verbal technique; one of my books is called 《再错也要谈恋爱》 (*Fall in Love Even When It's Wrong*). That book lays out both sides of every stage of falling in love and then debates with itself. Anyone who has read it knows I come from a debate team — I will debate the pros and cons of a matter with myself. By comparison, it would not matter at all if that book vanished. Between *Way with Words* and this *EQ Class*, I would rather *The EQ Class* be the one that stays.

So, yes. I think in the end there is always one book that you care very much about — whether it left a seed in someone else's heart.

![A still from the film The Godfather. Source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210232502JC47uBOZxRY2HEl0.jpg-w600)

**Q: You just mentioned film — we know film has had a big influence on you. About film, you once said: "What makes us happy is that entertainment has a lineage; what is regrettable is that film is no longer great. Most films today are disposable, use-once-and-throw-away products — they're the circus." Can you elaborate on why you think film is no longer great? And what role should film play in society now?**

**Tsai:** Think about it — for the generation that grew up on Douyin, how would they feel there is anything precious about the moving image? It's something you can get anytime, anywhere. You simply cannot accept a person watching a film like *The Godfather* on a phone — that feels like an insult to *The Godfather*. How can *The Godfather*, or Chaplin's films, be watched on a phone? How can something like *Lawrence of Arabia* — a film you should watch on a screen the size of a house — be watched on a phone? And then he answers you: "I've already seen that film."

For these past two weeks I've been doing something very interesting. Every day I watch three canonical films from film history that I've missed since my student days. Whether it's an Indian film, or a Greek film, an Italian film — watching, watching, watching. Then after finishing two of them each day, I have to watch one Hollywood film; by the third one, I finally come back to life (laughs). The first two wear me out. Sometimes I've already persuaded myself: keep a book at hand, and when it's really too slow, just read your book. Then I look up — oh, it's still playing this scene — and I keep reading, determined to fight it out. So that's how I got through most of those Italian and Swedish films.

I once debated on *Qipa Shuo*: is it necessary to go on a shoestring trip when you're young and have no money? Can't you save up and travel later? I said one sentence at the time: going somewhere at 18 and going at 80 mean different things. Seeing the pyramids at 18 and seeing them again at 80 are completely different. Likewise, seeing the great Italian films or Japanese films at 18 is a completely different thing from seeing them at 80. When I watch these films now, I know they were once great, but they have been imitated by later generations to death — imitated until overturned, copied a hundred times over. I guess if I told my students to watch *The Godfather* now, they would also find it not that amazing. They've seen a hundred other gangster films that copied *The Godfather* to death without ever having seen *The Godfather*.

So film losing its greatness is not film ceasing to be great — it is that people's experience has expanded enormously. The first time I saw Douyin, I still found it great fun. But when someone sets a scene from *The Godfather* to Douyin music and posts 30 seconds of it, you know: film is being forced to be no longer great. Still, I have never minded this.

**Q: So you don't hold an attitude of sadness or regret about it?**

**Tsai:** No. Whenever anyone laments to me that this is now an era of fragmented reading, I always think: reading a few fragments is better than reading nothing at all. They say, "You only read articles on your phone." I say: otherwise those people would read no articles their whole lives. Now they read articles on their phones — you should be happy for them; at least they read something. Lots of people talk nonsense, fantasizing that in ancient times everyone was virtuous and human relations were pure and simple — nonsense. In ancient times, so many people had no access to books, couldn't recognize characters, couldn't even get hold of a printed page, had no chance of schooling — how could you think that now is the fragmented era? Was there ever a complete-reading era before? I have never seen that we once had a society that loved reading. I've never seen such a thing.

![A still from the film Didi's Dreams. Source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210233355LnSYWANlUZqeH8j0.jpg-w600)

**Q: Will you make more films in the future?**

**Tsai:** People keep asking. The other day I answered: the most painful experience of my last film was the publicity period after shooting wrapped. I really, really didn't want it. Talking with you about EQ right now is a lot of fun for me because I'm still in a state of exploration. After I finished this book, my mood opened up enormously — wow, I opened up a world, and I hope to know more of it. But after I finished my last film, the post-production publicity terrified me. I had never been through such a thing in my life. I never had the experience of finishing *Kang Xi Lai Le* and then doing promotion — *Kang Xi Lai Le* just aired on its own. After I finished *Qipa Shuo*, there was no promotion either. Why is it that I make something and then have to keep telling people about it?

So I told my friend: if I make another film, I want to make one that requires no promotion.

**Q: But that's basically impossible in this industry.**

**Tsai:** Its budget would have to be small enough that no promotion is needed — and then you all can watch it or not, as you like. To do that, you have to make a very crude, wild film — crude enough that the budget is tiny, that the cinema chains ignore you, and that you can still survive. I'd rather make that kind of film; I don't want to go the regular big-blockbuster route again.

**Q: About entertainment — your talk this year at "Zaojiu" was very interesting. In your view, in the future entertainment industry, stars will become "spare parts" of our lives, and entertainment will bring more self-centered, more enclosed forms of satisfaction and virtual happiness. I'm curious: what attitude do you hold toward the future of entertainment? Because in my view, this just gives people the technical means to satisfy and immerse their own narcissism — yet humans also have a side of public life, and a future where each person delights in their own corner doesn't seem like a good thing.**

**Tsai:** I actually said that with a feeling of schadenfreude. Because when that era arrives, I'll already be gone, so I won't get bought up by a porn game with some star's face printed on it, letting you sleep with that star every night as you please, consuming that star endlessly. I think that day will come sooner or later. Take a Hollywood star — why would he care whether people in Asia fantasize about him as a bed partner? He got a royalty payment; how you fantasize about him is your own business. Even if a few Hollywood stars wouldn't agree to it, I think a few others would.

So that's the first point: your idol will definitely become virtual. He has a physical body, but you might feel: my idol should stay 28 forever. At 28, his real self keeps aging, and I don't want to face that, so please, Hollywood, keep making films with him at 28. We see in every film actors we like — Tom Cruise, say — perpetually young, performing on forever in their abundant prime. Fine — then it will inevitably evolve further: he becomes the housekeeper in your phone, the object of your pornographic fantasies... providing every kind of service.

On one hand, I won't get to enjoy that benefit; on the other hand, I won't have to endure that choice. Because if I had to endure it, I might agree too — anything goes (laughs) — so it's a good thing I won't have to undergo that test. As long as I'm not around for something, I don't care about it and have no attitude toward it. It's just something the people who come next will have to face themselves.

**Q: I think this may really come true in the near future.**

**Tsai:** In your lifetime, that day will definitely come. So you'd better decide in advance how many idols you're going to buy and put in one phone (laughs).

![Poster for season one of the variety show Qipa Shuo. Source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181210233023q9AfysPJkgc24YKF.jpg-w600)

**Q: You are one of the relatively few artists who have done variety shows successfully in both the mainland and Taiwan. From your perspective, are there any big differences between doing variety shows in Taiwan and in the mainland? How do you feel about it?**

**Tsai:** You know the TED platform. The three letters TED come from Technology, Entertainment, Design. Think — TED is so many years ahead of us; at its founding it had already confirmed that technology, entertainment, and design belong together. The most concrete example: take out our phones — they are design, they are technology, and they are entertainment.

So I think that in this atmosphere, talking about "variety shows" limits our field of vision too much. Douyin is a great example: it is entertainment brought about by technology and design. Before it existed, you could not describe it. You could not describe why we would become intoxicated by 30-second clips of different people doing ridiculous things set to the same music, and why we sink into them, unable to pull ourselves out.

In director Steven Spielberg's recent film *Ready Player One*, we saw many people who are disappointed with their real lives hiding in a virtual world. If that virtual world also offered rewards usable in real life — as in that film, where money earned in the virtual world helps you pay your expenses in real life — then you would no longer care what achievements you attain in the real world; achievements in the virtual world are worth just as much. The social status inside it, the degree of respect, the fame — they are all real.

So this is actually my view of variety shows. As long as experiments of sufficient scale keep happening in our lives, entertainment forms beyond our imagination will emerge. And those things are unprecedented — I think they don't even have regional boundaries. Douyin is now popular in the West too. You don't know where social platforms like Instagram will end up? They will keep recombining video length with sound, the way you watch them, whether to attach dating functions, and so on.

My old reading program didn't do well precisely because I treated it as reading. I introduced reading inside my show, instead of turning the act of reading itself into a show. That was my biggest blunder. So looking back, I think my reading program had no reason to exist — I merely introduced books within a show. I think someone seriously making a reading program should turn the show itself into a reading experience.

So, on what you just raised: I think the category of variety shows will simply disappear. You won't know whether you're playing a game or watching a show. You won't know whether you're watching a drama or watching a variety show. That is the future form of entertainment that I believe will arrive very soon.

**Q: On one hand, you care a lot about a sense of presence — you want what you make to be something the public responds to. On the other hand, you feel time pressing and want to clear time to do the things you truly want to do. You often swing between the two, and the reason, you've said, is that you're not brave enough — or a bit weak. Do you agree with this view? Why?**

**Tsai:** Completely correct. And I think everyone is probably like this. That's a good observation. For instance, if *The EQ Class* is well received again this time, inevitably there will have to be an *EQ Class 2* or an *EQ Class 3*. But its starting point is that I feel this matter hasn't been fully talked through. When I myself like *The EQ Class*, I feel there is so much more worth saying. I don't want to stop either, because I finally sorted this thing out after much effort, and I can't bear to just close it off there. But it delays my novel.

I don't really want a tombstone — I'm just using it as an abstract example — but if my tombstone were to carry one line, I wouldn't need to be introduced as: this is a man who talked about EQ (laughs). It sounds like I'm too kindhearted a person — I'm not that kindhearted. I'd rather he said this is a person who created a fantasy world. So for me there is still a trade-off here. And yet, plainly, after *The EQ Class* came out, many people said that at least one chapter, one viewpoint in it helped them think something through.

You're right about the weakness — how am I to set all this aside and go write that novel? Or should I write both? I don't think a person can manage both. So maybe the next time you interview me, I'll be starting my fantasy novel for the eighth time (laughs).
