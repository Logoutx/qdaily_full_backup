Or give free rein to that restless creative urge.

In the basement of No. 1 Building, Lane 298, Anfu Road, the most eye-catching spot just inside the door holds a poster: a red finger pressing a doorbell, with large characters beside it asking, "Is anyone in the hall?"

This is a place you can simply knock and enter, filled with posters, illustrations, and publications. Every Tuesday evening, films are screened here. If your ignorance of the place makes you nervous, a young woman named Runqiu will come up and chat with you, or crack a few jokes. Drinks are self-serve; just find a spot and make yourself at home.

Except it's in a basement. Most people who come either have a friend's introduction or follow the official account—the official account handles all announcements. For instance, if something comes up on a Tuesday and the screening can't happen, Runqiu will write a push notification the day before: "The hall master couple has a date tonight, so screenings are paused for one day. Hall friends, please come next week."

If you don't frequent the place, you might find it puzzling. The security guard of the residential compound that owns the basement feels the same: "I don't know what these young people are up to in there, and I'm not interested." Part of the reason may be the young people who pass the guardroom on their way to the hall. Their look is a little different from white-collar workers: not much chatter, free and colorful clothing and hairstyles. They duck into the building and head straight for the underground passage. After seeing this repeatedly, the older guards in the security room decide they're "not respectable enough."

Runqiu and her husband Li Xiaoyu rented the 250-square-meter basement in April 2017. It has five rooms. Usually, Li Xiaoyu and Runqiu, who work in advertising creative planning, use it as a studio. But they want more than just a space; they want interesting people and things to happen here. They call the place "Anfu Hall."

In June 2017, the protagonist of Anfu Hall's first exhibition was Jiang Long, Runqiu's desk neighbor at the advertising company, whose job was art director. Lacking planning, Jiang Long only got off work at the ad agency a few hours before the exhibition opened and began setting up in the hall late at night. He brought about 1,000 sheets of A3 paper printed with the exhibition contents—1,000 photos taken with phone filters—and the only other tool was tape.

The exhibition was titled "Real Filters, Phony Life." In Sichuan dialect, "zhen ge zi" means "the real deal, truly authentic"; in other words, "filters are for real, life is phony." Jiang Long said of this, "I quite like taking photos, but it seems that apart from adding filters and using a digital camera, I haven't really learned much else."

After the first exhibition, people began trickling in. In July, a young rainwear fashion brand approached Anfu Hall, wanting to use the space for a launch event. They felt Anfu Hall matched their brand's creative, experimental vibe.

In January 2018, through the "Hall Host Crowdfunding Plan," netizen "Zaojian Chun" discovered Anfu Hall and quickly decided to join the plan. Using the hall space, she organized a secondhand idle-goods market called "Chun Ji." Transactions covered clothing, miscellaneous items, books, music, and even piercings and tattoos. The expected crowd exceeded 300 people. It turned out to be a success, and half a year later they held it again.

Once different ideas and creativity were accommodated, Anfu Hall accumulated more secondhand objects.

Discarded sofas and coffee tables found on the side of Anfu Road would be carried down to the basement by hall friends. Each room began to develop its own function. By 2018, "Is anyone in the hall?" had become the phrase most often heard in the WeChat group.

Visitors ring the doorbell, come in, visit an impromptu material-collage exhibition, then attend a "challenge boredom" [Coslife costume contest](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/_IhAGdo2IMZB96SsYGZXUw), or listen to a Northeastern painter talk about [underworld stories](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/BaRpEmv8vI4DvX7nMp30Uw), while the sofa is packed with people. Some times are fixed: every Wednesday, Li Xiaoyu's band [*Peace and Waves*](https://www.xiami.com/artist/nmSyNA766e0) rehearses in the basement, when there are fewer people around. Photographer Wang Wei shows up anytime, adjusting lights in a corner of one room, working on his photography or shooting some commercial projects.

Wang Wei is a fellow townsman of Li Xiaoyu from Daqing; they've known each other for years.

Wang Wei came to Shanghai in 2017. [He excels at capturing impromptu scenes of life](https://www.rsdfuzz.com/page), with particular interest in street, fashion, and youth culture. This matches Anfu Hall's temperament. Wang Wei used to work at an oilfield "unit." "Back then, the way to get through winter at work was to hide in the oilfield metering station watching *Ma Dashuai*, with tea and snacks on hand," Wang Wei said. "It's a completely different lifestyle from now."

"This is a place where you can escape the dullness," said an engineer in manufacturing named Si Wei. During the day, he checks equipment at the factory and supervises workers handling phone screens. After work on Wednesdays, he often drives an hour from Pudong Airport to Anfu Hall to create, record, or rehearse with Li Xiaoyu. He is the bassist of the band *Peace and Waves* and also loves playing baseball.

"Since getting to know Li Xiaoyu and the others, life feels more open. Originally my life was just a small area. It's not that Anfu Hall has some high artistic achievement or deep meaning; it's just that its existence really does enrich life."

After participating in a baseball activity organized by the hall, Si Wei is now obsessed with baseball. Every night after work, returning home to practice baseball moves in the garage has become a habit.

Meanwhile, graphic designer "Nezha" has a side gig as a tattoo artist. She is also the only current patron of the hall's "Host Crowdfunding Plan." By paying a small amount of rent to settle into Anfu Hall, Nezha can freely use a corner set up in the hall as her tattoo studio. "Tattoo clients find the hall fresh and novel," she said. She works here an average of three times a week, mostly in the evenings.

After paying a full year's rent for Anfu Hall in 2017, Li Xiaoyu and Runqiu had actually spent all their post-wedding savings. The hall itself couldn't make much money; band friends helped out with friendly rehearsal-room rent.

Then they launched the "Hall Host Crowdfunding Plan," allowing residents to exchange a fee for access to shared studio space in the hall, free refreshments, event space, and other privileges. The fee was "starting at 500 yuan, no upper limit," valid for three months. Everything else was subsidized by the couple's main advertising-creative business.

But this state actually suited the atmosphere they had felt from Charlie.

Charlie was an old man Li Xiaoyu and Runqiu met on their honeymoon, a lone surfer on Malibu Beach in California. In his sixties or seventies, he lived in an RV. "In China I've never met anyone like him—so simple, pure, and free."

"Everyone coming and going on Malibu Beach knew Charlie. He sat in front of a particularly beat-up little car, behaving like a gentleman. Charlie would lay out a very clean little rug and place a small rattan chair with a cushion. He sat there, occupying the best spot on the whole beach for watching the sunset. Sometimes, seeing waves coming from afar, Charlie would quickly change clothes, grab his board, and go chase the waves; sometimes he would sit watching the waves all day."

They remember this vividly.

In 2017, a month after renting Anfu Hall, Li Xiaoyu and Runqiu went to the United States again. A year later, on the same beach in California at the same spot, the beat-up white RV was still there.

"Charlie had been living in the RV the whole time, with no phone, no Facebook or Instagram, but we found him. The year before, while surfing, he had given me a T-shirt with his face printed on the back. This time Xiaoyu and I also made him one, with 'FindCharlie' written on the back," Runqiu said. "But this time he had a new girlfriend, a national forest ranger who carried a gun."

After returning, the two quickly made a series of FindCharlie T-shirts—limited to 60 pieces, only one size (Charlie's size)—plus a matching red-and-yellow waterproof bag (which Charlie could use as a bucket in his RV), sold only offline at Anfu Hall (Charlie had no phone or internet).

In October 2017, Anfu Hall held a "new product launch" that recreated the layout of an RV (Charlie's RV). The series sold out that day, and the FindCharlie T-shirt gradually began appearing on every friend who regularly visited Anfu Hall.

"Earn money, pay off loans, buy a school-district apartment, tutor your kid with homework—just thinking about it is suffocating!"

In December 2017, when Runqiu wrote a recommendation for an upcoming project at Anfu Hall called "FxckOff 30," she described it this way: "So some people are also quite unreliable at 30. Seeing that, I feel relieved."

[*FxckOff 30*](https://weibo.com/fxckoff30) is a challenge project created by two women about to turn 30, Mary and Juan: they aimed to complete 30 somewhat absurd, never-before-tried things before turning 30. The 30th thing was to hold a summary exhibition at Anfu Hall reviewing the previous 29.

At the opening of that event, one of the ribbon-cutting methods involved a large pair of scissors cutting through the two characters "middle age" hung on a clothes rack.

In May 2018, Anfu Hall held a solo photography exhibition called "Pearl," aimed at "celebrating failure." In one room, red curtains covered an entire wall, with strings of pearls hanging down, and film photos shot by photographer Qian Yifan arranged among them. The idea was that fragments of failed life intertwine to become pearls.

Runqiu thinks this exhibition is especially simple: just look at it without thinking about the concept of art. All the pearls were bought on Taobao.

Regarding the event, Runqiu said, "Qian Yifan is a failed artist. After returning to China from her studies, she tried being an office worker and attempted to start a business, but both failed. But she lives vividly, creating vividly and instinctively. Capturing extremely delicate moments in life through the camera is something she has always been doing."

People came one after another; there was never any so-called concentrated growth.

Except for one event during the Shanghai International Film Festival in June 2017—Anfu Hall released a three-month screening schedule of 34 classic films and invited friends or cinephiles to watch. Li Xiaoyu recalled, "During that period, a lot of people knocked on the hall door for the first time, but most of them were film fans. Once the film festival ended, the number of visitors immediately dropped."

Anfu Hall wants to attract more people to knock on the door, mainly to encourage different thoughtful people to come in and create, so that "rough," "kind of interesting," or even absurd personalized ideas can be put into practice. Li Xiaoyu said, "Anyone can ring the doorbell, come in and talk about what interests them, and then we'll do something together."

The "Anfu Summer Cool-Down Exhibition" held in July 2017 embodied this.

A month before the exhibition, Runqiu and Li Xiaoyu discussed how to do it. Quickly, the principles were set: "promote real social interaction," "unlimited filling with works," "open without closing hours," "continuously blur the boundaries of art." The two quickly began preparing, writing copy and designing posters, while inviting acquaintances around them, mostly advertising people or illustrators in the creative industry, ensuring they would bring sufficiently special works on the day. In addition, each creator's contact QR code was printed in advance as a sticker to be attached beside their works, so that everyone could connect privately.

On the promotional poster for that event, Runqiu wrote, "This summer is the great heat; friends, please come in and cool off. Put your restless creative urges into Anfu Hall. Everyone is welcome to join the Anfu Summer Cool-Down Exhibition anytime. Watermelon and snacks will be provided on site."

Over that "Anfu Summer Cool-Down Exhibition" weekend, the hall door never closed, air-conditioning cold air kept drifting out, and people kept knocking to come in, sometimes carrying bowls of water and paint, or moving in projectors or birdcages.

"Hall friend" Zhang Zihao brought a few photos and blank canvases. The photos showed the gear he had carried while working as a bodyguard in the Philippines not long before; the canvases were blank (except for a few bullet holes) because "conditions at the time made it impossible to draw anything." His main job is still being a painter. Photographer Wang Wei hung several photos he had taken, including some clips he recorded during the New Year visit to his hometown Daqing for "a friend who had spent a few months in a psychiatric hospital."

Another illustrator, after choosing a room in the hall, began drawing countless waves and drowning little people on the wall, with speech-bubble dialog boxes floating beside the little people so that visiting participants could continue creating inside the bubbles. More than 100 people came to that exhibition in total.

Runqiu didn't find this event absurd or like a joke; "it was quite lifelike." "We don't interfere with what they want to do at all." She said that day someone deliberately left a hammer behind, standing alone in a corner of the room. Visitors took it for an artwork and rushed to the corner to take photos.

Most of the time, "whether to hold an event" is decided on a whim. If you insist on asking for the reason, as hall operators Runqiu or Li Xiaoyu usually only answer, "It's just fun."

On December 31, 2018, Anfu Hall launched a "New Year's jump rope" activity to celebrate the new year in the form of "jumping into 2019." As a result, many people drank too much and threw up before jumping into the new year.

Also in 2018, the hall's operating model began to look optimistic. A recent project came from the Puchi stand-up team, with design inspiration from meal tickets. The finished products included a calendar, performance tickets, posters, and packaging. Li Xiaoyu was responsible for overall project coordination and execution of the visual parts, Wang Wei shot the products, and styling was assisted by hall friends.

Li Xiaoyu believes this comes down to people recognizing the aesthetic sensibility Anfu Hall represents and developing trust, so they come to collaborate. Now this cooperation is long-term; every Sunday, the Puchi stand-up Shanghai show performs at Anfu Hall.

Another example of a collaboration with greater creative freedom and tighter integration of form and space happened during the World Cup. "Puma came to us wanting to combine the Anfu Hall space and football matches into the concept of a 'Puma Football Living Room,' using the hall's space and creativity as a vehicle to create some football-brand-related cultural content. This was very interesting."

In official Puma collaboration photos, the hall's old friends are sunk into sofas, shouting as they watch World Cup matches, with an "Anfu Hall × Puma" collaboration rug spread out in the center of the living room. During the World Cup, these people gathered in the hall every night to watch matches until late.

For the collaboration, Li Xiaoyu invited four designer friends from the hall to create four football-themed illustrations, placed in various corners of the hall for product photography, while organizing several offline FIFA game competitions and recording a few episodes of football content on the Anfu Hall radio channel. "For the hall, this was a bit like a milestone collaboration," Li Xiaoyu said, trying to seek more similar commercial possibilities. "We welcome mutually beneficial and fun commercial collaborations."

Li Xiaoyu and Runqiu are trying to bring Anfu Hall above ground so more people know about it, making operations more sustainable. They collaborate with Sasha's ["Music ONLY" radio station](https://music.163.com/#/djradio?id=792136423), carrying Anfu Hall merchandise and appearing in a number of independent shops, dessert stores, and coffee houses in Shanghai to record live radio "pop-up" events.

Sometimes they distribute Anfu Hall stickers and design posters, but most people who come to participate are still friends.

They also produced a program documenting Chinese and foreign bands performing live at Anfu Hall, "[DATINGlive](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/QmKknVmeJEhqB6Oc5ytkpQ)," currently with ten episodes, with the production crew all being hall friends. These bands on tour in Shanghai would come to this basement after their city shows to perform again. But filming costs are not cheap; equipment expenses are high. Because he himself is in a band, Li Xiaoyu values the ritual sense of documenting music. "This program is quite important to us now," he emphasized.

Beyond that, Runqiu mentioned the basement's limitations and current rent situation. "The basement ceiling isn't high enough, so when some pure-art exhibition or theater organizers come to look at the venue, they find it won't work. This limits many things we want to do." At the same time, the partitioned rooms also reduce the possibilities of spatial creation. This also affects venue rental and collecting rent.

And facing the reality of rising rents in Shanghai, the two have some concerns. If rents rise further, they can't afford it. Relying solely on salaries to support Anfu Hall operations can barely meet basic needs. They want to do more.

"But Anfu Hall will always exist. If rent goes up, the two of us will just move somewhere else," Runqiu said.
