@@QD_BODY@@
Slowing economic growth, industry and corporate transformation, and the like have left hiring demand and salary increases looking rather bleak

Have you felt that changing jobs has been harder this year? Or has a friend complained to you about how difficult it is to find work? That probably isn't your imagination.

Zhaopin forecasts a job-market prosperity index of 2.09 for 2016. That's slightly more optimistic than the third quarter of last year, but it isn't a good number. (The prosperity index takes 1 as the dividing line; the higher the index, the healthier the job market.)

According to the *ManpowerGroup Employment Outlook Survey*, only 7% of employers said they planned to hire and add staff in the first quarter of 2016 — the worst first-quarter hiring outlook in ten years.

Even foreign data companies and media outlets have taken note. The CEO of Quanton Data, a New York–based data firm, told QDaily that among the more than 100 Chinese websites they monitor (he declined to say which), large companies (1,000+ employees) have significantly cut back on recruiting since November 2015, with the number of job postings down 11.5% year over year this past January.

In 2015, China's GDP growth fell below 7% for the first time in 15 years, and research institutions forecast it would drop to 6.66% in 2016 — even lower than last year. This is considered one of the important reasons the job market is no longer as hot as it once was. As a key reference point for corporate salary growth, salary increases in many industries last year didn't even outpace GDP growth. This year's salary growth is forecast at around 8% — again, not optimistic. According to the National Bureau of Statistics, average urban salary growth exceeded 12% in both 2011 and 2012.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702110248Gmwoa50AhrQk8dOL.jpg-w600)

Another fact that Guo Sheng, CEO of Zhaopin, has observed: job-seeking activity is actually on the rise. In an environment of economic malaise and meager salary growth, "this means people feel insecure, and the pressure of finding a job is increasing."

Last year was also a turbulent one for capital, industries, and companies alike. The transformation of traditional industries and the frequent mergers and consolidation among internet companies have all had subtle effects on the job market. Noisy talk of a capital winter and an internet bubble has been increasingly believed — especially after Alibaba and Baidu "cut back on hiring" at the end of last year, sending a negative signal.

In addition, growth in campus recruiting lags growth in experienced-hire recruiting, so fresh graduates will find it harder to land an ideal job than people with professional workplace experience. When the economy is weak, companies prefer to hire experienced people to save on labor costs.

But don't be too pessimistic. Overall hiring volume is still growing — just not as fast as before. That means there are still plenty of options out there; it's just that the odds of finding a really good one are smaller.

That said, everyone finding a job that truly satisfies them has never been easy at any time. As someone who is, or is about to become, a "company person," these changes in this year's job market may be worth your attention.

## Hiring at internet companies riding the wave is starting to become more rational

Whether in hiring volume, salary growth, or appeal to talent, the internet industry has led the pack for years. After all, talent flows toward wherever the wave is. According to data from Zhaopin, internet-economy and real-economy hiring demand now stands at roughly a 6:4 ratio.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021102489tyDreSnuBbRQzEG.jpg-w600)

But two "blockbuster stories" from last year probably come to mind: Alibaba abruptly slashed the number of offers it handed out on campus, and Baidu also froze experienced-hire recruiting (supposedly you needed Robin Li's personal approval to join the company). At the time, it caused widespread panic.

Everyone wondered: has the economic winter really arrived? Is the internet bubble about to burst?

There's really no need for such catastrophizing. The internet companies led by BAT aren't failing — they've adopted a stabilization policy and are restructuring. After years of high-speed growth, they no longer need as many hands, so they've stopped large-scale hiring.

Justin Ren, an associate professor of operations and technology management at Boston University, believes this trend may be part of an industry cycle. China's leading internet companies (such as Alibaba and JD.com) are expanding into international markets, which will call for new high-end talent rather than masses of low-end engineers.

Wang Weiyi, Alibaba's senior director of human resources, told *China Daily*: "To become a globalized company, you need to build an excellent team with international backgrounds and global thinking." The implication being that the company hopes to recruit high-end talent with work experience in different countries.

Guo Sheng, CEO of Zhaopin, echoed the same point in an interview with QDaily. Speaking of shrinking headcounts at internet companies, he said: "What we may be seeing is a signal of internal adjustment at companies, not a signal that the overall market is bad. I think more than anything they want to raise the caliber of their internal staff and adjust their corporate structure."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702110249n9dVWKDE8hI6QfHY.jpg-w600)

For fresh graduates, that may be bad news: entry-level positions are shrinking, and getting into these big companies is harder than it was a few years ago. They need more people with work experience. Last year 51job posted more than 30 million job openings, and fewer than 10% of them were aimed at college graduates.

But BAT is seen as the weather vane of the internet industry, and its "hiring cuts" signal worried people: "People will think if even BAT is struggling, other companies must be doing worse," Guo said. In fact, the absolute number of positions BAT itself provides is not large within the overall economy. But there's no denying that hiring in the internet industry, which has entered an adjustment period, is no longer as aggressive as before.

## Companies merge and split, but frontline jobs are barely affected

Didi Kuaidi swallowed its smaller rival, 58.com and Ganji joined forces, Qunar merged with Ctrip, Meituan got together with Dianping... The merger drama of 2015 came in wave after wave, dazzling to watch. According to Pedata, under Zero2IPO Group, China completed 2,110 M&A deals in the first three quarters of 2015, up 60% year over year; of those, 1,716 cases with disclosed amounts involved US$139.288 billion in transactions, up 53% year over year.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021102498F6Pbleq4kicGrXK.jpg-w600)

For the overall job market, these mergers have had little impact. Merged companies may make some adjustments to their personnel structure — finance and HR departments may need to be aligned, for instance — but for now most merged companies still operate independently, and large numbers of frontline positions have been unaffected.

Take the O2O industry: entry-level frontline positions are chronically short-staffed, and the personnel changes caused by mergers are mostly at the middle and senior management level. After the Didi Kuaidi merger, some senior executives at Kuaidi cashed out and left; after the Youku Tudou merger, Wang Wei took his management team and founded Light Chaser Animation; after the 58.com–Ganji merger, Ganji vice president Chen Xu, head of product Huo Liang, and other executives departed.

But since senior executives usually make up no more than 1% of a company's employees, even obvious turbulence in the middle and upper ranks has little effect on the market as a whole.

These people also have no trouble finding somewhere to go. The wave of departures triggered by consolidation has actually increased mobility in the talent market. In terms of where people go: founders mostly turn to investing or cash out; senior executives tend to start their own businesses; professional managers hop between industries; and even ordinary employees produce no shortage of entrepreneurs, many of whom take what they've seen, heard, and learned in first-tier cities back to second-, third-, and fourth-tier cities to become small business owners.

Among the ten industries with the largest hiring volumes, the number of positions from the internet/e-commerce industry rose 32%. Online-to-offline (O2O) connection generated large numbers of jobs over the past few years. But affected by industry consolidation, hiring in O2O and P2P has declined noticeably compared with previous years.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702110250Kyu9EXReiP3hdtWZ.jpg-w600)

## As big companies transform, people in their 40s may face job-hunting difficulties

Another undercurrent in the talent market comes from the transformation of big companies, and the most representative case is Wanda. In 2016, Wanda announced large-scale layoffs within its real estate division, with headcount reportedly as high as 30,000 to 50,000 people. Wanda, which had never lowered its targets in ten years, cut its real estate sales target by 40%, while revenue from services and net profit are expected to each account for 55%. In Wang Jianlin's own words, "By the end of 2016, Wanda will no longer be a real estate company."

Shifting from commercial real estate to the information industry and to entertainment and tourism naturally adjusts the demand for and structure of personnel. But in absolute terms of headcount demand, people leaving here and joining there roughly balance out, so the overall change is small. Whenever an industry giant like Wanda transforms, it's also a good time for rivals to poach talent. Employees with top industry experience can find a new employer quickly and won't face too long a "gap period."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021102501SwaX2dTnDQri4hV.jpg-w600)

According to 51job data, demand for staff in commercial real estate peaked in 2014 and has been declining since. "Even though the economy is rather weak, big companies are still willing to invest in people. Keeping the pace and scale of hiring in the high-end talent market helps with overseas expansion and later-stage growth," said Feng Lijuan, 51job's chief human resources expert. Top managers who understand the international scene, understand China, and understand the industry are always in short supply.

Many big companies across industries are now in a period of transformation and change, and their existing management models and expectations of employees' knowledge and skills have raised many new demands — for example, a big increase in marketing-type positions on social platforms — making it considerably harder to find the right people.

Although many companies now prefer to hire people with at least three years of work experience, Feng Lijuan said that non-specialist workers in their 40s face job-hunting difficulties: "Once they lose their jobs, it's hard to say whether their knowledge, experience, and connections can still support the current model."

So in Guo Sheng's view, the advantage in job-hunting today is being "internet-plus" — that is, on top of professional skills, having the ability to handle problems in an internet way. Take someone in brand marketing: they should understand new technology.

## Total hiring is up, but college students still can't find jobs

The long campus recruiting season starts to get real in March. The fourth quarter, with its dense concentration of interviews, may look lively, but no more than 10% of students actually lock down a job before the New Year. Feng Lijuan noted that by March of the following year it's basically settled whether one will continue studying, become a civil servant, or take a job; the second quarter of each year is the peak signing season for campus recruiting. Last year 51job served more than 700 companies in campus recruiting; the year before it was more than 500 — a number that grows year by year, with the most obvious expansion in 2011 and 2012.

Whether it's the stubbornly high rate of offer rengeging or the year-over-year growth in job postings, today's college graduates have access to far more job opportunities and career directions than before — more than 400,000 companies are recruiting fresh graduates. Yet the data on college students finding jobs runs counter to the data on the hiring market.

Today's college students are pickier than before when job-hunting, and the standard for "satisfying" is changing. No constant business trips, no constant overtime, salary expectations of X amount... "For example, if the workplace isn't in the city center, it's very hard to recruit," Feng Lijuan said. "The fit between college students' career expectations and the actual market isn't what it was a few years ago — it's actually drifting further and further apart."

On one hand, college students have many expectations for their first job, and their demands are increasingly diverse. On the other hand, as overall economic growth slows, employers face mounting pressure on operating costs and profit margins, and the salary growth they offer college students has actually slowed. So in recent years the tension between the two ends has grown much greater than before.

From an industry perspective, Shanghai's tertiary sector accounted for more than 70% of the economy last year, and according to 51job's statistics, all of the ten job categories with the largest hiring volumes are service positions. The massive population shifts triggered by Chinese New Year created huge post-holiday gaps in restaurant, retail, customer service, and marketing positions.

In the week after Chinese New Year 2016, hiring in the top ten cities by job volume grew an average of 15% year over year. Suzhou's hiring surpassed Xi'an's, making it the city with the biggest increase, followed by Shanghai; Xi'an was the only city whose hiring volume fell below the same period last year.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021102504b3CJlFNQaeHK8Uh.jpg-w600)

But that has little to do with college students' job-hunting needs. Basic positions in restaurants, retail, and trade don't demand high educational credentials, and the customer service positions with the greatest demand are usually ones college students are reluctant to settle for.

Beyond that, 80% of China's hiring demand comes from private companies; whether hiring or laying off, they reflect the economy's temperature and direction more vividly and in real time. But most of these experienced-hire positions demand 7–8 years of work experience, and more than 80% require three years of experience.

## By industry: services are hot, manufacturing is cooling

ManpowerGroup publishes its *Employment Outlook Survey* every quarter. Based on its first-quarter 2016 forecast, done at the end of last year, employers in finance, insurance, and real estate had a poor hiring outlook: only 2% planned to recruit new employees.

Another industry unlikely to add much headcount is manufacturing, where only 5% of employers plan to hire. We've previously analyzed the migration of manufacturing industry: as many factories relocate to lower-cost places like Southeast Asia and Africa, jobs naturally decrease. Beyond light industry, hiring in related sectors such as machinery and heavy industry is also down from before — though this trend actually began to show two or three years ago and is now simply continuing.

The internet industry, though its hiring growth has slowed, remains relatively hot. Beyond the demand from "pure" internet companies, traditional industries such as apparel, food, education, and media are also digitizing: building e-commerce operations or websites, expanding online business, and adapting to consumers' electronic purchasing habits and ways of communicating. So they need a lot of IT talent.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702110251Y9EzToIyHr0lma1t.jpg-w600)

In ManpowerGroup's data, 10% of services companies are expanding their hiring of new employees. You can probably feel how "hot" services are from the increasingly professional and specialized services around you: home cleaning, chefs who come to your door — services that were once rare are now increasingly common.

This is related to the changing structure of Chinese industry. According to 51job data, in the ten cities with the most job postings, the tertiary sector accounts for more than 70% of output. In first-tier cities like Shanghai and Beijing, the tertiary sector contributes more than 80% of urban GDP. The development of O2O-type industries has brought more offline labor demand.

For now, the overall supply of services positions is still very large and continues to grow. But the growth rate has slowed as well.
