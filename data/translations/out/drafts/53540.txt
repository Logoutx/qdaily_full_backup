Drag culture has shifted from a purely entertaining form of performance into a space and a vehicle, allowing marginalized groups in society to find a place that feels like home.

Neil and Kris are ordinary white-collar workers in Beijing who have recently become the objects of pursuit for media at home and abroad — newspapers, magazines, and documentaries have all shown a strong interest in them. But the reason for the attention has nothing to do with their day jobs. It's about their other identity at night: drag queens.

“I've always had a desire to express myself, and I think drag is a great outlet,” Neil says.

“When you're in drag, you become a different version of yourself. You say things you wouldn't normally say and do things you wouldn't normally dare to do, because you've completely become another person,” Kris says.

![One of Neil's drag queen looks (Photo / VICE China)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524032358ufPeUFDcMgx5LaOY.jpeg-w600)

### China's Drag Queens: A Niche Within a Niche

Neil was born in Inner Mongolia; Kris is an ethnic Chinese born in Sweden. Two years ago they met each other at a drag performance. Their drag careers are roughly the same length — both about two years.

Last November, VICE China came to them and filmed a documentary, 《自造皇后》(*Self-Made Queen*), following their journey from Beijing to the well-known Shanghai LGBT bar Lucca390, where they took part in the annual drag queen competition.

《自造皇后》is part of VICE China's “Young People” series. Producer Liang Wanni says the series aims to document “people and regions overlooked by the mainstream, as well as controversial groups and hot-button topics.”

[Self-Made Queen went live on April 9 and has since racked up 810,000 views](http://www.vice.cn/read/young-chinese-drag-queen).

Neil and Kris say that after it aired they received quite a bit of feedback from netizens around the country — more praise than they expected. “A lot of the people giving feedback aren't gay, but they told me it changed how they see gay people. There wasn't as much snark as I imagined. I seem to have accidentally become someone who speaks up for the community,” Neil says.

“A niche within a niche” — that's how some people describe the status of drag queens in China.

Part of why Neil and Kris have drawn such close attention is that drag queens are a special group; another part is the 2009 American reality show *RuPaul's Drag Race*, whose airing made drag hot all over again.

Were it not for the popularity of *RuPaul's Drag Race*, many people might still have no idea what a drag queen even is.

![RuPaul's Drag Race](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524032824MEvlm7W4e2J51dF9.jpeg-w600)

*RuPaul's Drag Race* combines the features of shows like *America's Next Top Model* and *Project Runway*, the only difference being that the contestants are drag queens. Hosted by the famous American drag queen RuPaul, it is now in its tenth season. The show has influenced every drag queen, Kris and Neil included — both of them are devoted viewers.

As the show's ratings climbed, drag once again leapt into the American mainstream, and its drag queens became performers that bars competed to book.

Two years of drag experience would count as fairly shallow credentials in the United States, but in China it makes you something of a veteran. Even in Beijing, there are no more than 10 drag queens performing at the same time, Neil says, and in the worst-case scenario the only performers on a given night were Neil and Kris themselves.

![One of Kris's drag queen looks (Photo / provided by Neil)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524033826rXF8UtLfhZDAPdOQ.jpeg-w600)

“I think it's too early for China to talk about drag culture, because people haven't even fully accepted homosexuality yet — how could they accept a culture that's quite niche even within the gay community?” Neil says.

Even the venues and opportunities for drag performances are scarce. “Before 2009, I never saw a drag queen in Beijing. I had seen cross-dressing performances, which are also a kind of drag, but they're still quite different from the Western kind,” Kris says.

Although cross-dressing (反串) also involves men dressing as women, it leans more toward a form of theatrical performance. In the early days, women were barred from performing, so all actors were men — which is how cross-dressing performances came about.

As for drag culture in the American sense, the LGBTQ+ culture and gender connotations it contains are essential elements that make up the culture.

### A Riot That Changed What Drag Culture Meant to the LGBTQ+ Community

In the early hours of June 28, 1969, the famous American bar “Stonewall” was raided yet again, with police arbitrarily arresting gay people.

Gay people in the United States had long been suppressed, and years of accumulated grievances turned that day into a clash. That day, a gay person threw the first brick at the police, setting off the key event that changed the gay rights movement — the “Stonewall Riot” — which is also regarded as the first time in American history that gay people resisted government persecution.

Who exactly threw the first brick is still hotly debated, but the most common account names a Black drag queen, Marsha P. Johnson. “She” started the riot.

Even today, at the gay pride marches held every year around the world, you can see drag queens in the ranks, or giving speeches at key moments. They have always occupied the position of spiritual leaders in the gay rights movement.

George Chauncey, a professor of history at Columbia University, has long focused on gender issues and the history of LGBTQ+ people and urban development.

In 1995, George Chauncey published a book, *Gay New York: Gender, Urban Culture, and the Making of the Gay Male World*, which mentions that in the late 1990s organized drag performances began appearing in American bars. Chauncey explains: “Gay people created a culture through which to cultivate a sense of collective identity.”

In 2003, Leila Rupp and Verta Taylor also published a book about drag queens, *Drag Queens at the 801 Cabaret*, which states: “Drag culture shifted from a purely entertaining form of performance into a space and a vehicle, allowing marginalized groups in society to find a place that feels like home. Drag is also a culture that can break through multiple gender constraints.”

![Neil's drag queen name is Frozen Lolita (Photo / Franka Gulin)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524033540GIK6mrMVi0pT9xbn.jpeg-w600)

Kris believes drag is not only a performing art but also carries an interpretation of gender diversity. “We're not just being funny — we genuinely think women are powerful, so we use our own way to express that idea.” He adds that after doing drag he has more respect for women: “I really can't imagine how anyone can walk in high heels all day.”

“The reason drag is a culture is that there are lots of rules in it, or things you can argue about. Drag is also a holistic thing — it's not just makeup and performance; it also has to do with personality and attitude. It's really not as simple as ‘I just like wearing women's clothes,’” Neil says.

RuPaul has also said in media interviews that the art of drag is about trying on a new identity in order to escape the frame of one's original identity.

### “This Show Was My Masterpiece”

Since the end of 2016, Neil and Kris have performed as drag queens every month at the Beijing Sanlitun bar Migas, usually at the end of the month. Every performance is unpaid, mainly to raise funds for the nonprofit “Ji'ande Counseling Center.” It was at this bar that they first met.

On December 8, 2017, Neil and Kris came once again to the Beijing Sanlitun bar Migas.

By that day, Neil had been a drag queen for nearly a year. December 11, 2016 was Neil's first time in drag; he dressed up that day but did not perform. He was still at a raw, unpolished stage with all things drag — even his chest was padded with paper.

Talking about his past drag performances, Neil says that December 8 was his masterpiece: he wore a handmade silver aluminum-foil bodysuit, playing a sex robot.

The inspiration for the outfit came from the classic work of the famous Japanese illustrator Hajime Sorayama, “Sexy Robot.” Beyond its surreal, futuristic feel, more importantly it conveys meaning about female eroticism — a hallmark of Sorayama's work, and precisely the part that deeply attracts Neil.

![The work “Sexy Robot” by Japanese illustrator Hajime Sorayama](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524033147xWXlKH8FvknBwsf5.jpg-w600)

To embody the sex robot and accentuate the curves of the female body, Neil used two materials — silver cardboard and transparent acrylic sheets — designing the top to have a cinched waist with a slightly flared hem, while the lower half was leggings, paired with silver high heels; the chest was made into cones, and he wore a translucent hat on his head.

“I often think about how to apply these (designers' works) to Frozen Lolita. Actually, drag burns a lot of money, but I'm a handmade drag queen, so it's very economical — I can even turn garbage bags into clothes,” Neil says. His special talent is turning unremarkable materials into gorgeous performance costumes.

![Neil's sex robot look (Photo / Damo)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524033445DqmcftvOBAJ1ZCXR.jpeg-w600)

Frozen Lolita (冰雪萝莉塔) is Neil's drag persona — a woman with a sharp personality and great style, who keeps people at arm's length and has an untouchable air of mystery.

“Most drag queens like to show off how glamorous women can be, but I like sad women — maybe deep down I'm a sad person. When I perform, it's like I'm in a vacuum, like everything around me has disappeared,” Neil says in another documentary, 《花开之前》(*Before the Flowers Bloom*).

Neil keeps a notebook filled with drawings of all kinds of women — these are the sources of inspiration for Frozen Lolita's looks. “I'm obsessed with Japanese animation — those Barbies, geishas, wearing super-high heels,” he says his creative inspiration probably comes from the small town in Inner Mongolia, “that cold place with grass everywhere.” “The way nature creates things is beautiful.”

Neil lip-syncs to Kylie Minogue's *Speakerphone*, paired with self-choreographed dance, working the robot's stiff movements into the routine.

This kind of performance is generally called “lip-syncing,” which demands precise lip shapes and uses exaggeration to convey a song's emotion. Lip-syncing, impersonation, and comedy are several of the common forms in drag queen performances, characterized by creativity, exaggeration, and playfulness, cleverly fusing together all kinds of conflicting elements.

![A record of Neil traveling to Shanghai last November for the drag queen competition (Photo / VICE China)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524033758ZtKi0BNbjYWxcue9.jpeg-w600)

Neil says he taught himself to dance, practicing in his bedroom at home. “When I'm home alone, I put on music in the bedroom, imagine myself on stage, imagine myself as a diva.”

When performing, the feeling of fully inhabiting another character is something Neil can't tear himself away from — and so are the audiences gathered around him.

“I really love that feeling of something being realized one hundred percent — especially when I've calculated that the audience will scream at this moment, and they really do scream, and I'm so happy. But usually, as long as you do something outrageous, everyone laughs.”

### What I Learned About Being Gay From Drag Queens

Both Neil and Kris are self-taught drag queens. Besides watching foreign drag queens' performances online for reference, they consult each other's opinions before performing — makeup, costumes, music, dance; every element is up for discussion.

“A three-minute drag performance can require months of preparation and thought,” Neil says.

Kris was first inspired by watching *RuPaul's Drag Race*, but at the time Beijing barely had any drag queen performances at all, and there was no such opportunity. It wasn't until 2016, when a friend introduced him to a performance opportunity, that he set foot on the path of drag.

Krystal de Canteur is Kris's drag persona — completely different from Frozen Lolita's icy allure. She gives off a flamboyant, retro feel.

![Kris's drag queen name is Krystal de Canteur (Photo / VICE China)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524034216XMLPkjbR5tT7xEqG.jpeg-w600)

“She's a rich, wealthy lady, quite glamorous, occasionally sexy. She often attends those private parties open only to invited guests, eats and drinks only the finest things, and drinks quite a lot, so when you usually see her she's always a bit tipsy,” Kris says. The things Krystal would do, he would never do in real life — like flirting with people on the street. “The real me wouldn't.”

Last November, when Neil decided to wear the sex robot costume to Shanghai for the national drag queen competition, Kris chose an elegant, bright golden gown, a translucent silk scarf draped over her shoulders, an exaggerated bouffant curly wig on her head, and a small flower basket in hand.

On the eve of the drag queen competition, Kris went to Shanghai to meet up with Neil, and they discussed the next day's makeup, costumes, and performance content. Kris seemed a bit worried: “We do perform in Beijing, but it's different from the queens in Shanghai — they perform every week, so they're more experienced than us. For them it's a part-time job; we're just playing in private.”

In Beijing, the drag queen circle really is even smaller. Neil believes that at this stage “there's basically no commercial scene for it in Beijing (note: performance spaces for drag queens) — it's still a very small circle. I feel that I'm not doing this just with a playful attitude; it's about bringing joy to my gay friends, for one thing.”

Kris says they have a drag queen WeChat group with a bit over a hundred people in it, but in fact most of them long ago stopped interacting.

![Neil and Kris (Photo / VICE China)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524034651oIK2Mku8xJWdBwmY.jpg-w600)

![Behind-the-scenes footage of Neil filming Self-Made Queen (Photo / provided by Neil)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180524035652UojeDyzEmTprNhfX.jpeg-w600)

Since Self-Made Queen aired, Neil and Kris say they have received quite a few messages from people hoping to become drag queens. These people previously had no idea what channels existed in China for doing this kind of performance.

This looked like a good opportunity to spread drag culture and let more people know about it. On April 18, Kris held a free drag queen teaching salon with Neil at the hair salon Kris owns.

That day was the salon's day off, and a group of people began to arrive — men who each wanted to become drag queens. There were ten in total; besides Neil and Kris, the other eight men were all “newbies” doing drag for the first time.

The theme of that day's lesson was the fundamentals of drag — makeup. The event ran from 11 a.m. to 8 p.m., a full nine hours. In the salon, they listened to RuPaul's songs while hearing Kris and Neil share their drag experiences — and applied makeup with their own hands.

“Drag makeup is free,” Kris told the newbies.

Whenever Neil recalls that day, he's always quite happy. “It's probably one of my favorite days in recent years,” he says — it was like when girls gather at someone's home to pick out bags and clothes together; the drag queens, too, now had a community.

With two years of drag queen experience, Neil says he has learned a lot from performing — not just about styling, “but about how to be gay. As a queen, she must be completely unguarded with herself — that's why everyone loves drag queens so much.”
