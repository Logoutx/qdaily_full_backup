When the plane touched down at Hong Kong International Airport, it was already nine in the evening. Yang Lin had just wrapped up a rushed two-day business trip to Beijing, but she decided to head straight back to the office to put in a few more hours. The taxi crossed the Tsing Ma Bridge, the West Kowloon high-speed rail station, and the Western Harbour Crossing. The towers of the financial district were still lit. The elevator shot up more than a hundred meters in a matter of seconds. Yang Lin set down her luggage, opened her laptop, and began preparing the documents and slides she would need the next day. She didn't walk out of the office until two in the morning.

Yang Lin is 27 this year and handles IPO business at an investment bank in Hong Kong.

In April 2018, under CEO Charles Li — described by outsiders as aggressive, even radical — HKEX launched the biggest reform in 25 years, dramatically lowering the bar for listing. It allowed companies with dual-class share structures, as well as biotech firms that had yet to turn a profit or generate revenue, to list on the exchange.

Xiaomi, Meituan, and a host of other companies intimately familiar to mainlanders rang the bell in Hong Kong. Precisely because of this, media coverage has treated this round of HKEX reform as the harvest season for so-called "new economy companies" — the batch of firms that, like Xiaomi and Meituan, had grown big riding the mobile internet boom.

![Xiaomi officially listed on HKEX on July 9. Photo/[Ninebot](http://www.sohu.com/a/240139949_99945719)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181111194945l2rKdGJMbi4OqfcU.jpeg-w600)

In the second half of 2017, Yang Lin — already working on IPO business by then — began to sense that the market was about to change.

This was no great feat of foresight. HKEX had already issued multiple consultation papers, and through its communications with the exchange, her team too believed reform was only a matter of time. "The market's view was that this would definitely happen; it was just unclear exactly which day. Once the paper came out, it meant they could formally accept your listing application."

Reform meant more companies would be allowed to list, and it meant bigger business that could not be missed. So they sent staff out to pitch listing plans and services to potential clients, and built teams for the deals to come.

Since formally entering the finance industry six years ago, Yang Lin has moved through many different kinds of work. After more than two thousand days that all looked the same, she appeared to have grown accustomed to this long, life-draining way of working — long hours, high intensity, a relentless pace. But the stretch that had just passed was still the hardest of her career so far.

For more than half a year, at least three of her five workdays each week ended after 1 a.m. At least one or two days a month she pulled all-nighters. If time allowed the next day, she would go home first and sleep six or seven hours, skip work that morning entirely, and only return to the office after lunch.

Writing pitch books, designing restructuring plans, combing through financial statements, poring over relevant laws and regulations, completing due diligence, finding buyers, filing applications and prospectuses — the whole process was long and complex. Yang Lin, who had worked on multiple IPO projects, knew all of it intimately.

Recalling what her boss had asked her at the time — "We're about to set up a new team focused on biotech. Are you interested in transferring to it?" — Yang Lin said she was genuinely excited.

Not only because HKEX's lower thresholds had drummed up a wealth of deal opportunities for them, but also because of the sheer volume of information the biotech industry itself brought.

![Hong Kong street scene. Photo/[ifeng](http://sd.ifeng.com/view/detail_2013_09/30/1286400_1.shtml)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181111195425ZUNbj9YHtC5vIBw1.jpeg-w600)

Yang Lin did not come from a medical background, but as an investment banker engaging with these biotech companies, she got to observe how medical technology was advancing. "Take science fiction — some people write about tiny robots of the future that could be put inside the human body, letting machines perform surgery. You read that and think it's science fiction; you don't think it can happen in real life." But once she actually started dealing with biotech companies, she found that some were indeed working on exactly this and trying to commercialize it at scale.

"Whenever I encounter any branch of this field, so much of the change is news to me — for me it's a state of overload from constantly learning new things," Yang Lin said. "And that's when you feel that science is still beautiful."

Yang Lin felt that in this field she could genuinely help these companies. In biotech, taking a product from project approval and R&D all the way to actually reaching the market and generating profit is a long process. Take drugs: a previous report in *Nature* noted that developing a single drug takes an average of 13.5 years and costs $1.78 billion — and that doesn't count the many examples where huge sums were spent but the drug ultimately never made it.

To survive the long, profitless stretch, biotech companies need sufficient financing channels, "but the options available on the market are generally just two: equity and debt financing." "The thing about debt is that no matter what, you have to pay it back. If the R&D fails, you still have to repay the money. For a high-risk business like biotech, that's not an ideal way to raise money."

If you don't go public, equity financing means relying on private equity funds. Private equity terms are usually harsher and the valuation lower. "If conditions allow, of course you'd still hope to list." These are all things investment bankers can help companies with. "In this environment, you meet companies like this every day, trying to find among so many the ones you think have potential, that the market will also like, hoping to help them raise money, to help them do better. That in itself is a meaningful thing."

When HKEX formally approved the reform plan in April this year, Yang Lin also grew busy. Around that time, most of her hours went into writing pitch books. But whether an investment bank wins a mandate doesn't depend on the quality of the pitch book alone — it also depends on the bank's own influence and relationships. That was not an area where Yang Lin could directly make a difference, which left her somewhat frustrated and anxious.

![Photo/[Photophoto](http://www.photophoto.cn/pic/27005954.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181111195246cWM53x9V8mGsJBrl.jpeg-w600)

Yang Lin did not have to wait long before demand from biotech companies to list exploded.

On August 1, Ascletis Pharma, a maker of drugs against hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV, became the first biotech company without profits to list on HKEX. After that, Hua Medicine, BeiGene, Innovent Biologics and others listed one after another, while more than a dozen more companies had already filed prospectuses, awaiting HKEX review and approval. According to official HKEX figures, 166 companies newly listed in the first nine months of 2018, up more than 50% over the same period in 2017.

So many companies waiting to list and raise money that it exceeded what the investment banks could handle. "Right now lots of banks are hiring everywhere, because there's a complete shortage of people. Many analysts have been put directly onto pharma-related projects," Yang Lin said. "A lot of headhunters have also been asking me whether I want to jump ship, and even my own team has been hiring nonstop — but it's really hard." The price of every analyst in Hong Kong who covers biopharma has "gone up substantially." She couldn't say exactly by how much, "but their bargaining power is now enormous."

Yang Lin's work gradually settled into a groove. She is currently leading five or six projects, not counting the ones she helps colleagues with. She now flies to the mainland five or six times a month. During the day she runs due diligence across many different locations. In the gaps between shuttling around, she has to pull out her laptop in taxis to prepare documents, or even join conference calls. At night she returns to her hotel and quietly works in her room until three or four in the morning.

"I'd see lots of my colleagues online — they were probably also out running due diligence, also away from the office, but everyone was online at three or four in the morning. 'Hey, you're here.' 'Oh, you're here too.'"

![Charles Li, current CEO of HKEX. Photo/[Asia Pacific Daily](https://zh.apdnews.com/business/finance/573816.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181111195638SKTe9IkCxf4awinO.jpeg-w600)

Yang Lin says she can be emotional at times. If a company's situation is genuinely bad, she turns very negative, thinking: you can't list or raise money on your terms anyway. But if she finds a company truly has value, "is doing something pretty good, or has a management team she quite likes, then she wants to help it list, or help it raise money."

In fact, Yang Lin's attitude toward financial markets used to border on disgust — she especially felt that finance was at its core just a numbers game. "Especially with the ups and downs of the mainland stock market — how much of it reflects actual changes in a company's value? Most price moves are caused by speculation. Yet these frothy fluctuations have real effects on individuals. When you mismanage risk and a single stock investment fails, it can bring a major change to a whole family." That was Yang Lin recalling her past thinking: "Back then I felt finance was evil, that it contributed nothing big to society."

Yang Lin likes medicine. Both her parents are surgeons, and the family environment left her with a lasting strong interest in the pharmaceutical field. After graduating from university on the mainland, she interned at an NGO while studying in Hong Kong. The NGO worked with hospitals, companies, and other parties to study the effectiveness of pilot healthcare models. It compiled the results into reports to support the making of healthcare policy. Before graduation, Yang Lin — a business school student — had also applied to public health programs, but ultimately gave up, worried that learning how to craft public health policy in the United States might not suit China's environment.

Still, although her parents were doctors, practical concerns made them not want their daughter to follow the same path. In the end, Yang Lin's college major went with the flow toward economics and finance, but she always had doubts about the decision. In the very first class after entering university, when the professor told students that economics uses costs and benefits to measure human choices and decisions, she couldn't help asking the teacher: isn't that standard for measuring the world a bit too single-minded? Many other values simply can't be summed up in gains and losses.

The change came in her sophomore year. She took part in an activity organized by the school, where a seasoned industry professional served as mentor, leading a group of students to provide financial consulting services for a small or medium-sized enterprise. When the mentor walked them through using financial analysis tools to dissect the company's financial condition, Yang Lin found that they really could propose concrete suggestions for improving the business based on it.

That also pushed Yang Lin to keep looking, within the finance industry, for the kind of work that could directly affect real economic entities. When she first started out, her work leaned more toward secondary-market trading, which left her without a sense of mission. She wanted to be "closer to the real economy," and only by slowly seeking opportunities did she finally land in her current role in investment banking, working on corporate IPOs.

Even now, with most of her energy devoted to the pharmaceutical field, she still has that feeling. Innovation in pharma moves fast — biomedicine, devices, gene technology, AI diagnostics are all constantly advancing, and related products and services keep emerging at a relentless clip. In China and abroad, agencies like the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) are working hard to keep up with the technology, issuing relevant regulations, but in many areas the rules still lag far behind the pace of the industry's development.

The April news brought a boom, but the market's turn also came fast. In the second half of 2018, companies that had chosen to list in Hong Kong were the first to feel the cold shoulder from capital. By the end of September 2018, more than 70% of that year's newly listed companies had seen their share prices fall below their IPO issue prices.

"Sentiment in the second half of last year and the first half of this year was clearly better, but now everyone is clearly more cautious," Yang Lin said. "From my contact with investors, my sense is that they're more cautious now too."

In the first half of the year, Yang Lin's investment bank was still looking to expand. When she complained to her boss about the staffing shortage, his attitude was still "if you think there's a suitable candidate, just hire them." But by the second half, the company's hiring quota had been frozen, showing a more cautious posture.

Working in finance, cycles are a problem you can never avoid.

But Yang Lin does not see all this as the start of another financial crisis. Partly it's the habitual caution of industry professionals, and partly it may be that in this industry she has seen her share of ups and downs. "I remember June 2015 — the A-share market was still above 5,000 points then, and two months later it fell below 3,000. Actually everyone realized at the time that 5,000 points was unrealistic, but it was like a game of hot potato. Then one day someone tells you the music is about to stop, and when everyone believes it, it really does stop."

In her memory, Hong Kong's entire financial circle was cutting jobs in that period. In January 2016, Barclays announced it was closing its Asian cash equities business, cutting 1,000 staff across Asia including Hong Kong. In April, JPMorgan Chase laid off 30 people in its Asian wealth management business. In June, Bank of East Asia cut 180 jobs. Over the whole year, HSBC's headcount in Hong Kong dropped by a full 1,000 people.

On a longer timescale, layoffs seem to be the norm in Hong Kong finance. Since the 2008 financial crisis, news of job cuts at major financial institutions has surfaced from time to time. Year after year of volatility, troughs, and layoffs brings a more tangible feeling. "Before you live through a market crash, this kind of worry doesn't exist in your mind — or rather, you know the worry but don't feel it could happen. Only after truly experiencing the story's ups and downs do you realize it's real."

Looking at the current market with that mindset, Yang Lin doesn't feel overly pessimistic. The nature of biotech companies means their demand for capital is huge. Once the HKEX listing channel opened up, it became one of the best ready-made financing routes available to them. She believes a bad market only knocks down the more speculative projects; good companies, companies with genuine needs, still have a chance.

![Hong Kong street scene. Photo/[699pic](http://699pic.com/tupian-500260823.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181111200450jXvg05h2TE7GVYLk.jpeg-w600)

From arriving in Hong Kong for school to the end of 2018, Yang Lin has lived in Hong Kong for eight years. After SARS, in a bid to revive Hong Kong's tourism industry and economy, the mainland signed the Individual Visit Scheme agreement with Hong Kong. After that, mainland pregnant women coming to Hong Kong to give birth and strain its medical resources, parallel traders shuttling back and forth to snap up milk powder, and a tourism boom that drove the gentrification of Hong Kong and pushed up its cost of living all made Hong Kong people increasingly resentful of mainland tourists. The tensions between the two sides gradually intensified after 2010, until February 2012, when some Hong Kong netizens took out ads in Apple Daily and Sharp Daily denouncing mainland tourists as locusts.

Yang Lin watched all these sentiments. In her first years in Hong Kong she didn't like the city at all, feeling Hong Kong people held too many misunderstandings and hostility toward the mainland. But in the end, she slowly found herself being changed by the city. Especially after starting work, exchanges with colleagues made her realize that truly hostile people toward the mainland were few, and when things came up, views could be exchanged quite smoothly. "Gradually, I developed some sense of identification with Hong Kong."

Her feelings swing between the mainland and Hong Kong. She likes the mainland's convenience — food delivery, express delivery, mobile payment — but she also likes Hong Kong's sense of order.

The biggest change is in her way of thinking. Asked whether she still insists that the economics framework of gains and losses cannot explain human motivation, she says, in a vague tone, that the economics framework is still fairly practical in some respects. "You could say this is what working in finance now, or having been in Hong Kong so long, has done to some of my ideas and thinking."

Occasionally, when work pressure is enormous, she too gets filled with all kinds of negative emotions. "This industry is really exhausting. If you don't genuinely kind of like it, you probably won't stick with it for long." A pause. Then she adds, "I really do kind of like it."

(At the interviewee's request, Yang Lin is a pseudonym.)
