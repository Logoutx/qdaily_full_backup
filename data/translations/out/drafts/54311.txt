### What the Agenda-Setters Are Talking About

**[The Conversation: Why predicting suicide is a difficult and complex challenge for humans, yet machine learning can do it](https://theconversation.com/why-predicting-suicide-is-a-difficult-and-complex-challenge-98052)**

The author of this piece is Joseph Franklin, an assistant professor of psychology at Florida State University.

This month's suicides of Kate Spade and Anthony Bourdain made people confront, almost involuntarily, that terrible question: how exactly do these tragedies happen? Those close to someone who dies by suicide always fall into agonizing second-guessing, convinced they must have fallen short somewhere — "How did I not see it coming?" Yet Franklin, who has spent the past decade studying suicide, notes with regret that today not a single suicide expert, psychologist, or physician can predict who will take their own life — and this has nothing to do with how long you've known someone or how well you know them. All they can do is nudge the odds of a correct prediction up slightly.

Scientists took 50 years to reach consensus that "suicide is hard to predict," but now a new glimmer of hope has appeared: machine learning. Several research groups have reported in recent years that machine learning can identify who has suicidal tendencies and who is most likely to die by suicide, with accuracy as high as 90%. To understand how this works, and why humans cannot predict suicide precisely, we need to know a little about the properties of human cognition and of machine learning.

Humans like explanations with two properties: first, an explanation should be simple, involving just one or a few things — for example, "depression" can neatly account for suicidal behavior; second, the explanation should ideally be fixed, meaning it can explain everything or most cases — for example, "most suicides are caused by depression" is a fixed explanation. Such simple, fixed explanations are intuitive and efficient; they help us survive, reproduce, and get through each day.

But this way of thinking does not help us understand nature or essence, because "essence" is never simple and fixed. In recent decades, scientists have finally recognized that everything — from physiology to biology to human behavior — is complex and uncertain. You have to combine a huge number of elements in a complex way to explain most things.

Take an analogy: X+Y=1 is indeterminate. As humans, we instinctively search for a solution to this equation. But there is far more than one solution — there are infinitely many. And there are infinitely many values that fail to satisfy the equation. The indeterminate middle ground between "one solution" and "anything could happen" is exactly what confuses humans, yet it is precisely how nature works.

As with everything in nature, the causes and warning signs of suicide are exceedingly complex and unstable. Hundreds of elements are linked to suicide; prediction is scarcely better than guessing. People tend to regard depression as a major key trigger of suicide, but in reality only 2% of people with major depression ultimately die by suicide — only slightly higher than the overall U.S. suicide rate of 1.6%. This result tells us that we must consider combining multiple factors to find the causes of suicide.

A relatively ideal solution would be to find, by some method, an optimized combination of dozens to hundreds of factors. Machine learning can do this. It can now draw on data from electronic health records and scan hundreds of factors related to mental-health diagnoses, physical conditions, medications, demographics, and patterns of medical visits. The resulting optimized combination can successfully predict 80–90% of suicide cases. But people must also realize that the algorithm's solution is not the only one.

Still, although machine learning can foresee "who will die by suicide," it cannot predict "when" — and it may take humans many years to crack that problem. In the meantime, what we can do is care for the people around us. If you know someone who is struggling, talk to them and suggest they call a suicide-prevention hotline.

### 24-Hour Newsmakers

**[New York State sues Trump for illegally profiting from his charitable foundation.](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2018/jun/14/trump-charity-sued-latest-new-york-attorney-general-violations)**

On Thursday — Donald Trump's 72nd birthday — New York State Attorney General Barbara Underwood sued Trump and his three children, who serve as directors of the foundation, accusing them of illegally using their charitable foundation as a personal "checkbook," in violation of state charity law.

The suit alleges that the foundation amassed assets through tax-deductible donations, which Trump then used for his own benefit, for political promotion and to buy personal items. The investigation uncovered at least five instances of self-dealing that benefited the Trump Organization, including the settlement of a legal dispute involving one of his golf clubs and the purchase of a US$10,000 portrait of Trump to decorate another club.

Trump also held a charity fundraiser in Iowa in early 2016, but it was in fact a campaign event — he even participated in a televised debate — and its purpose was not at all "raising money for veterans," as the event claimed. The attorney general said Trump's aides and campaign manager at the time dictated the timing, amounts, and recipients of payments after the fundraiser, violating state and federal law. Under New York State law, charitable foundations are in most cases prohibited from engaging in political activity in order to maintain their "charitable" status.

The New York Attorney General's office began investigating the foundation in June 2016 and ordered it to stop raising funds. Trump said after the election that he would dissolve the foundation, but then-Attorney General Eric Schneiderman blocked the move on the grounds that an investigation was still underway.

Underwood is seeking a US$2.8 million penalty from the foundation and US$1 million in restitution to other charities. The suit also seeks to dissolve the foundation and to bar Trump from serving as a director of any charitable foundation for 10 years (and his three children for one year).

Trump immediately tweeted that the lawsuit was the work of "sleazy New York Democrats," claiming his foundation gave away more than it received, and that the case was filed only because he had been unwilling to settle at the time.

> "BREAKING NEWS! A German from 100 years ago didn't happen to hold views on race that are widely accepted by society today, like modern people do! What garbage! I guess this means we should remove all of his contributions to science?"

So commented Reddit user [caine269](https://www.reddit.com/r/news/comments/8qoo1s/einsteins_private_diaries_reveal_shocking/) on the Guardian story ["Einstein's private diaries reveal his xenophobic tendencies"](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/jun/12/einsteins-travel-diaries-reveal-shocking-xenophobia).

The newly published *The Travel Diaries of Albert Einstein* from Princeton University Press reveals that this physicist, long regarded as a humanitarian, held discriminatory attitudes toward Asian peoples, especially the Chinese.

Einstein traveled through Asia in the 1920s. In his diaries he described Chinese people as "industrious, filthy, obtuse people" with a "peculiar herd-like nature" — "a pity if these people supplant all other races." In a later passage written to his stepdaughter in Berlin, he continued, "Even those who work like horses never seem to have any awareness of pain; a herd-like nation, more like automatons than human beings."

Ze'ev Rosenkranz, the book's editor and assistant director of the Einstein Papers Project at Caltech, said, "A lot of what's in there is really unpleasant, especially what he says about the Chinese... it stands in stark contrast to his great humanitarian public image. Reading this, and then thinking of his public statements ('racial discrimination is a disease of white people'), is quite shocking. They were written without any expectation that they would ever be published."

He pointed out that Einstein regarded foreign races as a "threat" — a striking characteristic of racism; as for his failure to understand "why unattractive Chinese women could attract Chinese men to reproduce with them," that was an extremely misogynistic trait. Einstein's views were fairly common for his time — that is the broader social context — though progressive figures with more tolerant views already existed back then.

He noted in his introduction that exploring why a man regarded as a symbol of humanity would write such xenophobic words is of great importance, "and the answer to that question is especially relevant to today's world, given the rampant acts of hatred happening in so many places. It seems that sometimes even Einstein had a hard time seeing himself clearly when facing others."

**[Since Mexico's election campaign began last September, 113 political candidates and 8 journalists have been killed](https://www.pri.org/stories/2018-06-13/unprecedented-wave-political-violence-rocks-mexico)**.

Last week, Mexican congressional candidate Fernando Puron was shot dead while taking a selfie with supporters, becoming the 133rd political figure murdered since the election began; the killing was captured on video.

The general election formally begins on July 1, yet Mexico faces an unprecedented nationwide wave of violence. The campaign process started last September, with more than 3,400 political seats up for grabs, and the authorities cannot provide adequate security for candidates — especially those in small towns and rural areas. A total of 133 candidates and 8 journalists have been killed, 72% of violent incidents have targeted opposition candidates, and three female candidates were murdered on the same day this month.

The country's mainstream media mostly attribute political violence to drug cartels, as if all the violence were connected to the drug war. The truth, however, is that these cartels usually serve as the armed wings of powerful political groups, and are often used to execute opposition figures and journalists who bravely expose corruption.

The consequence of this violence is that Mexican voters find themselves with ever less information and ever fewer choices at the ballot box.
