Adidas Bets Its Comeback in Performance and the North American Market on a White Hoodie
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
Everything the company wants to say rests on this hoodie: professional credibility and everyday consumer appeal; technology and fashion. But will it all go as planned?
@@QD_BODY@@
Everything the company wants to say rests on this hoodie: professional credibility and everyday consumer appeal; technology and fashion. But will it all go as planned?

At 7 p.m. Eastern Time on August 30, roughly 300 people queued up outside 550 Washington Street in Manhattan, New York, filing in one after another. Adidas had gathered media from around the world to unveil a brand-new category called adidas Athletics — in its Chinese marketing language, the "Sport Style Series."

The vast white space, called Skylight Clarkson SQ, is one of the largest event venues in New York today, covering more than 5,500 square meters. Inside were eight concept installations showing how several sponsored athletes keep their focus before taking the field. Next to them sat a showroom displaying the first season of Athletics products.

Adidas spared no effort in promoting the new line. An Adidas New York staffer handing out white hoodies to attendees told QDaily that the event was originally limited to New York locals, and that it "somehow" ballooned into a 300-person global invitation. Beyond media from various countries, the guest list included New York's fashionable crowd and hot bloggers, as well as a host of Adidas executives and employees.

Adidas stressed that the new line was "designed for athletes." And the "star item" it pushed hardest was a white hoodie.

The hoodie sells for $100. Its full name is the Z.N.E (zero negative energy) Hoodie. It claims to block out noise, reduce visual distractions, and guarantee comfort, helping athletes stay highly focused. The hood is roomy enough to fit over-ear headphones, "thoughtfully accommodating athletes' habit of listening to music before taking the field."

In the press materials Adidas released simultaneously, NBA star James Harden was shown wearing this very hoodie. Harden was reportedly due to appear at the event that night, but in the end he never showed up. The interview program was reshuffled on the fly, the party music became the focus, and drinks flowed without limit.

Having neglected both the professional arena and the U.S. market for quite some time, Adidas opened its comeback with a series of sportswear pieces. According to Kate Ridley, vice president of the training category for Adidas North America, speaking to QDaily, the line balances trend elements with functionality. "We can't say one factor matters more to the customer, because consumers today won't compromise. If it's a high-performance product, it can't be ugly — it also has to look good while meeting functional needs."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160905230311ClMF3oHszRarSyjP.jpg-w600)

The training line that Kate Ridley belongs to has grown increasingly important to Adidas in recent years, yet its product portfolio remains sprawling, covering men's and women's training apparel, women's sports bras, and other athletic wear. Even so, you can hardly find a clear entry point for this product line on Adidas's official website — it sits in small gray type behind main entries like basketball and soccer. Meanwhile, you can barely dig up a clear founding date for this line. Inside Adidas, it seems to have never been a star.

Out of a desire to "break away from the sprawling training system," Athletics became an independent line, though it still falls under training. Kate Ridley stressed to QDaily: "We have two different brands targeting different groups. Original focuses more on street fashion and street culture; we (training) focus more on sports. So Athletics also puts a lot of emphasis on performance benefits for athletes in its product design."

Ridley's notion of an athlete is very broad by Adidas's definition. "Even if you only run once a week, you're still an athlete," Sigrid Buehrle, global senior director of product for the Athletics category, said in an interview with QDaily. "If you want to call yourself an athlete who runs once a week, then no problem — this product line lets you show off your athletic side."

A similar formulation exists at Adidas's biggest rival, Nike — Nike calls ordinary consumers "everyday athletes." However, when Nike emphasizes the professionalism of its products, it never associates them with ordinary people; it always talks about serving professional athletes.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160905230336isc02IoNaHZO7FVd.jpg-w600)

Kicking off the launch in New York with a hoodie, the German company Adidas was read by many media outlets as "trying to revive the U.S. market." Apparel accounts for 41% of Adidas's revenue, slightly below footwear, and its growth rate is less than half of the latter's. In 2014, Adidas's North American revenue fell to €2.21 billion (about $2.46 billion), rising to €2.75 billion (about $3.07 billion) in 2015. By contrast, Nike's home-field advantage is plain to see: its 2015 North American revenue was $13.74 billion.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160906101057Xtr1GqQNs0SFheKm.jpg-w600)

The U.S. is Adidas's largest market (followed by China and Russia), and a must-win territory for sportswear brands. It covers nearly half of global sneaker and athletic apparel sales, is the densest ground for sneaker culture, and the birthplace of the latest sports trends.

The hoodie also carries a cultural meaning in the U.S. unlike anywhere else: beyond being its birthplace, it is equated with "cool" and "rebellion." When an athlete's resolute face hides beneath the hood, and a single-tone frame always seems to create an atmosphere where performance and cool go hand in hand — Nike's high-end sportswear line from last fall and winter shot its lookbook in almost exactly the same way.

Over the following week, Adidas hoped this atmosphere would quickly spread to global markets. On September 10, some 20,000 athletes around the world would post photos on social media in their respective countries, appearing in this white hoodie — like some enormous gang on the internet displaying its arsenal. Ridley stressed that they had never attempted this method, at this scale, before.

It's worth noting that many of these athletes may have been recruited by Adidas only in the past couple of years. Earlier this year, Adidas announced that by 2020 it would raise the number of NFL (National Football League) players it sponsors from 95 to 250, and its sponsored NBA players from 70 to 100. Having enough athletes — influential ones — will be the fundamental guarantee for a sportswear brand to wield its subsequent marketing magic in the U.S. market.

On the same day as the Athletics launch, Adidas announced the signing of legendary quarterback Tim Tebow, because he was switching to baseball. Adidas executives including Kate Ridley looked quite excited. "You know, we've just been in the middle of picking people. Each of us has a long backup list, but my memory is so bad I can hardly remember their names."

Dramatically, while the Athletics launch event was taking place in New York, 62-year-old Adidas CEO Herbert Hainer was visiting Russia over matters concerning the 2018 World Cup. This was the final month before his departure.

In an interview with The Wall Street Journal, he mentioned that Adidas had now found the trick to its transformation: "We give brand managers more authority and responsibility. They now oversee everything from design to R&D to the final product, and they're also responsible for the entire supply chain. Our old system was more a little... let's say, 'feudal.'"

Adidas's "Germanized" conservative way of operating had been in place for too long. Since 2012, Adidas's growth curve came to an abrupt halt, and its market value at one point was only one-fifth of Nike's. In May 2015, Eric Liedtke — now Adidas's global brand director and a board member — told the media that young athletes no longer chased after Adidas the way they did ten or twenty years ago. "I think a lot of the time we're not even in the consideration set. That's what makes me sad."

Starting in 2015, Adidas undertook a series of major personnel changes affecting 40,000 of its 54,000 global employees, restructuring the organization and eliminating as many as 30 vice-president positions. Liedtke was one of the lucky ones promoted out of turn; 16 people now report directly to him. "On my (team) there's now just one person in charge of winning soccer," he said. "If he can't do it in 18 months, I have to replace him. Sorry, folks — the company now runs on a performance culture."

The recently announced adidas X KANYE WEST brand, along with the adidas Athletics line, may be the two "big projects" successfully launched during this period by brand managers who had been set free.

In the U.S., Adidas has always been associated with hip-hop culture. Since the rapper Run-DMC released the song "My Adidas" in 1986, track jackets and shell-toe Superstars took off, leading the company in 2002 to split its apparel division into performance and fashion halves. And for Adidas, now facing a share crisis, athleisure has become a lifeline.

Last year, when Yeezy shoes were at their peak, Hainer explained: "I think it's fair to say sport has been sublimated into fashion, a lifestyle, and streetwear. I estimate 80% of basketball shoes may never have set foot on a court. People wear them just to make a statement and look cool. Sport is indeed connected to lifestyle, and at Adidas we certainly want to benefit from that."

Indeed, Adidas's results have shown signs of turning up. Over these 18 months, anyone who follows fashion knows what happened: Stan Smiths swept the streets, Yeezys were flipped at sky-high prices, and people queued like mad for NMDs. And if you follow the company, the news you kept hearing was Adidas building some "new thing" somewhere — the local German "Speed Factory," or the new studio in Brooklyn, New York, with more and more product launches held in New York — from the Yeezy line, to new Ultra Boosts, and now Athletics.

But given that this is a company whose main pitch is athletic performance, Adidas's showing on non-sport product lines may risk stealing the show. According to the company's latest disclosed financials, Adidas posted 99% net profit growth for three consecutive quarters, with quarterly revenue up 21% to €4.4 billion. Lifestyle and footwear still contributed the bulk of profits: adidas Originals grew 36%, while sportswear grew only 10%.

Few people today know that Adidas's signature three stripes originated in founder Adi Dassler's 1948 technical innovation — using three stripes on the side of the shoe to make it fit athletes' foot shape better. And this spring's smash hit, the NMD, actually already uses the two most advanced technologies in Adidas sneakers: the Boost sole and the Primeknit upper. But don't expect that to reinforce the high-tech side of Adidas in people's minds, because almost no one plays streetball in NMDs and Yeezy Boosts.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160906002513rHk3cPqw1faNiGsJ.jpeg-w600)

NPD Group once released a survey finding that only 25% of Americans who buy sneakers actually use them for sports. True, the sports industry is like an off-road vehicle: high performance is the premise of consumption. But on the other hand, blindly following Wall Street's wishes and market opinion may lift revenue in the short term — over the long run, Adidas could also miss the future.

Some analysts had previously argued that Adidas pushed too hard in promoting Originals. John Horan, founder of the Sporting Goods intelligence firm, used beer as a metaphor when discussing the phenomenon of Adidas's hit products: "Imagine a glass of beer. Your fashion business should be the beer foam; performance is the beer itself. They (Adidas) have a lot of foam and not enough beer."

And the remark from Matt Powell, sports market analyst and vice president at NPD Group, has circulated even more widely: "You have to choose a lane and decide who you want to be."

Judging from the information revealed at the launch event, Adidas still seemed to fall short on articulating technology. Asked how she planned to promote product technology to consumers, Kate Ridley said: "If our product contains a technology, like Climacool, we'll have a tag on the garment." This approach has been used by the entire sportswear industry — Adidas included — for at least 10 years or more.

"After a while, I think we figured out how to do it in the U.S.," Herbert Hainer told The Wall Street Journal. Over the past 18 months, the company had completely changed the way it was managed. In early 2016, the Adidas board asked him to leave in September — half a year before his contract was due to end. He considered this unfair.
