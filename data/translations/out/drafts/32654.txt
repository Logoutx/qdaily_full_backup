@@QD_BODY@@
Xiao Laohu says he can feel hip-hop is about to blow up, and there's a good chance he'll be the one stoking the fire.

Xiao Laohu doesn't swear when he battles.

A battle is hip-hop jargon for, simply put, two people talking trash at each other. Two rappers either agree on a spot or just happen to run into each other, and it's like stepping onto a battlefield: one says how great he is, the other immediately fires back about how dumb the first one is. Of course it's not a street quarrel — you do it over music, off the top of your head, and in the end the roar of the crowd basically decides who wins.

Profanity is common in battles — gutter stuff, genitalia, the more vicious the better. Even on proper albums, R-rated, even NC-17-level words keep forcing their way into your ears. That's because hip-hop comes from Black street culture, where drugs, prostitutes and guns all overlap; art comes from life, and a life like that naturally gives birth to art laced with dirty words.

After crossing the ocean to China, some people decided hip-hop had to stay raw and authentic. Sure, they may have grown up in a Beijing hutong or a southern qilou arcade, never smoked weed, never been in jail — but when they rap, none of that can be missing.

Xiao Laohu doesn't quite agree. He says that's failing to find your real self. "Maybe once you really find yourself in hip-hop, you're not that gangster, and you're not that by-the-book either — you walk in the middle, you walk your own way. I think that's really important."

That's also why Xiao Laohu is especially sensitive about "English" in battles. At the 2007 "Dragon-Tiger Duel MC Battle" competition, he called out an opponent for rapping all in English even though he wasn't a foreigner. In 2008, he opened with: "Let me ask you one last question — is rapping for yourself, or for speaking English?"

He won both battles. In his résumé from then on, they'd be mentioned again and again as the start of his fame. After that he became "one of China's best freestyle rappers," and an Abilu Music Award winner.

Now he's also become director of the hip-hop channel at LeEco Music. The title sounds fancy, but in fact he doesn't manage anyone. LeEco has several hundred similar "directors," and he's nothing special among them.

But taking on the identity still added a strange new hue to a man who raps, "So what if you can rhyme every word under heaven. Better to flip this mahjong table and listen to the waves together." Inside a massive system like that, can his style still stay free?

In our interview, Xiao Laohu said he doesn't like the regimented life of a company — he even calls some of his previous jobs a dark chapter. But when the conversation turns to his work at LeEco, Xiao Laohu stops being casual and stops cursing; instead he talks about gathering statistics, doing research, pushing things forward. He wants to use the LeEco platform to make hip-hop blow up. He says it already feels close — it just needs a trigger point. He doesn't know what that trigger point is, though.

Not long ago, Xiao Laohu also shot the documentary *Beating Children* (《打孩子》). He went to six cities and filmed the lives of six local rappers. That, too, is part of pushing hip-hop forward.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161002130541Sbr8KRTgvLmxkDH3.jpg-w600)

It's clear that borrowing commercial machinery and the resources of a big company is Xiao Laohu's most realistic idea right now. He believes hip-hop will blow up one day. In fact, in the US, hip-hop is already one of the hottest music genres. Now, as hip-hop shows up more and more on mainstream variety shows, and more and more people come to ask him about the hip-hop business, the day it blows up feels closer and closer.

But—

"And after it blows up?"

Hearing this question, Xiao Laohu lowered his head, rubbed his face, and was quiet for a long while.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160926230715xT8sNBP29QGm0pi6.jpg-w600)

We talked with Xiao Laohu about his life, his rapping, and his experience of a big company.

Below, T = Xiao Laohu, Q = QDaily

**Q: When did you decide to make hip-hop your pursuit?**

T: I've always thought that deciding to make it a pursuit happened the moment I first heard the music — learning this thing existed, I instantly loved it. I was in high school, and there was this teacher — a history teacher — who ran an elective course on rock music and art-house cinema, teaching the history of a lot of art films and rock. Through that, I started to love music.

Because before that, when I was little, there was music class. Staff notation, and singing — we sang, and I went up to sing together with another boy. He was much cuter than me — a good-looking kid with big dark eyes, good with words, the teachers liked him. We sang together, and I thought he was the one going off-key, but the teacher said I was off-key. I felt I wasn't off-key, but the teacher sent me down: you're not fit to sing with him, get down! After that I developed a huge aversion to music — and it has haunted me to this day. Look, I have zero music theory, and I'm not interested in many instruments; I like them but I can't do anything with them. Because whenever music class came around and I had to deal with the teacher, I got miserable, I hated it, so I had no interest in music. Even ordinary stuff like pop songs — I didn't much like those either. When I was a kid my brother bought a Michael Jackson cassette, and I bought one too; I listened and thought it was pretty great, but I never really listened to anything else. Until I met that teacher, took that elective, and started getting interested in music. And back then, what you encountered was always bigger than music — it wasn't just the music itself. It taught you a wild way of life, a rebellious set of values, gave you a defiant character — including a lot of philosophy the textbooks never taught you, new ideas, offbeat knowledge. Your brain just exploded: this is fucking amazing.

**Q: Especially absorbing all this in high school.**

T: Right. The first stuff I encountered was through buying those notch-cut import CDs — discounted discs with a saw cut in them — with my buddies. A buddy tossed one to me: listen to this, it's rap. I took it — really? Let me hear it. One listen and I knew it was nothing like the rock I'd been hearing — a simple drum, just a drum, just a guitar, always those same two notes, that kind of feeling. And a few guys spitting hard over it. I thought, this is so interesting — it sounds so simple but hits so hard, and your head starts nodding free. After that I got into Eminem, another shock. Eminem has that movie *8 Mile* — hearing his music, I feel that movie was a shock for me too. Back then I knew about the movie and had just gotten into hip-hop; I thought Black people all dressed super cool, platinum chains and all, expecting that kind of life with lots of girls, pure swag, pure swagger.

But look at Eminem in that movie — the guy's a total loser, the whole film is gray, that industrial city, Detroit's decay and depression, and this man always so repressed, failing, shy, living by himself in a beat-up trailer, his mom getting fucked by some guy his own age — just piles of his own rotten mess, his girlfriend fucked by his rival, all that stuff. And in the end he wins, but he still turns and walks away, back to his factory, back to the repressed life. I still remember it: in high school I bought a pirated VCD, two discs, and after watching it I didn't feel that deeply. I thought, this isn't the hip-hop I imagined — I'd just fallen for this music, thinking it was this incredibly badass thing; how did it end up like this? But every time since, remembering it, I think that film is great, and it's exactly the thing I love about hip-hop: music — this kind of music — leads you out of a poor, dignity-less life, but only for that one moment. That's art too; it's all the same. I don't believe art changes the world, and I don't believe art overthrows governments. People always cite the Plastic People of the Universe from Czechoslovakia, but I always feel that stops being beautiful in the end — once you succeed, you're not beautiful anymore, right? So it doesn't have that much power, but it can at least yank you out of your life for a few minutes, and that's enough. That's why I've always loved this thing — that's the whole story.

**Q: Literature is a bit like that too. Maybe part of it can change a bit of reality, but the most important thing is the feeling while you're reading that suddenly none of reality matters.**

T: Right — it carries your soul away, or lets you look at the thing from another dimension. Actually it should make you more detached, not more radical.

![A still from *8 Mile*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609262300339KycDVFER0MGhH6N.jpg-w600)

**Q: Right — but you studied film in college, didn't you?**

T: Film production, but honestly speaking, honestly speaking, it had little to do with film or TV, because the education was terrible — really terrible. It didn't teach you more creation-related things, and the teachers all just worked part-time: teach you a bit, then go take other jobs. But the good thing was I kept playing music all through college, kept rapping.

I mean, back then with a few guys — I was in the management school, studying production management. Actually production isn't that related to artistic creation; it's more a business — how to manage the process, how to invest in a cultural project and ultimately get a return. But I wasn't that interested in it. I loved film, but I didn't want to do production, and I felt I didn't learn anything real. The moment I got to college, during military training, I fell in with the recording arts school — basically the people in our school who handled sound, arranging, recording, the studio, music-making, post-production — because there were lots of creators there. To put it plainly, I just played music with them, played in bands, gigged out, performed on campus. And in my junior year — or the second semester of sophomore year — I entered a Beijing underground rap competition, a bit like Eminem entering 8 Mile, and took second place. Anyway, I came out of nowhere — who is this guy, where'd he come from? After I surfaced, I signed with a record label and started putting out records, yeah.

**Q: Did it feel like being famous?**

T: A little. Before that I wasn't in that circle, but I'd always loved it; I knew there were people in Beijing and around the country making this music, that there was a scene, and I'd follow it all online, listen to it, and occasionally go to their shows. But I didn't like hanging out with them — I don't like casually getting to know people. I'd quietly buy a ticket, stand at the back, and watch those Beijing rappers of the older generation spit while a crew played around — everyone saying it was so cool. I'd watch, then take a cab home. But that time I couldn't hold back; I went up too — I could spit. And then those guys started coming at you: who are you? Where do you get off being so hot?

**Q: And then you gradually entered the scene?**

T: Not really. I've always kept a certain distance. After I got in, people knew who I was, but pretty soon I still wanted to make things, wanted to express myself — not so into all the socializing. Because this rap music, I find it interesting — it also stresses unity, a sense of the group.

Say you're an honest kid, but you listen to that vicious rap and you feel like you're a thug too, or you feel you've got power — that's art's hypnotic function. Or you hang with a crew and feel you're somebody: you were a shy, unconfident person, but with a crew around you, you feel you have face, you're dazzling. I've always been a bit averse to that part — not that I don't rely on it at all. When it might boost my confidence, the more I want to reject it, because I don't think that's a true, independent ease. So the aversion keeps me from really embedding in the scene and enjoying the ease that comes from relationships.

**Q: Right — I noticed your documentary mostly describes their state of life when they're alone.**

T: Right, I think that's very important, because I think the interesting thing about hip-hop is you find yourself, and then you find how you differ from others — those two things are really important. Lots of people, after finding hip-hop, only find a path to rebel against the current rules, the good-student way of life. But they fall into another kind of huddling together, another kind of blindness — I'm just mixed into a different crowd.

![The documentary *Beating Children* (《打孩子》), produced by Xiao Laohu](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161002130417t0xTCUj3YS8eWBbK.jpg-w600)

**Q: Pretending to be a gangster or whatever.**

T: Right — because maybe once you really find yourself in hip-hop, you're not that gangster and you're not that by-the-book, but you walk in the middle, you walk your own way. I think that's really important.

**Q: After graduation, what jobs did you do first?**

T: After graduation, I didn't much want to work for a long time. Back when I was still in school I put out records, but I kept writing pieces for magazines, music-related pieces. Writing, I could get a few hundred yuan a month — a very meager fee, not enough to live on. After graduating I was naive for a while, thinking, why don't I just grab some random job — work by day to make a bit of money, then at night take off the suit and go rap again. I was obsessed with that fantasy. I found a job selling apartments — I even went to the interview.

**Q: Selling apartments?**

T: Something like Wo Ai Wo Jia, the real estate agency.

**Q: You really would have worked in a suit.**

T: I went, and they said, what are you doing at this interview? Aren't you from Communication University? I said, never mind that — is this a job or not? Can I do it? He said you can, you can, just get a license, take the exam in June. He was telling me this with a white wall behind him, and I watched a cockroach crawl across it. Honestly it was a bit disgusting, you know? So I left — the room was tiny, one guy sitting behind a desk talking to me, cockroaches behind him — quite surreal, like *Naked Lunch*, you know that film? After I left I said I'd think about it, and I never thought about it again. I stayed home for a few months, then went to Time Out, the lifestyle magazine.

But that was right when the financial crisis hit — even if you joined, they wouldn't hire you, only internships. I interned for half a year without getting paid, you know? Brutal. The first three months of the internship I just uploaded content to the website — pure mechanical work. They gave you a 200-yuan meal stipend. In the last three months I started turning to writing a few pieces, and they said, you can write? Why didn't you say so? I said I can write a bit. So I wrote, and got maybe one or two thousand yuan a month in fees, roughly. Then after a while I did a really weird job: it was a magazine publishing group, and I worked on the group's internal newsletter — like the neighborhood-committee bulletin for sharing information — exactly that kind of thing. Daily interviews about what everyone was up to, and I was a free spirit, I guess. Made very little money, but I stayed, had lots of time, and nobody really minded me.

**Q: So you still had time to keep creating back then.**

T: On the whole I never went through that kind of struggle — where real life keeps you from making music anymore and you have to do something about it. Because I found I couldn't throw myself into making money — like finding a job and working hard at earning. At most you'd have me do other things, but I absolutely had to make music, absolutely had to rap; it was the thing I loved most, so there wasn't much struggle. Later, my demands on life weren't that high either.

**Q: How did you end up on *Very Close Distance* (《非常静距离》)? I heard that counts as a dark chapter for you.**

T: It really is a dark chapter. The other day my girlfriend sent me a clip from *Very Close Distance*. I said, that's so embarrassing. Back then there was a senior alumna — they were all in the TV industry — who came to me: didn't you rap? We're doing a Chinese version of the Ellen show, the host is Li Jing, she wants that variety-heavy feel — so, rap. He asked if I was interested. I found a DJ I knew, came in for one audition, they said great, let's start. And it started.

That job was really simple for me. Simple how? I felt I was using rap as a tool, and through that process I realized something: I can't do entertainment — I can't do that kind of entertainment. Not that I can't be entertaining at all, but that TV brand of entertainment, I can't do it. One, I had to put on makeup every day. Two, they thought my expressions looked stiff. Three — the ultimate goal they set for me was: you have to become a Chen Handian type — you've got to be able to move around, you can't just spit one segment; you've got to be able to play with people, you've got to be willing to play the fool, you know? I'm not saying Chen Handian is an idiot — I think he's pretty badass.

But I realized I'm not that person; I don't have that talent. What interests me is what I love; maybe my take differs a bit from others, maybe I can improvise some different opinions on the spot. But asking me to truly entertain myself — I don't enjoy it, so I couldn't keep doing it. The stalemate went on quite a while, until they said, if you can't do it, then that's that.

It was also part of my income: I'd go there and record three episodes in one day, each opening just 15 or 30 seconds where I improvised an intro of that day's guest — say, he's a singer, he's... ambiguous, looks like a donkey — but you had to say he's Li Yuchun, that kind of thing. Very simple, but the writers kept making me write scripts. I said I can't write them, just let me improvise. They couldn't be bothered to care.

Every time I'd haul out to Daxing, a really far-flung place, to record. One episode, 30 seconds, done — then I'd lie down backstage with a book, play some games, rest. Two or three hours later I'd record another, another two or three hours, another, then go home and have a meal.

**Q: Overall the workload sounds light.**

T: Very light, and for my age back then, the money was enough to live on — super relaxed. Record a dozen-plus episodes a month, get a good several thousand, about ten thousand yuan, roughly like that. I just did that much; no need to show up at an office; I was fine. The thing is, I was still working that magazine job then, and I'd squeeze the recording in between work. So it went on for a while, and then it ended — they later revamped the show, and I heard they brought in a Black guy to do the talking, all for the ratings anyway.

![A still from *Naked Lunch*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609262302525nOCgHQMYpEqDxPB.jpg-w600)

**Q: You just joined LeEco this year?**

T: Right.

**Q: So before joining LeEco, you were at that magazine the whole time?**

T: Right. Around 2013 I left the magazine, because I had made the record *Wanderer* (《逍遥客》) and wanted to do a national tour. I figured I'd be away from Beijing for a long stretch, and the job — although it didn't take much of my energy — I felt every minute I just wanted to be by myself. So I thought, this isn't fair to them either; no point in them always putting up with my slackness. I left entirely.

**Q: So after that—**

T: After that, no.

**Q: No formal job?**

T: Right.

**Q: So when did LeEco's invitation come?**

T: Around last year. A good friend of mine is at LeEco, and he came to me: are you interested in building a hip-hop platform here? This thing — I think you're a great fit; you've got a clear head, you've got pull, and you know all the grassroots hip-hop people, where they all are. Do it! I thought, OK. But at the end of last year I was making my new record *Color Weakness* (《色弱》) — the album with Soulspeak — and I was about to do a national tour. At that point I conceived two ideas: one was to shoot a tour documentary, called *Bullshitting Animals* (《扯淡动物》) — that's the one that came out. The other was the *Beating Children* series, because I was going to more than ten places across China — I thought, why not shoot a documentary series recording the lives of people making hip-hop in different places? My tour would reach those places anyway, so I just filmed along the way.

**Q: So you could visit the local people, local friends, along the way.**

T: Right. And LeEco Music gave huge support — basically covering all the shooting costs and so on. That started our cooperation: it began with works and creation, not with management or "big platform" talk from day one. I find that way comfortable — let's actually do some things, stop talking airy stuff all the time, right?

**Q: Right. So what exactly are your responsibilities there now?**

T: If you want the grandest version, the job is truly building a so-called Chinese hip-hop ecosystem platform. It's not just running the website. The most basic part: you bring in good overseas content, you shoot and create good domestic content, and you turn it into — first of all — a video platform where everyone can see the freshest hip-hop content, domestic and foreign. Another part: you bring the power of musicians, domestic and foreign, to settle in on this platform, where you can directly get their latest info, see their creations, even participate in consuming their works. Then there's a lot of offline shows — organizing things like bringing Stones Throw, a legendary LA-based American hip-hop platform, to China this year, and bringing Jingqishen, a legendary Guangzhou music label, to Beijing. It gathers young people's interesting poetry collections, talks, sharing sessions, exchanges, performances, and exhibitions — because both of these labels have ten, twenty years of history, produced countless records, lots of design activity, lots of precious archival material, including films — so it's a concentrated exhibition of all of that.

All in all, going forward I really want to make this into — later on we might well do an idol-style talent show, make more documentaries, all about hip-hop — build it into a true gathering platform for hip-hop culture, and a brand that can even take on record-label duties for these musicians in a new era.

**Q: I saw you said before you wanted to bring *Show Me the Money* to China.**

T: The licensing import is already done. Whether to do a Chinese version — whether it'll be *Show Me the Money*, or a local project borrowing its format — is still being planned and negotiated.

**Q: So there's no very concrete plan yet.**

T: We definitely will do it, but the exact form is unclear, because a reality show is a very big, very complex project, so it needs proper thought.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160926230434GyguCmVW9FpsAZM6.jpeg-w600)

**Q: How many people report to you?**

T: Just her. (laughs) Can't call it reporting — we work together. There are quite a lot of partners overall, because LeEco is one big whole; it's not necessarily split by department, like "your department has this many people" — everyone works on all projects together.

**Q: You just said you're pretty loose about work — does that affect your work at LeEco?**

T: I think it's fine. I've grown up.

**Q: Do you still have time to tour?**

T: I think that's a shared understanding. Though in some sense I'm a manager, even an operator, I'm more an independent musician. On that point I think LeEco Music is fairly respectful — it really respects the individual — and that matters a lot going forward.

**Q: When LeEco Music came to you, or before that, were there other job offers?**

T: I don't think I ever considered it. Many artists came to me with offers — to make me an artist, a more professional musician — and I basically turned them all down. I feel this era is one where all the big record companies have collapsed, and it's an era where you insist on being yourself, make an effort to be individual, know clearly who you are, and don't want to easily attach yourself to an institution or to anyone.

**Q: LeEco Music doesn't interfere with your creation, or your art?**

T: Definitely — relatively free. Because lots of the people at LeEco Music came from rock-music media back in the day, from this kind of music media; they really understand music, and what they understand is non-mainstream music — rock, hip-hop. Even the girls I work with all listen to the newest music. They all get what youth culture is — that can't be captured by rigid dogma or company rules; that produces no charisma.

**Q: Roughly how much time do you need for making music?**

T: By inspiration, and by physical doing — most of the day, honestly. On this I believe what Haruki Murakami says: you can't just wait for inspiration to strike one day like lightning. You feed it four or five hours every day; maybe you feed it for a week and nothing comes out — it's like a monster, a blob of chaos — but you've given it the time, and one day a thread suddenly emerges from the chaos, and you grab it in that moment. You must feed it regularly, every day; that's far more reliable than waiting for some sudden force to appear.

**Q: How do you feed it?**

T: Just sit there. You can freestyle — a lot of improvised rapping — or you just sit and think. Music: dare to think, operate, write.

**Q: What music do you usually listen to?**

T: Basically all of it.

**Q: Including mainstream pop?**

T: I listen very little to Chinese mainstream pop — except Taiwanese pop of the Teresa Teng, Fong Fei-fei era; some Hong Kong pop I heard early on, sure. Current stuff, not much, truly.

**Q: Because it's still dominated by ballads.**

T: Way behind. Lots of people ask me when hip-hop can go mainstream. In the US that's not a question — for 20 years, seven of the top ten on the charts have been hip-hop. And we're still on about this — from square-dance music to those love songs, still talking about it. I think in this era everyone can see it: lots of post-90s kids say to me, "your era is coming." I ask why they say that. They say, all the college kids I know, all the people I know are listening to people like you, this kind of music, certain rock, certain folk — nobody listens to that pop, that idiotically packaged pop anymore. I think that's totally normal; it should've been this way long ago. In an era with information this abundant, where everyone wants a bit of uniqueness, a bit of something realer and more current — you have to be this kind of creation, not that outdated pop packaging.

**Q: American hip-hop is tied to rebellion, but Chinese hip-hop is a bit different.**

T: A big part of it is still that — having things you can't stand. There are many ways to express what you can't stand, but hip-hop's strong gene is: I feel something's off here, and I'll say it with humor, or say I'm uncomfortable — I think that's a really important point of this music. So this music has to be created by yourself — nobody can write your lyrics for you; you must speak your own ideas. Grumbling, looking cool, bragging — fine — but behind it all what we see is you; what I remember is you. If we must talk about consumption: I don't just consume your face, your body, I don't even necessarily consume your voice — what I consume is that you're an interesting person.

**Q: Does China have unique cultural traits it can bring to hip-hop?**

T: Honestly, not really. The old traditional culture has vanished almost entirely, and the new stuff hasn't been learned thoroughly; the Great Firewall blocks you, and countless people climb the wall just to read some news. Stop dreaming of grafting on "Chinese characteristics" from the start — sticking in a bit of Beijing opera, a bit of folk song, always that kind of thinking. You're both self-abasing and self-important: self-abasing because you can't keep up with the world, self-important because you always fancy you've got some unique Chinese dragon soul. Cut the crap. Immerse yourself in it and learn: whatever you like, go listen, go experience, experience yourself, look at more interesting things — that's it. Wanting to have a dialogue with the world? Way too early.

**Q: You also know the current state of thought and speech in China — no need to spell it out, everyone knows. It must affect hip-hop's development, right?**

T: Enormously.

**Q: Including the songs banned in that recent spell.**

T: If the clampdown goes on like this, this thing will become low-age bubblegum — an inescapable fate. But I believe no one can truly ban and strangle a culture. There will still be — rappers are the same: even if you grab them by the throat and cut out their tongues, there will be new people.

**Q: Hip-hop really isn't niche — it may be in China, but its commercial potential is already proven. LeEco focusing on it also wants to use commerce to push this music's development. So what concrete plans are there now? You mentioned reality shows, concerts, your own label — any more specific plans for the next step?**

T: Next step: probably one hip-hop reality show next year. And we'll keep going to places across China, continuing the documentary series. Because you know, in all these places, even though it hasn't entered the mainstream view — or only partly — there are tons of kids who have already created ample work, and a huge crowd lying low there. I hope to keep going to those places, discovering them, watching their wonderful music and interesting ways of life — rappers and producers alike, even crowds tied to extreme sports, like breakdancers. Another aspect: to see the different temperaments of hip-hop music in each city, the local labels representing the place, their whole way of life, their musical creation — and bring this fresh information back. I think all of this is important reference. A bit like after the country was forced open in 1840 — going to fetch the scriptures. I think it's the same; that process never stops. And it's not only about bringing good stuff back — it's also about taking interesting stuff out. It's an exchange.

Hip-hop is a unity. Though levels of development differ from place to place, hip-hop is like a code: you arrive, you find the other person is hip-hop, you're hip-hop too, and there's an innate closeness, a natural common language, because you're both inside the culture. Though each has their own life and their own unique things, that shared thing is an unspoken understanding. This year, traveling — Spain, the US, France, Switzerland — I met lots of local rappers, friends who make music, and found there was no barrier at all. Really interesting, this thing.

**Q: On Weibo, someone criticized the documentary, saying "this platform is really just for that group of people to promote themselves — it has nothing to do with hip-hop."**

T: That idea is interesting — I think that comment is very hip-hop. First, hip-hop really is, as I said about the scene: people huddle together, warm each other, play within the group — it's music between good buddies. Plus that person's statement is very hip-hop too, because hip-hop is naturally "me! I focus on me! I'm greater than everyone, cooler than everyone — why did you film them and not me?"

**Q: You said before that hip-hop is one trigger point away. What could that trigger be?**

T: I don't know either. I just have this hunch, because more and more people have started asking me about it. Before I started doing this, nobody paid attention to it. All these years of making music, I honestly never thought that one day I'd get rich and famous off music, or become a superstar — I never even thought about becoming an industry or a trend. I only ever felt that it pulled at me intensely on one hand, and on the other it was my best vehicle for expression. But now more and more people ask me industry questions, culture questions — can this music become a social phenomenon, whether by topping the charts or ultimately changing young people's lifestyles? All these questions and this attention make me wonder: why are so many people curious about this? If you're really all that curious — maybe the point it's missing isn't necessarily a commercial one, but a more understanding, more accurate, deeper attention.

**Q: How many hours do you work a day now?**

T: Not fixed — I'm pretty freestyle about it.
