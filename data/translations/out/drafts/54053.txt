"It's just a trim, yet its applications know no bounds."

The lace we broadly refer to today is a kind of openwork embroidery that emerged after the Renaissance. In the age of handcraft, people used tools such as bobbins and needles to make lace into narrow finished pieces, which were then sewn onto garments as decoration. Yet because of the intricate, time-consuming manual labor involved, lace at the time remained a "luxury" that only certain privileged classes — clergy, royalty, and nobility — could afford to wear.

In 1828, the first hand-operated embroidery machine was born in St. Gallen, eastern Switzerland. In 1869, after several rounds of improvement, the Swiss embroidery machine maker Saurer (founded by Franz Saurer) introduced a steam-powered embroidery machine, making mass production by machine possible. Only then was the market for lace craftsmanship truly opened up.

The story that follows is one that Mary Ma, founder of Fabric Library, wants to tell us.

From April 22 to June 22, Fabric Library mounted “*Lace to Meet You*” — the Chinese subtitle puns on the idiom 秀外慧中, replacing "beautiful" with "embroidered" — a China–Switzerland crossover exhibition dedicated to lace. Founded in 2016, this materials design and research center had, in less than two years, already staged a series of fabric-centric exhibitions such as “1-20 Project” and “20-1 Station.”

Still, singling out one material craft for its own show was a first.

As Mary sees it, most of the lace items we encounter today are French or Italian lace, and the impression they give is invariably delicate, luxurious, romantic, vintage, even feminine — but that is not the only form lace can take. In fact, in its earliest days, lace served as a symbol of religion, politics, and status, and had nothing to do with women.

"It was only after I visited the Textile Museum of St. Gallen in the winter of 2015 that my existing mental framework for lace was broken. That visit was unplanned — my itinerary was to study at the Jakob Schlaepfer AG fabric research and development center in St. Gallen. When they learned I run Fabric Library, they recommended I visit the textile museum. I arrived at eight that evening, long past closing time, but the director was still waiting for me. He then went floor by floor, switching on the lights to show me the collection. It was an overwhelming sight — especially with me being the only person in the entire museum."

Rooted in Switzerland's textile tradition, the Textile Museum of St. Gallen was founded in 1878 and now holds more than 40,000 objects, covering machine embroidery, hand embroidery, lace, textile patterns, printed materials, and other textile techniques. It is also Switzerland's most important textile collection system.

What impressed Mary most was a small library "hidden" inside the museum — they preserve classic textile samples from each era along a timeline, recording them in bound volumes. The "secret" to their pristine condition is that the library is never open to the public.

In this exhibition, you can see one such volume documenting postwar textile craftsmanship — the very one Mary brought back from the Textile Museum of St. Gallen.

What won Mary over about St. Gallen lace was its "simplicity." "This is a style St. Gallen lace developed from its handicraft era onward; they applied lace to garments made of inherently plain materials such as cotton, silk, and linen. After the machine was invented, St. Gallen only exported the technology. Meanwhile, in France, Italy, Belgium, and elsewhere at the time, lace moved toward an exquisite, luxurious style," Mary said in an exclusive interview with QDaily.

After that trip, Mary returned to the Textile Museum of St. Gallen several more times, persuading them to lend some pieces from their collection for exhibition back in China.

Beyond personal preference, this also fits Fabric Library's positioning: "We want to present all materials in a very humble, very primal state — we exist to serve fashion designers, fabric merchants, materials researchers, and people like that."

While the exhibition takes St. Gallen lace as its entry point, Mary stressed that she had no intention of copying the textile museum's curatorial approach — rigorously tracing lace's development along timelines or stylistic movements. "I want to do something fun that people will want to take part in." Unlike professional institutions such as museums and research institutes, Fabric Library places its emphasis on new experiments and ideas. In Mary's understanding, the two can offer audiences — and a city — things that complement each other.

More importantly, she hopes visitors will treat “*Lace to Meet You*” as a starting point. "If you're a creator, I hope you can be inspired by it, rather than simply marveling at how exquisite it is — that's just the surface." She chose to read lace through the lens of a creator.

In the "Lace Forest" section, Fabric Library built an immersive "meditation" space for the exhibition. Using projection, installations, and other means, they combined lace with light, shadow, and music to conjure a fantastical forest scene — pointing to lace's very origins: flowers, plants, forests, dappled light and shadow... They are the sources of inspiration with which all lace creation begins.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607174413HYG2d8hTW1f7Ev9s.jpg-w600)

In the "Diverse Inspirations" section, you can see paintings, illustrations, and even film costumes from the early 20th century deeply influenced by the "Oriental" vogue.

Among them, for instance, is *Plum Park in Kameido*, painted by the Dutch artist Vincent van Gogh in 1887, which was itself influenced by the work of the famed ukiyo-e artist Utagawa Hiroshige; there are also illustrations by the Danish artist Kay Nielsen for the tales of *One Thousand and One Nights*, rendered in a richly Oriental style. "The trend of that era was to study exotic, diverse cultures. This section wants to show people how designers of that time thought and innovated. Why did they create works with that particular spirit? You have to look at them against their historical backdrop. I believe it can provoke people today: what you should do is not copy, but reactivate this craft and create designs with a contemporary sensibility."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607223440qKv30Pk4Id1WnYps.jpg-w600)

Given lace's religious connotations, "White Faith" creates a pure, sacred space. On display are vestments worn by clergy during rituals and prayer cards, on which you can find delicate, elegant lace patterns.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607174442ozYkI4uSC1bWtrge.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607174454Md4gUx3Sj1kX0Hil.jpg-w600)

Corresponding to it is the more flamboyant "Postwar Color" section. After World War II, as the economy recovered and living standards rose, the development of lace products and creative vitality were further unleashed. In 1947, Dior unveiled the New Look in Paris, laying the foundation for postwar fashion. By the 1960s, with the rise of youth culture, lace fabrics and styles took on a younger, more colorful character. For example, you can see printed matte sequined lace with a stronger personal style, and colored sequined mesh. At the same time, lace was no longer confined to traditional textiles such as clothing; it came to be applied far more widely, to hats, shoes, furniture, and other products.

One could say this has already broken through our "narrow" understanding of lace. "By this point, the concept of lace had been extended. A piece of openwork fabric, with its connected loops and interplay of solid and void — that already counts as lace," Mary explained to us.

Though she didn't follow traditional curatorial methods, she was clear about what she wanted to convey to visitors: "The pieces brought from the St. Gallen museum represent lace's past. Lace's present and future are what I want to focus on more."

"The flourishing of St. Gallen's lace craft owed everything to the development of machines and technology." This fundamental understanding of Mary's shaped the curatorial language of *Lace to Meet You*. "These days everyone talks about how beautiful handwork is, how wonderful nostalgia is, how natural fabrics surpass synthetics. Actually that's all fantasy. You need to look at everything on an equal footing. Handicraft laid the entire foundation of lace craftsmanship, but in that era, demand for it amounted to no more than a sensibility for life — it couldn't spread. With the advance of technology and the expansion of market demand, the emergence of new techniques was only natural. What you need to do is create materials with people as your starting point."

In the era of the first-generation dual-head embroidery machines, the machines were still at the stage of imitating the movements of human hands. In the "Mechanical Beauty" section you can see a small horse handkerchief and, on the reverse side of another, a swan — it is hard to tell them apart from hand embroidery.

Then machines began to transcend the limits of the body and developed a new logic — besides working faster, they could produce all kinds of patterns that were nearly impossible by hand. In the first-generation machines, embroidery still had to be worked on a ground fabric. With the second-generation shuttle embroidery machine, mesh-structured lace began to appear. In 1883, St. Gallen combined embroidery with chemical solvents to invent "etched lace" — which is the form of lace we are most familiar with today. The third-generation punched-card Jacquard embroidery machine was built precisely to make this kind of etched lace trim. Without relying on any ground fabric, it could form a strong bonded structure.

Today, St. Gallen lace keeps moving toward high technology. In the "Rebirth of Lace" section are 3D-printed lace collars Mary brought from Switzerland — designer Martin Leuthold developed an entirely new lace fabric using nothing but "silicone" as the raw material.

From here, even the concept of "material" itself was broadened.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607174837b0eMEFvBN2ZGQJuk.jpg-w600)

In the "Lace Forest" section, Mary and her team made an interactive art installation. They placed salt and alum on the surface of the lace, forming solid crystals. When visitors gently pat the installation's lamp, they can see lace ground patterns of different designs.

"Lace patterns are actually very similar to crystals. I wanted to use this kind of experiential approach to connect lace with the general public, and to further break the boundaries of lace's forms of expression," Mary explained to us when asked about the source of inspiration.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607174908hQm83FU4TCMpfVsS.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201806071749196PlaeBE1XR042SF7.jpg-w600)

Similar interactions run throughout the exhibition. In the "Hands-On Experience" area, you can pick one piece from lace items cascading down like a waterfall and take it apart, feeling the weave design of bobbin lace; in the "Stamp Collection" section, you can pick up a magnifying glass and examine the fine textures of lace patterns.

This approach has indeed worked well. "Through the exhibition, we've met all kinds of people. What moved me especially was an 81-year-old who dressed very 'lace' to come see the show. And many people even wanted to donate their own collections to me for display. That was something I hadn't expected," Mary told us.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607180111DpdylO56QGRLCIjz.jpg-w600)

What form will lace take in the future? That is the theme the "Future of Lace" section wants to explore. Fabric Library provided the collaborating designers with a series of new-style lace fabrics, helping them complete garments and accessories in widely varying styles. The secret within needs a close look to be discovered.

For instance, designer CHEN PENG used lace-coated fabric and nylon fabric to make three exaggeratedly shaped down jackets; you can also see unexpected designs such as lace umbrellas and lace rain ponchos. "Many people think lace is openwork — how could it possibly keep out rain, wind, and cold? So we'll show you: lace has already reached that level of technology."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607174947awNfC6MLg3QKtkDh.jpg-w600)

After 21 years of working with fabrics, Mary feels that "many people see textiles as nothing more than a supply." In fact, the scope of textiles is enormous — it's just that no one has ever mined their charm. The original intention behind founding Fabric Library was to establish a direct dialogue with independent designers and with the people who genuinely hold decision-making power at fashion brands, and to express materials in ways that are a little different.

The main problem to tackle here is this: how to establish a virtuous cycle among materials researchers, brands, and consumers.

"What a brand needs to consider is: after I buy your fabric, can I actually sell the garment? Fabric Library has no salespeople whatsoever. I'll simply answer your doubts with an exhibition. *Lace to Meet You* ultimately grew into one big 'community' — we once had a Kerry Center sub-venue offline, and we also launched an online Lace Festival in sync on Tmall. Meanwhile, the main venue's exhibition also featured a coordinated product release. This in fact formed a lace 'cultural wave,' and consumers were willing to buy lots of lace products during the event. In the end, the ones who really benefited were the merchants."

Now, the Kerry Center has also entered a strategic partnership with Fabric Library. Fabric Library will help activate the property's foot traffic by introducing brands that all of Hangzhou lacks and by staging interactive, experiential exhibitions; in turn, the Kerry Center will help Fabric Library incubate more content in the future.

As for each future exhibition, Mary says she doesn't want to set any frame around themes or formats: "Next we also plan to do exhibitions on 'materials and photography' and 'materials and psychology.' I just want to use fabric to help everyone make all kinds of things."
