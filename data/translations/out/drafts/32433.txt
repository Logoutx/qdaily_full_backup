Some of the selected restaurants don't see it as a big deal, while civilian foodies seem less than satisfied

German chef Stefan Stiller has already opened a bottle of champagne, ready to celebrate his newly opened Shanghai restaurant "Taian Table" winning one Michelin star. This is the second time Michelin has favored him; a restaurant he ran in Germany had also earned a Michelin star.

"For our team, this is an achievement," Stefan Stiller told *QDaily* in a phone interview, saying they plan to hold a dinner to celebrate.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921114014fFv6TTxnNBEQD0Pm.jpeg-w600)

Taian Table among them, the *Michelin Guide Shanghai 2017*, released this morning, lists 122 restaurants: 1 three-star, 7 two-star, and 18 one-star. This time, Michelin also released a mobile app for iOS and Android.

Normally, these restaurants would be swamped with customers thanks to a recommendation from this world-famous dining guide. Stefan Stiller's wife's East Eatery in Shanghai also made the list, and they hope business will pick up as a result — previously, hampered by a poor location, the restaurant had not been doing so well.

Because of Michelin's broad fame, the guide can steer more customers toward these little-known restaurants. This honor, arguably the highest accolade in the chef world, belongs to the Michelin tire company; it was originally created to promote automobile travel, but has now grown into a content provider headquartered in the Paris suburbs, employing several hundred people and publishing more than 20 million books a year. Three stars is the highest rating in Michelin's food evaluation system.

Michelin only announced its entry into Shanghai this past May; before that, it had already published 29 food guides worldwide. Compared with its long investigation cycles abroad, the mere four months of preparation that followed seemed extremely tight — during this period, nearly 100 professional inspectors, mostly Chinese, conducted anonymous visits, tastings, and scoring. Most industry insiders and chefs had long since nervously gotten wind of the news, hoping they could stand out on the five criteria: quality of ingredients, mastery of technique, personality of the chef, value for money, and consistency of cooking.

While some consumers here already trust this "honor" completely — they are used to following wherever the Michelin guide points to find food — Michelin clearly also hopes to secure a foothold in the Asian dining market as quickly as possible. When the local cuisine here meets this evaluation system born in Paris, awkward moments may ensue.

Cities with an international background have naturally become Michelin's first choices. Tokyo, Hong Kong, and Macau were the first to have Michelin restaurants; this year, besides Shanghai, Michelin simultaneously released guides for Seoul, South Korea, and Singapore — seen as a signal of Michelin's big push into the Asian market.

Shanghai is Michelin's fifth city in Asia, and the *Michelin Guide Shanghai 2017* is the 29th guide it has published globally. In 1999, M on the Bund opened on Guangdong Road in Shanghai, making its first contact with Western food; after the 2008 Beijing Olympics, Shanghai's Western dining culture accelerated, and more and more chefs with Michelin stars to their names came to this market — for instance, this past March the famous French chef Joël Robuchon just opened a new restaurant at Bund 18, which, as expected, won two stars in the *Michelin Guide Shanghai 2017* released this morning.

That said, until the press conference on the 21st ended, the entire list of Michelin restaurants had been kept strictly under wraps, and none of the media attending could get the complete list. But once the list was officially released, T'ang Court — the sole, much-rumored three-star Michelin restaurant — stirred up considerable controversy. Jocelyn, founder of TastyTrip, a global Michelin dining group, told us she had just eaten there last week and felt the food was not even up to the level of some one-star restaurants. Jocelyn has eaten at hundreds of Michelin restaurants around the world.

"This time it's still on the conservative side — 26 starred restaurants, less than half of Hong Kong's. It's actually a bit of a pity," she said.

![From left to right: Michael Ellis, Michelin's international director; the head chef of three-star restaurant T'ang Court; and Claire Dorland-Clauzel, Michelin's director of brands and external relations and executive vice president of the group](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921164312pCQeky8GirwzZb0q.jpg-w600)

### Michelin's political correctness: Cantonese cuisine and time-honored restaurants

Of the 26 starred restaurants, 19 are Chinese restaurants, and of those, 9 serve Cantonese cuisine. Add to that T'ang Court and 8 ½ Otto E Mezzo Bombana — restaurants that had already earned Michelin stars in Hong Kong — and it all makes one feel like the whole of Hong Kong was simply transplanted here.

With the European market in decline, Michelin, like many chefs who have left Europe, is looking for new opportunities. In markets where Michelin has been cultivating for a long time, unless one is a devotee who puts food above all else, many people "buy one copy and are not likely to buy a new one the next year."

But for every newly entered market, Michelin has been particularly conservative and localized — much like the situation with Hong Kong back then. At the end of 2008, the *Michelin Guide Hong Kong Macau 2009* was published, at roughly 300-plus pages, with 28 restaurants winning stars at the time, but most of them hotel restaurants; famous Hong Kong Cantonese establishments such as Fook Lam Moon and Yung Kee received only one star, drawing questions from much of the Hong Kong media and the dining world. A year later, Michelin began substantially adding relatively affordable restaurants, bringing the number of starred restaurants to 42. Hung's Delicacies in North Point and the Tim Ho Wan dim sum shop in Mong Kok were the most direct beneficiaries, becoming the cheapest one-star Michelin restaurants in the world.

German chef Stefan Stiller, whose Taian Table won one Michelin star in Shanghai this time, had previously commented: "Maybe it's more of a political decision. Because they need to sell books — what would be the point if there were no starred restaurants?"

Another obvious feature is that many Shanghai time-honored restaurants received Michelin's Bib Gourmand recommendations, including Da Hu Chun, Lao Zheng Xing, Godly, the Nanxiang Steamed Bun Shop... These legacy restaurants represent dining at under 200 yuan per person, or even less. This cluster of listed restaurants may well reflect lessons Michelin learned in Hong Kong.

But the time-honored restaurants themselves are hardly aware of Michelin's coronation. When we visited the Da Hu Chun flagship on the Bund at noon, both store manager Ms. Hu and outlet manager Huang Huichao said they knew little about Michelin — which did nothing to keep business from being remarkably good at midday.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921165738bqU4TG27lyPKwuRo.jpeg-w600)

Operators of these time-honored restaurants believe Michelin may not make restaurants that already carry historical credentials any more trustworthy. Huang Huichao said Da Hu Chun never lacks media visits — simply opening new, larger outlets lifted revenue from the previous 400,000 yuan to 1 million yuan, and now his headache is that yet another new outlet is about to open.

The Da Hu Chun snack shop was founded in 1932 on Sichuan Middle Road. Since Da Hu Chun went corporate last October, it has opened four new outlets in a single year and moved its old shop from No. 243 to the current address. The new shop is closer to the Bund and can attract white-collar crowds. The white-collar crowd indeed has been attracted: "Old customers now know to avoid the white-collar lunch rush and come a bit later at noon," said manager Hu.

As a newcomer, Michelin may be the one that needs these time-honored restaurants more. It looks like a choice that can't go wrong — when they make the list, this food recommendation guide from Paris seems to understand the local food culture better. But in fact, young people may patronize these shops less than expected.

### Shanghai's Michelin hotspots

We marked all the Michelin-starred restaurants on Google Maps. You can see that in Shanghai, the Bund and Jing'an Temple are the densest clusters of Michelin restaurants — basically also the two areas with the highest dining spending in Shanghai.

![An overview of Michelin restaurants in Shanghai](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921161913AsQoTZ302kHfW1ca.jpeg-w600)

The Bund is home to five of the seven two-star restaurants, and essentially all the non-Chinese restaurants, including those serving innovative cuisine, are located there. Chinese restaurants are mainly concentrated in three areas: the Jing An Kerry Centre & West Nanjing Road area, the IFC in Pudong, and the Huaihai Road & Xintiandi area. The first two are dominated by Jiangsu-Zhejiang cuisine, while the last has both Chinese and Western restaurants.

![The Bund](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609211635079Wd0LQqpRf5xvIGt.png-w600)

![Jing'an Temple](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921163508yOCL9g6izbDF1Gsx.png-w600)

These Michelin-starred restaurants vary widely in price: the lowest per-person spend is 100 yuan, while the highest reaches 5,571 yuan; overall, though, they mostly fall in the 300–700 yuan range.

### Highlights of the listed restaurants

**The cheapest Michelin two-star**

More than half of the two-star restaurants are Chinese, and as you know, Cantonese cuisine dominates. Canton 8, located on Runan Street in Shanghai, averages 127 yuan per person, making it arguably the cheapest Michelin two-star restaurant in the world: "At Canton 8, diners can enjoy two-star dishes for just 48 yuan, or a lunch for just over 6 euros."

![Dishes at Canton 8](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921180128qFDUO7il15H2mKNM.jpg-w600)

**The only barbecue restaurant**

The Taiwanese yakiniku shop Kanpai (Lao Gan Bei), located at Bund 5, has been wildly popular since opening last summer. Its Shanghai branch made the Michelin one-star list after being open just one year, making it the only barbecue restaurant to hold a Michelin star besides "Zen," a wagyu specialist in Tokyo.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160921163324WQ4eSzbd9jI5awmN.jpg-w600)

**The one that got away: Jean-Georges**

Jean-Georges at Three on the Bund had long been a favorite to win a Michelin star — this offshoot of a New York Michelin three-star restaurant settled in Shanghai 12 years ago. But this time it did not even get one star, which many diners and fellow chefs find unbelievable.

![The Jean-Georges restaurant](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609211816039qbgJ2RlspIykoxz.jpg-w600)

### "Dianping, what do you think?"

For ordinary diners, competitors aiming to offer "dining recommendations" like Michelin are becoming more and more numerous.

One example is the Best 50 Restaurants list (the World's 50 Best Restaurants), founded in 2002 by the British magazine *Restaurant*, regarded as Michelin's number-one rival. Its judging panel consists of more than 1,000 international dining-world figures, including restaurant critics, gourmets, food columnists, head chefs, and restaurant owners, and the annual awards ceremony is always a grand affair.

Beyond the "Oscars of the food world," Best 50, challengers to the Michelin guide also include La Liste, backed by the French tourism board, and the "Dianping" of countries around the world — the US has Zagat, Japan has Tabelog, and TripAdvisor and Yelp work globally. Some of them have more distinctive features or venture into markets Michelin has not entered; some are rooted in the internet, updating daily. Moreover, these guides are considered to understand local food markets better than Michelin.

Of course, in Shanghai Michelin's main competitor is Dianping itself. As for how the starred restaurants come across on the review site, we picked out some rather interesting reviews:
