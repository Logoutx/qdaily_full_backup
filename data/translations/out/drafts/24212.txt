Oki Sato and his Nendo are a perfect illustration of design as an indispensable part of business.

## Who he is: Oki Sato (born 1977), a Japanese designer who founded the Nendo design studio in Tokyo in 2002. From furniture and interiors to products, clothing and accessories, Sato and his team typically juggle more than 250 projects at a time, delivering around 50 finished works a year. Newsweek once named him one of "the 100 most respected Japanese people in the world."

## His curiosity: "Giving people a small '!' moment."

If it hadn't been for the 2002 Milan Furniture Fair, Oki Sato might well have stuck to the path of architecture.

Born in Toronto, Sato returned to settle in Japan at age ten. After graduating from the architecture department at Waseda University, he went on to pursue further studies in architecture. But a graduation trip to Milan made him "switch careers" before he had even officially entered the profession.

In Milan in April, he felt the tremendous impact of this international creative event. "For the first time we learned that so many architects also design furniture and products, that every corner of life is made better by design — it was as if we could see the future of design."

Holding fast to the belief that "my work must be shown here one day," Sato returned home and founded the Nendo design studio, and within just three years had opened a branch in Milan, the capital of modern design. He also fulfilled the wish of that first Milan trip: at Milan Design Week 2015, he staged a retrospective of more than 100 products created over the previous year for 19 brands.

Today Nendo is a fixture at international design shows and awards juries, having collected numerous honors: Germany's Red Dot Design Award, ***Wallpaper**** and ***ELLE DECO*** magazine's Designer of the Year awards, the Maison & Objet Paris Designer of the Year award, and countless design prizes in Japan. Sato himself was named one of Newsweek's "100 most respected Japanese people in the world," and his representative works are held in the collections of the Museum of Modern Art in New York, the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Centre Pompidou, and other world-famous museums.

But in Japan, with its mature design industry and dense concentration of design talent, is Sato's emergence an anomaly?

Every era in Japan has produced phenomenal industrial designers — from the older generation's Sori Yanagi and Kenji Ekuan to Naoto Fukasawa and Kenya Hara, who enjoyed considerable acclaim in China in recent years. Nearly all of them held fast to their own design philosophies. Yanagi, for instance, advocated "feeling with your own hands the fit between an object and the person, stripping away superfluous ornament to create simple utensils of the greatest practical beauty." Hara championed "Re-Design," while Fukasawa championed "Without Thought."

Each has his strengths, but they also share common ground: even when embracing Westernization, they remain faithful to their own traditions. Their work is simple and clean, which many take as an expression of the Zen sensibility of Japanese design, though in substance it reads more as an emphasis on function rather than playfulness.

Japan produces design talent in abundance, and many continue this legacy of either Zen sensibility or clean functional expression. But Sato's emergence has slightly loosened our understanding of Japanese design. Perhaps because he grew up in Canada, his design thinking is not "very Japanese": he does not over-pursue Japanese rigor or Zen, but instead emphasizes a playfulness wrapped around functionality. He also treats design as a business activity, placing marketing at an equal — if not higher — level.

"Good products will always find buyers" does not hold true for Sato. How could you not actively show customers the value of a product?

Take the "HALSUIT" suit specialty store that Nendo designed in 2011 for Japan's Haruyama Shoji. Sato moved the fitting rooms — usually tucked in a corner — to the very center of the store, provided DVD players and magazines for accompanying guests, and designed the shop to feel like a suit-required office environment. The entrance of every "HALSUIT" resembles a corporate front desk; the cabinets are filled with neatly hung shirts; and to evoke a conference-room feel, even the model and color of the chairs are uniform.

Haruyama Shoji's president, Masafumi Haruyama, remembers what Sato said when they first met: "Design means getting customers to buy a product in your own way. If you can't achieve that, no matter how good the work, it's wasted." After the "HALSUIT" Higashi-Okayama store opened in the autumn of 2011, suit sales rose 10% over the previous year, prices went up 40%, and the time customers spent in the store increased by 40%.

Sato never thinks of himself as an artist. He doesn't obsess over minute differences in a product's details, and he knows that what clients need is not your flights of fancy. Designing is like designing a tree: he doesn't start from the perspective of the leaves; he focuses on the trunk and the roots. Applied to brand design, he believes a well-designed "trunk" and "roots" are the foundation for establishing brand equity.

When designing stores for the Spanish shoe brand Camper, he knew that Camper shoes neither help people run faster nor confer any status on the wearer, so he focused on letting people discover the joy of walking — conveying the idea through images of shoes strolling freely through the store's space. In this case, the shoes floating and wandering through the air were not a beauty or whimsy Sato deliberately manufactured; they were the "magic that makes you fall in love with walking," imagined from the comfortable character of Camper shoes.

This is why Nendo has become an international design studio. Selling products well matters, of course, but more important than immediate profit is raising the brand value of the company and the product itself.

They have no shortage of big-name clients: Coca-Cola, Hermès, Nissin, Lotte, Puma, Issey Miyake, Tod's, Shu Uemura, Toshiba, Häagen-Dazs, Camper, Starbucks, Isetan, Elecom, Swarovski, NEC, and more.

But marketing tricks alone, without a foothold in design, won't work either.

In Sato's mind, design and marketing are inseparable: "If one studio turns 100-point ideas into 40-point designs, while another consistently turns 70-point ideas into 100-point designs, it's obvious which one clients will choose." This is Sato's understanding of creativity.

Of course, this is not only a matter of creativity itself; it also involves Sato's management of the studio — how to create the environment and conditions in which creativity can be realized.

First, naturally, comes understanding the client's expectations. This can't be solved by simple Q&A; it may require you to grasp the company's history, its present situation and future direction, its short-term goals, product positioning, competitors, and past successes and failures. Sato calls this process the "project warm-up," and he even considers it more important than submitting proposals.

If, after communicating with the client, he still can't pin down what they're hoping for, he will turn the project down.

And much of the creativity is discovered during the "warm-up." Racking your brains for a single 100-point idea is inevitably hard, but what Sato pursues is "finding a large quantity of 70-point ideas. If you can shape ideas at three times the usual speed, you can present three times as many proposals within the same schedule, leaving the remaining two-thirds of the time to refine and perfect the scheme. The benefit is that even if the first proposal fails, you still have ample time to make up for it — and that wins the client's trust more."

Sato admits that no other studio currently places such importance on a "sense of speed."

Last April, we reported [the news that Nendo was running nearly 400 projects at once](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/9020.html). Sato compares himself to a fish that might die if it doesn't keep swimming. "Sometimes I feel I can't keep up with my own pace. I'm thinking about so many things; I have too many ideas I want to get out — they are like breathing or eating."

Won't inspiration ever run dry? Sato's answer: "Stay curious about everything in the world forever." It sounds a bit like chicken soup for the soul, but the fact that inspiration knocks at any moment stems from his extraordinary power of concentration. He never deliberately does anything to stimulate inspiration, and the benefit is that he cherishes more what he discovers in daily life and work.

Take, for example, a staircase no one is climbing, a lamp in the daytime or while someone sleeps, a television no one is watching — what meaning do they have? This is a blind spot in most people's thinking. Sato wants to capture the "rest time" of these everyday things. In the interior design project for the ILLOIHA OMOTESANDO fitness club, he moved a climbing wall from the outdoors indoors to accentuate the sense of spatial design, bringing visual beauty even when no one is climbing it.

"I can't do anything except think about design projects." Even when traveling abroad on business, he keeps his range of activity limited to his lodging, restaurants, and meeting venues. You might think someone who says this is a boring person — but then how do you explain the knowing smiles his works elicit? Don't forget that "giving people a small '!' moment" is the studio's design creed.

He has organized his design thinking into ten distinct approaches, including creating a sense of "wrongness," breaking "balance," and designing by "stepping back" — all solutions he uses to create that "small surprise."

Sato's oft-quoted philosophy, "the more ordinary, the more profound," is the core of the Nendo way of thinking.

It is somewhat similar to Kenya Hara's proposal to "look at familiar things as if seeing them for the first time." But Hara values the process of re-designing ordinary objects with creativity and technique, whereas what Sato has always believed is that "what is truly rich and profound is always the 'extraordinary' scattered through everyday life" — such as what kind of table a cup suits, what style of convenience it calls for, who uses it, and what the surrounding environment is like — unfolding your field of vision bit by bit like this.

Although the market offers all kinds of stands for smartphones and tablets, nothing is as comfortable as a cushion. For the patchwork-fabric version of the cushion, Nendo left openings along the outer seams, making it easy for people to browse their screens while relaxing on a sofa or in bed.

You see, Nendo's designs all start from the everyday things at hand, but they always think a little more than you do.

Take "mash-ups." The combination of chocolate and pencils is a wonderful example: chocolates of varying cocoa concentrations shaped like pencils, complete with a dedicated pencil sharpener. The shavings are no longer discarded trash but the finishing touch on a food — that is the delight of the design.

![Chocolate pencils](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703150551sKJhOtDoLRSpqwf7.jpg-w600)

Then there's the interplay between curiosity and the "art of hiding." Using the curiosity everyone has, you arouse people's interest by concealing parts of a thing. When designing rulers for its own brand by | n, the white parts of the markings disappear on a light background, while the black parts disappear on a dark background. The [Border Table](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/16078.html) we once featured can also serve as evidence of the "art of hiding."

In QDaily's design section we have covered many of Nendo's works and exhibitions. When trying to sum up the stylized elements in his design, it's easy to see that Nendo's work softens rigorous, well-disciplined minimalism with a childlike humor, making it look more like a product of Japanese pop culture.

Browsing Nendo's body of work, you'll find all kinds of little amusements that combine function and aesthetics: the Stay-brella umbrella that stands on its own without any support; Bubbles, a home fragrance that imagines the scent filling a room as soap bubbles; a nine-grid bookshelf that stretches; and slippers that store as handily as a wizard's hat.

Japanese industrial design art often expresses its content through a traditional Eastern mode of thinking and sensibility, but Sato's Nendo is a success shaped jointly by Western and Japanese design thinking. On one hand, it treats design as a business activity, quickly and accurately meeting clients' needs. On the other, it does not lose the characteristically Japanese obsession with perfect detail.

This philosophy has reference value for many designers. Even if you don't design, when we encounter in daily life the problem of how to express what's inside us, Sato's perspective is worth borrowing.
