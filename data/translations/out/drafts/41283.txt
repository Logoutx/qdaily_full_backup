@@QD_BODY@@
It reversed the DC franchise's slide in critical reception — and it may bring in bigger box office, too.

"*Wonder Woman* is the best film in the DC Extended Universe so far, and it has the humor this franchise desperately needs."

"I love this movie. It reminded me of the Reeve *Superman* — a real superhero movie, with no anger or cynicism in it. It's exactly what we need right now."

On May 19, first social-media reactions for the upcoming *Wonder Woman* were released. [Twitter was soon flooded with such one-sided praise.](http://io9.gizmodo.com/the-first-reactions-for-wonder-woman-are-spectacular-1795356828) The DC Extended Universe finally had a critically well-received film. The franchise's three previously released films — *Man of Steel*, *Batman v Superman: Dawn of Justice*, and *Suicide Squad* — had all scored low "fresh" ratings on Rotten Tomatoes, with the latter two both under 30%.

The DC Extended Universe is a film project Warner Bros. launched in 2013. Its foundation is the comics company DC Comics, which Warner acquired in 1969, along with the superheroes it owns, such as Superman and Batman.

Clearly, when Warner launched the DC Extended Universe, it was aiming at the Marvel Cinematic Universe. At the time, Marvel was at its peak. From *Iron Man* to *Iron Man 3*, the Marvel Cinematic Universe had released 7 films with a combined global box office of over $5 billion. If Iron Man could do it, why couldn't Superman?

Warner adopted the "universe" concept. It refers to a series of films sharing the same worldview, which makes it possible for different characters and stories from different films to interact. *Wonder Woman*, for instance, tells the origin story of this superhero, yet a year earlier she had already fought alongside Batman in *Batman v Superman*. Put simply, a film universe is like a big-budget TV serial released in movie theaters.

To build the DC Extended Universe, Warner invested enormously. Counting marketing costs, *Batman v Superman* cost a total of $410 million, making it one of the most expensive films ever made. In addition, the DC Extended Universe has announced 20 films to date, each a standard Hollywood blockbuster with a budget over $100 million.

By box office, the DC Extended Universe can't be counted a failure. The three released films have grossed a combined $2.286 billion worldwide. On average, each one made the global top ten for its year.

But it can't be counted a success either — *Batman v Superman* least of all. The film holds just a 28% "fresh" rating on Rotten Tomatoes, and headlines like "Dawn of Disappointment," "Even Wonder Woman Can't Save This Mess," and "Why Is It So Bad?" filled the pages of media outlets.

The poor reception also dragged down box office. With a $410 million investment and the two most famous superheroes on the planet — Superman and Batman — its eventual $873 million global gross could only be called mediocre. By comparison, Marvel's *Captain America: Civil War*, released around the same time, grossed over $1.1 billion worldwide.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705261026013Uv2YC8NBFR6uKt4.jpg-w600)

![These two 2016 films about superheroes at war with each other were seen as a head-on collision between Marvel and DC](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170526102054ipghmVPdf8tOKz7b.jpg-w600)

Part of the blame was pinned on Warner's impatience. *Batman v Superman* was formally greenlit just two months after *Man of Steel* hit theaters. A year later, Warner announced ten film projects in one go, booking all the most important characters and organizations — Wonder Woman, The Flash, the Justice League, and more — at a pace of roughly two films a year.

And they had no intention of following Marvel's approach — paving the way with individual hero films and only capping a phase with a team-up extravaganza. The almost-fully-assembled *Justice League* came just one year after *Batman v Superman*; before that, DC's most important superhero, Batman, didn't even have a solo film; and most of the solo films would only come out later.

The moment this plan was announced, it drew strong backlash. Most in the media believed Warner, seeing Marvel's rapid rise, was eager to copy the success of *The Avengers*. In their view, Marvel's model was the sounder one — the necessary groundwork for a climactic hero team-up. By comparison, Warner's approach seemed impatient and shortsighted.

But at that moment, Warner truly couldn't afford to wait. In 2013, Christopher Nolan's Batman trilogy and the Harry Potter series had all concluded. Warner lacked a franchise that could generate steady revenue and had lost the title of Hollywood's most profitable studio, which it had held for years. It needed the DC Extended Universe to fill that gap.

In addition, the DC Extended Universe seemed to get the tone wrong. Zack Snyder, director of *Batman v Superman* and the franchise's longtime producer, inherited Nolan's style. Despite its superheroes, the DC Extended Universe was set on a realist course — dark and brooding.

Nolan's Batman trilogy was indeed successful enough, grossing over $2.4 billion worldwide, but Nolan is ultimately inimitable. Sticking to that path would likely yield far less of a positive effect than Nolan did.

In the end, Warner found that worry becoming reality. *Man of Steel*'s focus on immigrant issues and Superman's anxious state of mind won no praise, and a controversial ending in which Superman kills his enemy put it in the eye of the storm. In *Batman v Superman*, the setup of Batman as a villain likewise displeased many viewers. For most audiences, going to the movies is an escape from reality in the first place. They want brighter, funnier superhero films.

Precisely because *Batman v Superman* and *Suicide Squad* were received so poorly, the advance praise from audiences who had seen *Wonder Woman* this time seemed especially precious. Many therefore believed the DC Extended Universe had taken a great leap forward.

In fact, since the release of *Batman v Superman*, changes had taken place inside Warner that, to some extent, altered how the DC Extended Universe operates. QDaily interviewed three producers of the DC Extended Universe — Zack Snyder, Deborah Snyder, and Charles Roven. They explained how the changes happened, how the current mechanism works, and why *Wonder Woman* is different.

### 1. First, the new chief creative officer really knows DC

The failure of *Batman v Superman* was the trigger [for a Warner management shake-up that May](http://www.hollywoodreporter.com/heat-vision/batman-v-superman-fallout-warner-895174). Geoff Johns, chief creative officer of DC Entertainment, was elevated to oversee the DC Extended Universe; he would work with another Warner vice president, Jon Berg, to supervise future films and the development of a separate television universe.

![Geoff Johns](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170526102718pNYlLSw2zTIWRPtJ.jpg-w600)

Over the past 20 years, Johns gradually grew from a comics fan into a writer favored by DC; his best-known comics are *Green Lantern Corps* and *Crisis on Infinite Earths*. In 2010, DC Comics was reorganized into DC Entertainment, and Johns was named chief creative officer, charged with extending DC Comics into television, film, games, and other fields.

The success of the standalone television universe and several Batman games, along with his influence in comics, made Johns the ideal person to supervise the film projects: he had cross-media success under his belt, knew the source material well enough, and understood what comics fans like.

Interestingly, [Geoff Johns's vision for DC Comics was one of hope and optimism](http://www.vulture.com/2016/05/geoff-johns-dc-films.html) — the exact opposite of the heaviness and darkness for which *Batman v Superman* was criticized. The general expectation was that Johns's involvement would effectively counterbalance the director's overly strong personal style and bring the DC Extended Universe a touch of the brightness audiences wanted.

In fact, Johns's role would go beyond being an executive producer in the administrative sense; he would become a more hands-on producer, even working on story development and screenwriting. His presence is felt strongly in several future DC projects. He will serve as screenwriter on *The Batman*, story developer on *Aquaman* and *Green Lantern Corps*, and producer on *Justice League*, *Gotham City Sirens*, *Justice League Dark*, and more.

The appointment of this seasoned comics veteran also gave the outside world hope that the DC Extended Universe might turn things around.

### 2. Second, directors still wield considerable power

The overarching framework of the DC Extended Universe is worked out in discussions between Zack Snyder and Geoff Johns, who set the direction the story will take next and sometimes offer writers story ideas drawn from the rich comics source material. For the directors and writers in charge of individual films, they need to know where their film sits within the series and on the timeline, but they also have plenty of room to maneuver, because Warner places great value on their "unique point of view."

"We don't want these films to look like they all came from the same committee, off the same assembly line," producer Deborah Snyder told QDaily. "Having directors like Zack, Patty, and James Wan means you get to see different characters' stories through their eyes."

In fact, it was these creators' ideas that gradually expanded the series from *Man of Steel* into a universe. Zack Snyder said the idea for *Batman v Superman* came to him and screenwriter David S. Goyer while they were working on *Man of Steel*; and the concept for *Justice League* came from *Argo* screenwriter Chris Terrio, who was then writing the *Batman v Superman* script.

These creators' ideas allow the universe to keep evolving, says Charles Roven, Warner's longtime producer and one of the DC Extended Universe's leads — that, in his view, is one of the advantages of having many directors. The producers have a rough blueprint in mind, but the moment a new director or writer comes aboard, it shifts and evolves.

"It's like you have a destination in mind, but there's more than one road that leads there. That's what happens when you bring in all kinds of creative talent — and it's also the different-points-of-view issue we just talked about."

Zack, a film director by trade, has played mostly a producer's role within the DC Extended Universe, but he is firm about delegating power to directors. "That's something we've always tried to do," he told QDaily. "Warner is a filmmaker-driven company — that's their creed. Give directors enough leeway so they don't get backed into a corner."

![Man of Steel presented a brooding, conflicted Superman, sparking widespread controversy](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170526103449CQw7AVOZ9SfqnM1y.jpg-w600)

As a director who often inserts controversial sequences into his films, he has benefited from that creed himself. The widely criticized scene in *Man of Steel* in which Superman kills General Zod (viewers accustomed to the Reeve Superman felt that an embodiment of justice shouldn't kill) was his idea, and producer Nolan, once persuaded, gave it the green light.

This is completely different from the approach of DC's biggest rival, Marvel. Under Marvel's system, a creative committee does the overall planning for the entire film series, so the creative freedom it grants directors is very limited.

Edgar Wright left *Ant-Man* over creative differences; Patty Jenkins exited *Thor: The Dark World* when no agreement could be reached on the script; [the best-known example is Joss Whedon leaving the MCU after *Avengers: Age of Ultron*](https://moviepilot.com/p/marvel-real-life-civil-war-kevin-feige-ike-perlmutter/4207757). Under the control of Marvel CEO Isaac Perlmutter, Whedon's shoot was subjected to heavy-handed interference; to keep a shot he liked, he was forced to agree to add a scene setting up the next film.

Although Marvel's creative committee has since been disbanded, Marvel Studios chief Kevin Feige still wields enormous control over the films. Remember those special effects in *Doctor Strange* where buildings and the city twist and fold? Those were content Feige demanded the director add in large quantities — to the point where they became the film's biggest selling point.

Although the DC Extended Universe's builders take pride in their directors' unique points of view, the approach carries considerable risks.

Zack Snyder himself is a textbook example. A visual director with a highly distinctive personal style — cold color filters, ubiquitous slow motion, a love of action set pieces, and oblique homages to comics — his work has sharply polarized reception, with "too dark," "like a music video," and "unfriendly to casual viewers" making up the main criticisms.

For that reason, Warner is also learning from Marvel to strengthen its control over the DC Extended Universe. Starting with *Wonder Woman*, the series' tone has brightened considerably. And in the *Justice League* trailer, people likewise spotted quite a few jokes.

![Wonder Woman's trailers emphasized the comedic elements](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170526103838rRJf8VXpPIE1UihT.png-w600)

The rise in Geoff Johns's involvement and influence can be seen as a counterweight to creators' personal styles. One clear sign: *The Flash* has already lost two directors to creative differences. In the DC Extended Universe, directors' power has also been somewhat constrained — not quite the freedom Zack Snyder described.

### 3. Third, it's not just about setting up individual films — it's about connecting the whole universe

On this question, Marvel provided a template. In the Phase One films, movies like *Thor*, *Captain America*, and *Iron Man* served only to introduce the characters — a bit like the filler episodes of a TV series between the climactic ones. And once the character groundwork was done, *The Avengers*, assembling all the superheroes, became the true climax.

The DC Extended Universe did the opposite. All-hands-on-deck films like *Batman v Superman* and *Justice League* appeared early and often, while solo films were pushed back.

This entirely different route gave *Batman v Superman* a heavy burden, because it had to introduce the other members of the Justice League without making the related plot feel too contrived.

In fact, that was one of its many criticisms: Wonder Woman's appearance was too abrupt — she didn't even have any prior connection with her two future comrades; and the metahuman files were seen as forced product placement.

Zack Snyder has his own explanation: "Audiences saw in *Batman v Superman* who the woman would ultimately become. They go, wow, where did Wonder Woman come from? What's her story? What has she been through? And then you see the answers in her solo film — it explains who she is and where she's from."

![Wonder Woman in armor, first glimpsed as an old photograph in Batman v Superman](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170526104848FDKGruCNx4dMYz8O.jpg-w600)

"In this big event that was *Batman v Superman*, even though it had nothing to do with Wonder Woman's own story, it told audiences how we would treat her story," says Roven.

It's a technical craft. Zack Snyder describes it as the process of "sewing different worlds onto the same tapestry" — arguably the most challenging part of creating a complete universe. Their choice is to fill in the blanks left by earlier films with clues from later ones, and one film typically becomes the shooting guide for the next.

In the current plan, *Justice League* is a milestone destination. "*Justice League* is, you could say, the backbone of this universe," Zack Snyder says. "It guided how we shoot *Aquaman*, and after it come many new stories that let everyone look back and find clues in the Justice League."

*Justice League* will also shape the direction of *The Flash*. Roven reveals that once the script is finished, the film's final form will be closely tied to *Justice League*, because the Flash's history and personality will be defined by the Justice League story — "you can't entirely break away from the setup."

This means directors need to work closely together, however different their styles. Deborah says that although everyone is doing their own job, there is a community that includes all the film crews, and people's work overlaps to a degree. The directors share information and reach fairly consistent conclusions through constant discussion. Right now, they are in discussions with James Wan about how to handle the character of Aquaman.

### 4. How do you bring comic-book characters to life on screen?

Zack Snyder has become the most important bridge between comics and film. He contributed the comics adaptations *300* and *Watchmen* to Warner — the former became a talked-about hit in the dead month of March, the latter adapted a Hugo Award-winning work widely considered unfilmable and won the approval of most fans of the source material. On top of that, he is an open comics fan whose knowledge of the source material runs deep.

In the compositions, Easter eggs, and costume design of *Man of Steel* and *Batman v Superman*, you can see Snyder's fan fingerprints everywhere. He wants to reproduce, as faithfully as possible, the classic characters and scenes in every comics fan's mind.

![This shot from Batman v Superman faithfully reproduces Frank Miller's classic The Dark Knight Returns](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705261001405K2omkXpzdlD6jGy.jpg-w600)

Speaking of Wonder Woman's costume design, he says things in the film world are mutable, whereas in comics you can draw however you like. Bringing that costume to the screen required certain changes — for instance, they tried to give it a historical touch, to make it look like it came from a place like Paradise Island, protected by metal and armor.

"What's interesting is, whenever we pull an iconic character like Wonder Woman, Superman, or Batman out of the comics, when the costume is right, you know it at a glance. If what you make doesn't match everyone's comics memory, you've changed too much. So we tried all the elements of her comics look, so that the moment people see her, they realize: this iconic figure is Wonder Woman."

On the surface, Zack Snyder's on-screen vision for DC Comics matches that of most fans. Back when the news of Affleck playing Batman broke, angry netizens even petitioned the White House website to stop him from playing Batman — but when the first Batman still was released and he moved for the first time in the teaser trailer, nearly everyone fell silent. Similar cases include the Kryptonian fight in *Man of Steel* and the scene in *Batman v Superman* where Batman rescues Martha — both widely recognized as "highly faithful to the comics."

But when it comes to character traits, Zack Snyder often faces questions about straying from the characters' original personalities. Not everyone likes his take on the heroes. In fact, in the comics, every character spawns many different versions under different writers; choosing one of them inevitably dissatisfies another group — a problem all creators of comics adaptations must face.

Faced with these hard-to-please fans, how do you strike a balance between comics and film? Zack Snyder's answer: "I love DC fans — I'm a fan myself. So I can empathize, especially when I see the things I love up on the screen... Our goal is to bring the characters to life, to realize childhood dreams as much as possible. But in the end, you still have to follow a director's instincts."

### 5. What plans does the DC Extended Universe have for the future — and what challenges will it face?

For now, the DC Extended Universe is advancing the universe at its own pace. Some of the news looks worrisome: DC, not yet recovered from the bad reviews, announced that *Nightwing*, *Gotham City Sirens*, and *Batgirl* had been put on the schedule — once again raising suspicions that Warner was being too hasty. And the news that Ben Affleck, after the dismal failure of *Live by Night*, which he wrote, directed, and starred in, had given up directing *The Batman* drew considerable speculation.

But amid the rumors, there is also some confirmed good news: *Cloverfield* director Matt Reeves will take over from Affleck to direct *The Batman*; and *The Flash* is close to locking a director, with *Kingsman* director Matthew Vaughn the favorite.

The DC Extended Universe has 15 unreleased projects, and it is still full of possibilities. "In the DC Extended Universe, anything can happen," Zack Snyder says. "Talking about this now might be a bit of a spoiler. But I think I can tell you: the stories to draw from fill this huge, fascinating palette, and everyone should look forward to those potential paths."

![Batman v Superman teased a major event in a future/parallel universe](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170526105435bKiOalhvNH2ToCXy.jpg-w600)

However, a piece of breaking news on May 23 made the future look uncertain once more. In [an interview with *The Hollywood Reporter*](http://www.hollywoodreporter.com/heat-vision/zack-snyder-steps-down-justice-league-deal-family-tragedy-1006455?utm_source=twitter&utm_source=t.co&utm_medium=referral), Snyder revealed that his 20-year-old daughter, Autumn Snyder, died by suicide in March. He had intended to throw himself into work to heal: "But I later realized we really needed to step away for a while and be with the children who need me... I understand what kind of talk it'll cause online — they'll do it no matter what — and the fact is, I no longer care about any of that."

Warner's first move was to delay the release of *Justice League*, but Snyder refused. He turned to Joss Whedon, who had signed on to direct *Batgirl*, and Whedon will take over his work, finishing *Justice League*'s post-production and a small amount of reshoots.

"There will be very little shooting, and it will fit Zack's style and tone completely. We won't introduce new characters — just a few new scenes," Warner chairman Toby Emmerich said in response to questions about how two directors' styles would merge.

Beyond that, the bigger challenge is that audiences seem to be growing fatigued with superhero films. *Avengers: Age of Ultron* failed to top the first film's box office; *Captain America: Civil War*, billed as "*Avengers* 2.5," came in a bit lower still than *Age of Ultron*. Over the past 10 years, audiences have been fed all kinds of superhero movies, which is why *Deadpool* — with its long-missing freshness and peculiar style — found unexpected success.

Asked what freshness the DC Extended Universe can bring to the market, Snyder's answer was history — DC Comics' classic characters and its rich trove of story resources.

"When you travel around the world, you see lots of people wearing Batman or Superman T-shirts," Deborah adds. "Each of the big three has more than 75 years of history, and their stories continue today, welcomed around the globe — not just by American audiences but by others worldwide. That says something. There's a reason for it."

The appeal of the comics speaks for itself, but freshness and distinctiveness in film are another matter. The strategy the DC Extended Universe can stick to is probably still letting different directors shoot stylistically distinct films in their own ways. It may displease some people, but a creative team ultimately has to decide which path it wants to take — and what it is willing to give up for it.
