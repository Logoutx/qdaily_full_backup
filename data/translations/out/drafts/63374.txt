Liu Heung Shing believes that photographs—especially good photographs—depend more on what the photographer knows than on what the photographer sees.

Liu Heung Shing became a photojournalist in 1976, first working for *Time* magazine and the Associated Press. He has taken many photographs; the one he considers the most difficult is the portrait of Gorbachev at the moment the dissolution of the Soviet Union was announced.

The difficulty was obvious. Liu Heung Shing had slipped into the venue with others, and officers of the Soviet Committee for State Security, the "KGB," had warned him not to take pictures. Even without outside obstacles, it was still a gamble. He set his shutter speed to one-thirtieth of a second, but capturing the blur of the papers at the moment Gorbachev closed his speech still required a bit of luck.

![Photo: Liu Heung Shing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190430113218Z3hdEqLNoItGJzkp.jpg-w600)

This photograph, taken on the night of December 25, 1991, at the Kremlin, is also regarded as a model of news photography. Liu Heung Shing and his team won the Pulitzer Prize for Spot News Photography in 1992. The Pulitzer Prize is the highest honor in journalism.

Before that, Liu Heung Shing had been the Associated Press's Photographer of the Year, a title awarded in recognition of his news photographs taken in China. Most of these China photographs do not look as difficult or as historically weighty as the Gorbachev portrait. They belong to the "everyday moments" that Liu Heung Shing has always valued and that are now very much in vogue. The years he applied to go to China were unusual—between 1976 and 1983, right when Mao Zedong died and the Cultural Revolution ended—so these everyday photographs were also part of a great historical transformation.

Liu Heung Shing has published more than 10 photography books. In 1983, Penguin Books published his collection *China after Mao* in the United Kingdom. In 1992, the Associated Press published *The Collapse of the Soviet Union: The Fall of an Empire* (《苏联解体：一个帝国的坠落》).

His latest book, *A Life in a Sea of Red*, was released in March of this year by the German publisher Steidl. It is an interesting edit: Liu Heung Shing wanted to present both the collapse of the Soviet Union and the rise of China, placing photographs he had taken in both places side by side. "There are still many China stories that have not been reported. Not too many—too few. This year there are so many anniversaries in China. After the Berlin Wall fell in '89, the Soviet Union quickly disintegrated, and I happened to be there."

He also quotes Susan Sontag's *On Photography* to explain his habit of repeatedly re-editing his photobooks over the years: as time changes, the context of a photograph changes as well. This was not the first time he had quoted Sontag—making few judgments but restating them on different occasions is also Liu Heung Shing's habit.

Liu Heung Shing was born in Hong Kong in 1951. His father was from Hunan and his mother from Fujian. In 1954, just after he learned to walk, Liu Heung Shing returned with his mother to Fujian to attend school. For a child, this may not have been the wisest decision. Because of his mother's landlord-class family background, Liu Heung Shing was classified as a descendant of the "black five categories." The Great Leap Forward brought widespread malnutrition, affecting all the neighbors who had moved into the private courtyard of Liu Heung Shing's family during the wave of nationalization—Liu Heung Shing wrote about this experience in the preface when the Chinese edition of *China after Mao* was published in mainland China in 2009. His writing is filled with the same astonishing detail as his photographs.

Liu's father was a newspaperman in Hong Kong; Liu Heung Shing later recalled that following his father into journalism was only natural. Not long after, Liu Heung Shing returned to Hong Kong. He studied painting for a while but was frustrated by his lack of talent. During that period he spent most of his time learning English and Cantonese; what left the deepest impression was "during the summer vacation, my father taught me to translate short news dispatches from the Associated Press and Reuters into Chinese."

In 1970, Liu Heung Shing left Hong Kong to major in political science at Hunter College of the City University of New York. In his final year of college, he took a photography course taught by *Life* magazine photographer Gjon Mili. But at the time Liu Heung Shing did not know who he was. In retrospect, the course had a great influence on Liu Heung Shing. After graduating, he interned with Gjon Mili at *Life* magazine. Later, a Chinese professor at Hunter College introduced him to a Harvard classmate of his—Kelso Sutton, president of Time Inc. When they met, Sutton asked Liu Heung Shing what he wanted to do with his life. "I immediately said I wanted to go to China." A week later, a well-known picture editor contacted him.

Coinciding with the normalization of diplomatic relations between China and the United States on January 1, 1979, Liu Heung Shing became *Time* magazine's first foreign photojournalist based in Beijing in 1978. For the first three years, he was the only foreign photojournalist in Beijing.

"The right person was placed at the right time." Summing up his career as a photojournalist, Liu Heung Shing often says this. Do not mistake this for pure modesty. Naturally, some people have rare opportunities: they are present when the times undergo great change. But David Remnick and Liu Heung Shing are rarer still. After years of posting in the Soviet Union, David Remnick won the Pulitzer Prize in 1994 for *Lenin's Tomb* and later returned to the United States to become editor-in-chief of *The New Yorker*.

Liu Heung Shing has explained why it had to be Liu Heung Shing. The influence of Gjon Mili was part of it: Mili never mentioned aperture, camera, or depth of field (although Mili was one of the inventors of flash equipment), but he taught him that "one must care deeply about one's subject."

"Being able to speak Mandarin and several provincial dialects did make my work easier than that of other photographers, but that advantage should not be overestimated. This was a country in which foreigners were excluded from major events." Although Liu Heung Shing wrote in the preface to one book that "I was never obstructed by the government because of my reporting and photographs," in fact he encountered several direct interventions. Another "profound lesson" came when, while photographing the nighttime celebrations in the square after the Chinese women's volleyball team defeated the American team, his car and overcoat were splashed with sulfuric acid—something he discovered only afterward.

In the 1970s Liu Heung Shing had been influenced by Marc Riboud; he had seen Riboud's *The Three Banners of China*. Some people also compare him to Henri Cartier-Bresson, who came to China many times in the 1940s and 1950s and left behind some unforgettable images of China. But the China he photographed was completely different from theirs. Liu Heung Shing believes he had a deeper understanding of the body language in the culture. He holds to one view: photographs—especially good photographs—depend more on what the photographer knows than on what the photographer sees.

Tiziano Terzani, former Beijing bureau chief of *Der Spiegel*, once assessed why Liu Heung Shing was so rare—at least for the images of China between 1978 and 1983. He said: "In Vietnam, the basic quality of a great photographer is courage. ... In China there is no comparable danger; what is needed here is wisdom. The courage to see through the surface of things and pierce the web of lies and false impressions woven for those who are easily satisfied and want to live easy lives. Liu possesses this enviable courage."

Liu Heung Shing seems to have become an important figure, and an anecdote he often recounts proves this. "Yao Wei, an official in the Information Department of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, once invited me to a meeting. He told me they had investigated the activities of foreign media between 1979 and 1981 and found that sixty-five percent of the photographs about China published in the West had been taken by me." He relayed Yao Wei's words: "Mr. Liu, please don't work so hard from now on."

In photography circles—if such circles exist—the same was true. Penguin published *China after Mao* in 1983, but the book could not circulate in mainland China. Liu Heung Shing later learned that "at that time Chinese photographers would translate the captions, by hand. Because back then there were no photobooks about everyday life in China. That version is still in circulation today."

After winning the Pulitzer Prize in 1991, no one doubted this anymore.

For many years, to prove himself, Liu Heung Shing was posted all over: New Delhi, Punjab, Sri Lanka, Seoul, Moscow, Georgia, Afghanistan. He sent back news photographs that were widely circulated around the world and accumulated experiences that ordinary people cannot have: rigging up his own generator, developing photos on a toilet seat, a bullet passing one centimeter from a vital organ... People always like to ask him about these experiences, which makes him seem more legendary. On the other hand, these war-zone experiences reinforced a certain trait in Liu Heung Shing—a tendency to solve problems rather than criticize and complain.

In the late 1990s, Liu Heung Shing decided to return to Asia. He was no longer satisfied with being a photojournalist; years in war zones and revolution sites had also left him somewhat "compassion fatigued." He photographed less. He wanted to start his own media outlet. "Information is benign." Liu Heung Shing also wanted to promote the flow of information in China.

After briefly founding, operating, and shutting down *The Chinese* (《中》) magazine, Liu Heung Shing returned to Beijing and moved into a siheyuan on Jingshan West Street. From there he could see the White Pagoda and Jingshan, a detail that fashion media loved to dwell on. But Liu Heung Shing's ambition did not become any easier to fulfill; to this day he has not managed, as David Remnick did, to make the natural transition from an outstanding reporter to the editor-in-chief of an influential publication.

In 1997 Liu Heung Shing became Time Warner's chief representative in China. Three years later, he was appointed executive vice president of News Corporation (China), helping Star TV's Chinese channel secure a foothold in China. The business was always very tricky. Afterward, Liu Heung Shing had brief stints at the talent agency CAA and the local media company Modern Media, but he considers these experiences not worth mentioning.

Five years ago, Liu Heung Shing moved to Shanghai, to the area of the former French Concession. The reason was that the Xuhui District government was developing cultural projects on the West Bund and had invited Liu Heung Shing to found the Shanghai Center of Photography. In fact, Liu Heung Shing's cooperation with Shanghai began earlier. He and his partner Karen Smith were the curators of the image collection campaign for the Shanghai Corporate Pavilion at the 2010 Shanghai World Expo. Commissioned by the Shanghai World Expo, they also co-edited *Shanghai 1842–2010: Portrait of a Great City* (《上海 1842-2010 一座伟大城市的肖像》). In August 2013, Liu Heung Shing held a solo photography exhibition titled "Chinese Dream" at the China Art Museum Shanghai in Shanghai.

![Shanghai 1842–2010: Portrait of a Great City (《上海 1842-2010 一座伟大城市的肖像》), image from Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904301201226CuHBpFVTnNWeira.jpg-w600)

Everyone wants to work with Liu Heung Shing because he is somebody. But not always has Liu Heung Shing been "placed at the right time in the right position." From the start of his career as a photojournalist, Liu Heung Shing had a gift for making friends and knew how to seize opportunities. Officials understood what he said and valued his opinions. Liu Heung Shing is also clever; he refuses to be a "mascot."

Regarding this country, Liu Heung Shing has some basic judgments. He likes to quote A Cheng's frequent invocation of "common sense." "Much of the social and political turmoil and trauma of the previous era was caused by the loss of common sense. Between 1976 and 1983 ... at that time the country was taking its first tentative steps to recover and rebuild what A Cheng called 'common sense.'" He criticizes the fact that common sense has not yet been fully restored, but looking around, he believes there is still much that can be done. This is also puzzling.

"She will steady herself again; she has always done so." Liu Heung Shing once quoted a passage from the preface to Lin Yutang's *My Country and My People*. "She" refers to China. Most of the time, this can be understood as Liu Heung Shing's American-style optimism, combined with his special status as someone between insider and outsider.

In March of this year, we met in Hong Kong. Liu Heung Shing's influence has not diminished. Because of the new book's publication and Art Basel Hong Kong, he had six speaking engagements that week. Our interview was squeezed in whenever there was a gap. Crossing the road in Central, Liu Heung Shing said with apparent conviction: "The only thing you can do is show them a book. Show them photograph. And don't say their theories don't work, or that his practise doesn't work."

"This is very much like what Deng Xiaoping said back then: don't argue. Without that, China wouldn't be where it is today."

We also talked with Liu Heung Shing about much more:

**Q: You mentioned that when foreigners photograph China, there is a sense of detachment, a different China from what Chinese people see. That is an advantage. But you also mentioned that the French, such as Cartier-Bresson and Marc Riboud, had romantic imaginings of China. That seems like a disadvantage.**

Not all French people are like that. The two of them came from relatively bourgeois, relatively well-off families. I went to France quite often in the 1980s. After the French got rid of their emperor, their path was different from Britain's industrial revolution, but both countries have very clear attitudes toward class. If you look at French history, early on they imagined that there was an excellent culture in the East—in fact it was imagined by them—a highly trained bureaucratic system, a civilized country; they used this to attack the French king at the time. But none of these people had been to China.

Cartier-Bresson's family, for example, was a very wealthy textile family; he was a member of the French Communist Party, but not a very active one. This has a little in common with the founding of the Chinese Communist Party, when some Communist Party members also came from very good families. So when they came to China, part of them sympathized with communism and had a desire to build an egalitarian society. On top of that, they instinctively had a French romanticism. These two things combined, and the China they photographed made Chinese people feel very comfortable.

Now Magnum also has several French photographers who come to photograph China, like Patrick Zachmann, from a younger generation; they don't have this relatively aristocratic worldview.

**Q: How would you describe your identity and perspective? Your background is very complex.**

In photography there is something very important that has to do with cultural and emotional upbringing. I photograph my fellow countrymen. But I was influenced not only by American education; right after graduation I went to report in Spain and France. I met Song Huai-Kuei in Paris very early on. She had just arrived. Her marriage to the Bulgarian artist Marin Varbanov was personally approved by Zhou Enlai. In Paris, they also lived in an artists' apartment. Later I went to France many, many times.

I think it would be different for an ABC who grew up purely in America. There are also artists and photographers who went to the West, and I'm not the only one. So it is the influence of many cultures. I am also very interested in China. My father was a newspaperman at *Ta Kung Pao*. At that time in Hong Kong, *Ta Kung Pao* was a progressive newspaper. But when the Americans landed on the moon, you couldn't report it—no different from today. Sitting across from him was Cao Juren. These were all literati. But they still had a certain—you know, *Ta Kung Pao* was originally a Shanghai newspaper, and it seems to have been the first Chinese newspaper to send a reporter to cover World War I. At that time, everyone pooled their money to translate Edgar Snow's *Red Star Over China* into Chinese—back then they had a very positive, intellectual enthusiasm for new possibilities.

So last night I mentioned (referring to the talk and conversation at the University of Hong Kong) that the May Fourth Movement was China's first enlightenment, at least for the literati. Then the late 1970s and early 1980s were the second enlightenment.

At that time, if not for my reporting on the Stars Art Exhibition, today perhaps no one would know about it. Because on the Democracy Wall there were also other artists who were not connected to government organizations. They also used a clothesline and clothespins to hang up their own works.

![1979, artist Ma Desheng, one of the core members of the Stars Art Exhibition, giving a speech. Photo: Liu Heung Shing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190430113343TLKvmlZ2xHtM7gj5.jpg-w600)

**Q: Many historians, especially art historians, point out that documenting the present is difficult, because in the moment you cannot judge who is important and who will be remembered by history. After the Artists Association issued the "Open Manifesto for Painting Societies," there may have been a dozen or so similar groups in Beijing at the time.**

Yes, and there was also the April Photo Society.

**Q: How did you realize what was important and worth recording?**

There was no such awareness at the time. Because back then there were also the earliest writers with feminist consciousness, as well as scar literature, and publishers were putting out books on all aspects of foreign countries—politics, philosophy, literature, everything was being translated. Publishing was suddenly loosened up. Because publishing had always had state budget allocations, and before that there wasn't much worth publishing; suddenly this environment came along, and they all published. At that time, students from Renmin University also took part in demonstrations; the Second Artillery Corps occupied their campus, and they went on marches. I also photographed Peking opera actress Zhao Yanxia leaving the state system to start her own performance troupe. Agriculture was beginning the household responsibility system, so the whole of society was moving out of the pattern of the previous era.

At that time the Democracy Wall had many dissidents and a large number of unofficial journals. If you look at my photos, you'll see I climbed on top of the Democracy Wall; those people were holding a few mao to buy these journals. So at that time I felt it was the second enlightenment. Looking back, especially after '89, or even after '83, Deng Xiaoping criticized Bai Hua's *Unrequited Love* and Yuan Yunsheng's nude mural (note: in 1979 Yuan Yunsheng created a mural for the Capital International Airport that included a nude female figure; in 1980 it was covered up, and in 1982 it was sealed off and nailed shut). Ideologically, things retreated again.

**Q: Listening to your talk, one gets the feeling that your descriptions of history are more moderate than those of other witnesses. In the past few days, I've also met many people who want to get from you a Western perspective on China. Judging from their reactions, your answers don't satisfy them. Why is that?**

I think the West now has its own narrative, and mainland China has its own way of narrating; Hong Kong and Taiwan are different again. My education tells me that telling stories requires details. In the end, pictures are also a kind of detail; what you photograph are details of our lives. This may also be my habit of observing things over the years. When I discuss issues and views with people, I also feel that things ... things are not black and white, they are gray. So in this 24-hour, all-round information age, I think people increasingly tend to read and see things in a very black-and-white way.

Things keep changing. Including Fukuyama, who thought the fall of the Berlin Wall was the end of history. Including my capturing the moment Gorbachev resigned and the Soviet Union disintegrated—but in fact history does not stop instantly; it keeps moving forward. I sometimes find that when I tell Americans that China is not like this, and I tell Chinese people that China is also not like what you think, and to Hong Kong people I also encourage them to relax and let go. Young people today don't know what Hong Kong was like in the past. They always say that after '97 things became such and such. I think One Country, Two Systems has been around for 20 years, and in fact enormous efforts have been made in all respects to maintain it. If you move aside this noise, it is no different from the Hong Kong I know.

**Q: Is the idea that "things are gray" something that has remained unchanged from the 1970s until now, or something that gradually formed?**

I think it gradually, gradually formed. Some people say read ten thousand books and travel ten thousand miles. Having walked through so many places, not just going somewhere for a few weeks or months, but years, going through many springs, summers, autumns, and winters in each place. I think leisure is very important in a person's life and learning process. In a state of leisure, listening to people talk. Rather than while others are talking, you can't take it in, and then you want to express your own views. This process, well, different people have different ways of handling things.

**Q: You have described the working method of a foreign correspondent. "Basically you stay in one place for four to five years; the New York head office never tells us what to photograph, except for major events. The editorial department tells us you are the eyes and ears of our newspaper, you are in India, China, the Soviet Union—you tell us what is happening there. This editorial philosophy is what Western mainstream media has always practiced. So our job may be to make friends; friends tell me about their society, their country, and I bring an unfamiliar eye to learn and to feel—this is actually the original intention of reporting."**

**In China in the 1970s and 1980s, you not only made friends with artists but also with Xinhua reporters and government officials. How did you become friends with them?**

Mr. Luce, who was born in China, founded *Time*, *Fortune*, and *Life* magazines. When he founded *Life* magazine, he wrote something to the effect that to see life, you look at the arrogance of those with power and wealth, and also at the lives of the poor. You look at the women men love, and the men women love. Life is about observing, being inspired, being encouraged. Only then is life interesting. The American interpretation is actually also a kind of humanism. It places a journalist, or a photographer's observations and inspirations, in a very suitable position.

**Q: Making friends is a skill. How specifically did you become friends with these people?**

You are sincere with people. What you yourself bring into the conversation ... your way of speaking directly affects whether the other person wants to talk to you. I often find that many people won't tell their parents or family many things, but they may tell a stranger. And who is this stranger? I often find that when I go to interview and photograph people, I am also being interviewed and photographed by them. More than once I've found that the interviewer can't continue the interview. The knowledge structure this person brings is strange. He wants to take you to a place that doesn't exist. "What do you like most ..." He takes the very interesting things in life and society, and the process of describing each thing ... he has already drawn some conclusions, and he wants to fit me into his conclusions.

![1981, Yuetan Park. Photo: Liu Heung Shing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190430114701omyJAT4IhCBXqt9c.jpg-w600)

**Q: At that time you photographed so much daily life in China—for example, two lovers with crossed feet expressing intimacy. Could Westerners understand its meaning?**

Yes. If you look at the assessments of people like Fox Butterfield (note: from 1978 to 1981, the first editor-in-chief of *The New York Times*'s Beijing bureau), they all consistently used the word "intimacy." They said very few photographers could get so close to Chinese people. Although I am also Chinese, the problem is that other Chinese photographers simply couldn't see this kind of thing, or they would say, what does this have to do with us. For them, I had to enter the Great Hall of the People to prove I was a photojournalist.

There was a photographer in Guangdong called An Ge, who was in Hong Kong at the time. Once when I passed through Hong Kong, he came to talk to me. I said you are wasting your time in Hong Kong; you should hurry back. Don't go to Tibet or Xinjiang; just photograph the Guangdong you know, the things around you. Because I knew that in the China News Service environment, it was impossible for them to send you here today and there tomorrow, nor would they give you three to five years; so in this situation, the most practical advice I gave him was to photograph well the environment you are familiar with. So he later went back and published a book called *China after Deng* (《邓小平后的中国》).

When Beijing photographers saw the book *China after Mao*, Wang Wenlan, chief photojournalist of *China Daily*, said it was as if someone had hit him in the head with a hammer. So that book actually influenced a generation of photographers.

![1982, Beijing, Deng Xiaoping at a welcome reception for American entrepreneur Armand Hammer. Deng hoped to use the occasion to attract foreign investment and promote Sino-foreign joint ventures. Photo: Liu Heung Shing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190430114718Uu7Yb9BmhFcnvRQN.jpg-w600)

**Q: Let's talk about your personal experience. In the late 1990s, you photographed much less. Returning to the question I asked you at the beginning, when you first came to China there was a sense of detachment that let you see different things; later you became more and more involved in China's development, including Time Warner's and News Corporation's projects landing in China, Modern Media Group, and more cooperation with the Shanghai government. Did deeper involvement make you stop photographing?**

My time at Modern Media was actually very short. I mainly spent my time at News Corporation and Time Warner. At that time they asked me to do some things for them in China. I don't know if you know about a monthly magazine I started in Hong Kong.

**Q: *The Chinese*.**

Yes. In the West, it is a very natural process for a reporter or a photographer to become an editor-in-chief.

After Moscow, my possible choices were the White House or the European Union. By then I already felt this kind of assignment, this kind of work, was not what I wanted to do. I went to India, to the former Soviet Union, to China, and found it very interesting. But at that time Sondhi Limthongkul—now the leader of Thailand's "Yellow Shirts"—was printing my book at his place, so we met in Bangkok. He said, come back. I said, what would I come back to Asia to do. Because he wanted to be Asia's Murdoch. He had bought some American magazines; his printing plant in Thailand was the largest in Asia. He had his own *Manager* newspaper, which is very influential in Bangkok today, equivalent to Thailand's *Financial Times*. He encouraged me to return to Asia. In fact, he also hit on what I wanted to do: return to Asia, return to China. Because I felt that China's story was far from finished.

From the age of 26, I entered the most mainstream of American media and was posted to China. I very much hoped that after reform and opening up reached a certain stage, the next wave would be media opening up, because I feel that for society to progress, the ability to create hardware and production capacity is there, and Chinese people are very pragmatic and hardworking. But to build a bigger, more civilized country requires the development of the humanities. So I felt media was a platform to present another kind of social force to ourselves and to the world. Because our conversations and speech are always aphoristic, slogan-like. This is not the most excellent civilization. So I always hoped to use very good pictures and very good writing. That is what *The Chinese* monthly was meant to do.

Why didn't it last long? Because the 1997 financial crisis started in Thailand, and the Thai Farmers' Bank wanted to call back the 10-billion-baht loan it had given Mr. Sondhi. I returned to the old Time Warner to save my magazine. I said, buy this magazine. They flipped through it and said, the magazine is good, but we don't do Chinese publishing; you'd better come back to us. So I went and helped the two groups land very difficult operations like television channels. You know how hard it is in China, especially Murdoch's Star TV, which was a channel the Chinese government had never even seen, starting from zero, and you had to get it landed.

But at that time they didn't even know where the door was, so it was very exhausting. Later it was sold to CMC Holdings, and they knew how to land Star as a production company in Guangdong to produce programs. Because Murdoch's News Corporation producers were all foreigners; the cost of producing content in China was high, the subjects weren't necessarily what Chinese people wanted to watch, and they couldn't attract advertising, so they couldn't open up the situation.

**Q: How did you help them land? It seems to have required a great deal of connections and resources. How did you have these resources?**

I had opportunities to communicate with Chinese leaders. If you open an automobile factory, they see it as a business. My point was that media is also a business, an industry; if you interact with European and American society but don't let these media companies' businesses enter China or let them do anything, then you have no influence whatsoever over the media industry. They took this in.

![1981, Beijing, boxing champion Muhammad Ali challenging the gilded lions in front of the Forbidden City, his wife Veronica smiling nearby. Ali had coached Chinese boxers during a closed period. Photo: Liu Heung Shing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190430114911GEp7iWYLRTaytwXk.jpg-w600)

**Q: Did they want to demonstrate their influence through this?**

No. Influence is sometimes mutually constraining. Like *The New York Times* and *The Washington Post* constrain each other. If they don't do anything in China, then they can say whatever they want about China, because there is not the slightest constraint on them. What I said was meant positively, not that through this you can do your propaganda; that is completely impossible.

This is the friendliest way for both sides to safeguard their respective national and social interests. Because what Chinese people say, the world cannot understand. I don't know if you have understood that this way of expressing things, others can't take it in. The Western way, except for internal reference materials and *Reference News*, is basically not taken in.

**Q: Do you have ambitions to build a media group yourself?**

I don't. I'm not a good manager.

I always have an intuition. Information itself is benign. In the process of being benign, you have to allow different viewpoints, but you don't need to rush to conclusions about this view or that view. Last week the president of Harvard University gave a speech at Peking University, and in the end it was all about this. If China does not develop good media, I think we will regretfully hand over all these issues of humanities, human rights, and so on to others. Because Westerners see humanistic values as universal values. Then universal values are not peculiar to Europe. But if you don't express your views, you are giving up your universal values.

**Q: You think there are two different universal values.**

There can be more than two. But you need to clearly express what your values are. China has not been very successful in this regard, because of the language and methods it uses. If you want to be a superpower and sit as an equal with everyone, you should tell them where your values lie.

**Q: You think this is only a question of language and method?**

This is a question of mentality.

**Q: In the 1990s you moved to Beijing and lived in a siheyuan. In 2014 you moved to Shanghai. Was moving to Shanghai a big change for you?**

Yes. In fact it was also a return to something. I founded the Shanghai Center of Photography (SCôP). You let people see. Photography as an art, as a language. What you can express, how you express it. How to curate an exhibition. This thing, you can talk about it one way, but making it is another. When I was at university in the United States, I went to see ICP, the International Center of Photography. I said, oh, so images have this kind of power.

At that time the Xuhui District Party secretary and the others wanted to build the West Bund cultural corridor. They said, Mr. Liu, come to Shanghai.

If I put it this way, you can understand: from the first day of my work, very consistent. Through photographic images, through language, through the way of reporting, through the way of recording, including *China after Mao* or this book, I am telling you what China is like through my eyes.

**Q: Are you someone particularly sensitive to opportunities and platforms? For example, the opportunities that AP and *Time* offered to come to China were seized by very few people. Including the opportunity the Xuhui District government gave you—you thought maybe you could build a photography center like the ICP.**

But many people say Liu Heung Shing is lucky. They don't say Liu Heung Shing works very hard. Opportunities are created for those who are prepared.

The Shanghai Center of Photography on the West Bund—the building had to be renovated. Dealing with them for ten months, I was basically a construction worker. China has this mechanism. But neither the West Bund nor the Xuhui District government would send someone to do it for me. If SCôP now sets up a small art museum, there are standards for small art museums. Exhibitions have standards of quality, introductions in Chinese and English that have to look authentic to Westerners. So every matter, big or small, comes down to details.

![Image courtesy: Shanghai Center of Photography](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190430121100UnCNEo1gbAdc7eV8.jpg-w600)

**Q: You are a hands-on person.**

Yes, yes, yes. This is also the influence of the foreign-correspondent experience. The newspaper gives you a very, very long leash; they never reel it back in unless you haven't done your job well.

But these institutions don't say, you go to California, you go to our very important western bureau, because you're from the East and don't need to come; they say, we know your ability to report on politics, don't go to Washington. You go to a western place. You know how more than a thousand American newspapers normally operate. I would give up a sunny California house to go to India; none of my AP colleagues understood. They came into the darkroom, "I heard you're going to India? Every AP reporter wants to be posted to Southern California. You finally came back from China with such difficulty, and now you want to run off to India—are you out of your mind?"

**Q: Really? AP reporters don't have any ambition to report?**

You think so? There aren't many foreign postings at AP. I understood very well. In China, they said, you're Chinese, your Chinese is such and such, you have a natural advantage. I said, I'll go to India to prove myself. In India I had no advantages.

**Q: You went with the idea of proving yourself.**

Yes, yes. In Sri Lanka, Nepal, and Pakistan, I opened all the photo bureaus. When the Soviet Union sent troops into Afghanistan, I was the first Western journalist to enter Afghanistan. I was based in New Delhi and kept drinking whiskey with Afghan diplomats until finally he gave me a visa. After entering, on the battlefield, what do you do when the fax machine has no power? I went to rent a generator, then there was no diesel, so I went to buy diesel from Soviet soldiers to generate electricity. Otherwise you can't fax. Normally, if you go to the newspaper and say my computer has no power, everyone accepts that. But as a war correspondent, as a foreign correspondent, you don't tell people what your troubles are, because whether it's page one or page 10, if your story isn't there tomorrow, you don't exist.

**Q: Did this have a big influence on your working style? Always wanting to solve problems.**

I think it was a training for me.

**Q: In 2013 you published the photobook *Chinese Dream*. In an earlier interview, you mentioned that the term "Chinese Dream" was actually your own idea. It has nothing to do with the "Chinese Dream" later proposed at the national level.**

Yes. At that time they wanted to hold an exhibition for me. My "Chinese Dream" was the China I observed—I had a time frame: entering the 21st century, in first- and second-tier cities, materially speaking, Chinese people's dreams had already been realized. In the 1950s and 1960s, for Chinese people, a single cigarette could get something done. This exhibition was about what that process was like.

**Q: Later, when you saw the term become official discourse everywhere, did you want to avoid confusion, to clearly distinguish your use from it?**

I wanted to distinguish it. Because that big Chinese Dream they talk about, the things that need to be done are exactly what I'm talking to you about today, but they haven't done them, or don't know how to do them.

**Q: You just expressed at the China Club, and also in our conversation, that political leaders may come and go, but as a photographer, there is always the opportunity to capture moving moments in everyday life. Have you looked at Chinese photographic works recently and captured these everyday moments?**

Chinese photographers, I think, have a problem: from my observation, they have an unverified, macroscopic way of looking. Their understanding of photography is to use words rather than images. Because for a long time in China there has been a theory, widely accepted and even official, that the caption is more important than the picture.

Today, of course, there are many young people who sometimes rush to imitate the West, different new styles, or fashion. I can understand this feeling. Because Chinese people still have an anxious mentality in all respects. To really integrate into the world and connect with the world, I can understand this feeling. But they don't have this humanistic training to observe details. This detail is what photographic language is best at. For an observant person, photography is a very important tool, but you have to be interested in the subject. So last night, I told the people at the University of Hong Kong that you need curiosity. This curiosity is the wisdom that gives you the ability to understand the things you are curious about.

Moreover, Chinese photography has another problem: the media. Chinese media cannot walk alongside the world's media. Foreign media can't see it, and Chinese media is not up to the task. Even monthlies, in terms of images. Western magazines have established norms and standards over so many years, how to tell stories. So the influence here starts from childhood. If we say we are good here, what standards do you use to say we are good? You see someone's photography that has never been published. So the media is an enabler, a platform that allows people to show their work, to be seen, to be criticized. Only then is there progress. If you write an article and no one reads it, and no one gives you their opinion after reading it, then you are just one person.

Chinese media often has a heroic tendency. It thinks media is supervision. It takes Western media theory wholesale but can't digest it. Chinese journalists all want to win awards; only after winning an award do they have a platform and can speak. I say I have never once applied for an award in my life. They were all given to me.

I was a foreign correspondent for more than twenty years, and not once did I have to hold any meetings with anyone. I just went and did the work I needed to do. You go to a different country; if you don't have friends, how do you discover that society, how do you more deeply understand it, where is its value.

**Q: Your identity is still special. Compared with domestic photographers and journalists, you are an intermediate state, which gives you more possibilities.**

Yes. There is no doubt about that. But Xinhua and *People's Daily* also send people abroad.

**Q: This brings us to the difference between Xinhua and the Associated Press.**

No, the Associated Press also sends photographers all over the world. After I left, Reuters and the Associated Press also had quite a few ethnic Chinese reporters. Different eras have many people with this special identity. They are no different from me. The question is, how do you handle the work. So, it comes back to the individual. Your own learning, your own understanding, your observation of things. How you look at things. Whether you listen carefully when people talk. If you listen carefully, you will be inspired and help yourself. Isn't this what we do every day?

In this line of work, what readers need most is respect. You are not facing one reader but a large readership. You should have an understanding of yourself: readers are smart, so don't try to fool them. If a journalist always wants to be a hero, it will produce something: you will want to lecture and instruct your readers, "I know more than you." This is very wrong.
