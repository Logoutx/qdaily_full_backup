This is, in fact, a classic success story in the traditional sense for a traditional Chinese company: sign the hottest celebrities as spokespeople, spend big on hard-hitting TV commercials, title-sponsor the hottest variety shows... pouring anywhere from several hundred million to over a billion yuan into all this every year.

Wrapped up in it are ambition, courage and boldness, self-satisfied calculations, the shortcuts of clever people... and then, riding on a vast and bewildered consumer base, it succeeded — it won in the market.

We're talking about Kans. In 2015, Kans claimed retail sales of 9 billion yuan for the year, close to the China sales of the L'Oréal Paris brand — which made its owner, Shanghai Chicmax Cosmetics Co., Ltd. (hereafter "Chicmax"), the domestic cosmetics company with the second-largest sales, behind only Shanghai Jahwa.

Today, Kans has become that kind of presence: it comes up whenever people discuss the rise of Chinese homegrown cosmetics brands; whenever they talk about how WeChat selling made people rich, and how important having a brand is for a WeChat seller (including the decline of WeChat selling); whenever some ill-defined "pain point" is invoked, its Red BB or its Mega Hydration line gets mentioned; and whenever seizing market opportunities is discussed, it still comes up...

How did this company, which seems to have burst out of nowhere into our lives, turn Kans into a first-tier brand? What exactly did it do?

## Before Its Sudden Fame, It Had Already Walked Nearly Every Path a Homegrown Cosmetics Brand Could

Kans was founded far earlier than you might imagine. From its founding in 2002 to 2010, the road it traveled was no different from that of most Chinese homegrown cosmetics companies.

Back then, Chinese homegrown cosmetics brands typically started with a mass-market product — usually one without much technological content, with both the product and its name suspect of knocking off a big-name brand; they then built their distribution around the "regional offices" established across the country, pushing goods into third- and fourth-tier cities, where the terminals of the older, declining direct-sales channels often became their own points of sale.

Kans's approach was largely the same.

Cosmetics companies at this stage are usually small but highly profitable, which favors primitive capital accumulation. By 2005, Kans claimed it was already bringing in 100 million yuan in sales.

![Image from [Nuomi](http://www.nuomi.com/deal/19v2kvks.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607174126Sswxthz4lMIU3bjC.jpg-w600)

This momentum also allowed Kans, a company born in Shanghai, to reconsider the first-tier-city market. [As an article in Cosmetics Business Online pointed out](http://www.cbo.cn/bencandy.php?id=3049), one of Kans's goals in 2009 was to use department-store counters to lift its brand image and escape low-price competition; at the time, it opened counters and standalone stores in department stores such as Shanghai's Super Brand Mall.

But it's hard to call that strategy a success — if the TV shopping market hadn't opened a new opportunity for Kans at the same time, it might well have retreated once again to lower-tier markets. In 2012, Chicmax CEO Lü Yixiong stated plainly: "Kans cannot compete head-on with products in tier-one and tier-two markets," and "the merchants in tier-three and tier-four markets have to hold Kans up so it can get ashore, and only then can it climb into tier-one and tier-two markets."

Tier-one and tier-two markets remained occupied by foreign brands and venerable domestic names like Shanghai Jahwa. "Move the battlefield to tier-three and tier-four cities — there are more opportunities there," argued Zhou Ying, Chicmax's director of media and public relations, in an interview with QDaily.

![Image from [Baidu Tieba](http://tieba.baidu.com/p/3402324161)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607174126MJoXew4CmYacILA3.jpg-w600)

It's worth noting that even around 2010, when the cosmetics industry had begun reviving the concept of "homegrown national brands," Kans was rarely to be found among the homegrown brands that drew media attention — at the time it was still a small company nobody had heard of.

## In the Three Years After 2012, Kans Became a Marketing Case Study

Entering the TV shopping market gave Kans more points of difference from domestic brands of similar caliber and trajectory.

In a sense, this was an upgraded version of the tier-three and tier-four market. The advantages were obvious: no stores needed, a direct-sales concept, promotion and distribution rolled into one, and a ten-plus-minute program [costing only a few thousand yuan](http://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/20420355)... But this low-cost channel also doomed it to exist only on the TV shopping channels of remote regions. Not to mention that the average age of its audience was far removed from the "20 to 40" range the company now emphasizes.

But there's no denying that TV shopping made Kans "richer."

By the time people noticed the brand, Kans was already bound up with the image of Lin Chi-ling. It then began showing up frequently as a sponsor of variety shows. Its retail sales for 2013 through 2015 were 1.8 billion, 3.1 billion, and 9 billion yuan, respectively. Over the same period, ad spending soared from 150 million yuan in 2012 to 550 million, 1 billion, and 1.3 billion yuan. In 2016, its advertising budget reached 1.3 billion yuan — one-seventh of the previous year's retail sales.

![Kans spokesperson Lin Chi-ling, image from [ccb.com](http://buy.ccb.com/products/pd_0000113160.jhtml)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607174127uvH6UGBn04eFNTJE.jpg-w600)

Buying advertising and being "good at" advertising are two different things.

Kans's biggest single marketing outlay came in October 2014, when it spent 500 million yuan to title-sponsor Jiangsu TV's *If You Are the One*. The hefty fee earned it the reputation of "the biggest single ad deal in Chinese advertising history," but more important, it pushed attention toward the Kans brand to an all-time peak — in the first week after the title sponsorship began, Kans's Baidu Index surpassed Pechoin's for the first time.

In Kans's brand history, none of its ad spends were small change: it title-sponsored Jiangsu TV's *If You Are the One* twice, for 230 million and 500 million yuan; title-sponsored Tianjin TV's *Only You* for 55 million yuan; title-sponsored Dragon TV's *《背着青春去旅行之花样爷爷》 (Traveling Back to Youth: Grandpas Over Flowers)* for 58 million yuan; paid 19.6 million yuan for ad slots on Dragon TV's *Chinese Idol*; and sponsored Dragon TV's *《女神的新衣》 (The Goddess's New Clothes)* for 18 million yuan...

![Image from [5588.tv](http://www.5588.tv/marketplan/20140528100942000.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607174127YhKCXJp5kjx8MBcS.jpg-w600)

![Image from [Baidu Tieba](http://tieba.baidu.com/p/3160903411)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607174127btICBSrHAu5RdUDn.jpg-w600)

In a way, title-sponsoring variety shows has an "either/or" quality to it. Brands pay for title sponsorship partly to actively fight for consumers' attention, but partly also, in a disguised form, to reduce competitors' exposure. After all, there are only so many variety shows that can capture young people's attention in one concentrated place — and once you understand that, you also understand why homegrown cosmetics brands like Pechoin scrambled to title-sponsor *If You Are the One* as well.

This fits the judgment of Chicmax CEO Lü Yixiong, who once said: "Advertising doesn't necessarily make you a brand, but without advertising, it's hard to become a brand."

This value system also won consensus inside Chicmax. Zhou Ying, Chicmax's director of media and public relations, told QDaily: "We're a fiercely competitive company with a wolf mentality; we have to maintain a certain level of buzz, and (advertising) also shows we have the confidence to build the brand well."

These ads reached at least one Chinese young woman whose Weibo handle is Cherry. The 19-year-old college student, who studies in Hunan, is a consumer of Kans hand cream; she first learned of Kans from TV commercials. "Advertising coverage is very important. Once a brand has visibility, it makes the shortlist when you're choosing; if you've never heard of it, you definitely won't buy it."

## Kans May Understand Its Consumers Better Than They Understand Themselves

First, one thing needs clearing up: who exactly are Kans's consumers?

In Feng Chen's description, Kans's consumers are "people striving for a better life." Feng Chen is Kans's director of brand marketing, and she repeatedly stressed to us that these consumers span all regions and occupations. What they share, she said, is being "a very distinctive segment of Chinese consumers who reach the state they hope for and idealize through their own hard work" — and, as you know, they are supposed to fall between 20 and 40 years old.

Of course it isn't so. The Cherrys of the world — Kans's mainstream consumers — get a much blunter description from a Watsons promoter at Shanghai's Global Harbor mall: "People who buy Kans generally don't have high spending power."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016060717412815qefhr34O9vkgcJ.jpg-w600)

Li Wanwei attends college in Guangzhou, and her first verdict on Kans was "great value for money." That conclusion originally came from a beauty blogger she follows on Weibo, who had once done an ingredient analysis of a Kans whitening product. Li Wanwei trusted the basis behind that analysis, and on the strength of it bought her first Kans product.

The skincare brands she also uses include Shiseido and Sulwhasoo — both positioned far above Kans's 60-to-150-yuan price range. Li Wanwei admits she generally only buys mid-size samples of these big brands: "I'm still a student, so I don't spend too much money on skincare."

But even so, she already counts as a heavy skincare buyer among her peers — her roommates "basically never buy the expensive stuff." "Expensive" here generally means products priced above 100 yuan apiece.

In fact, Kans is targeting precisely consumers like Li Wanwei: they have just entered college, have begun consciously looking after their skin, but temporarily can't afford higher-priced products; these people "have higher expectations for life" — but for now, compared with international brands whose products easily run into the hundreds or thousands of yuan, they are still more willing to try lower-priced brands.

![A brand popular with college women, image from [kimiss.com](http://9night.kimiss.com/thread-1384182-1-1.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607174129WRvfz3US9FYpbo51.jpg-w600)

Without the interlude of WeChat selling's sudden boom in 2014 and equally sudden bust in 2015 — in 2014, Kans's WeChat-selling operation averaged monthly sales of 100 million yuan, and it projected that full-year WeChat sales [would top 1.5 billion yuan](http://www.iheima.com/project/2014/1210/148269.shtml); but once 2015 arrived, it was [accused of suspected pyramid selling](http://finance.sina.com.cn/chanjing/gsnews/20150530/020722304464.shtml), and that same year it fell out with Weimob over licensing issues, [ending with the partnership dissolved](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzAwMTI4MTYzMg==&mid=210752533&idx=1&sn=2f8e6a9ab4be93a7574f9241ae4a32d1&scene=1&srcid=0406WAgLBZ8A7ddRKnYgRg16&key=710a5d99946419d93b25ed4cba1bc3a1ab88f22c61fdc4f2737d1dc92e1197d5ab9999439aaf5d2bcb90d0da1094154c&ascene=0&uin=ODI5OTc5NTIw&devicetype=iMac+MacBookPro8%2C1+OSX+OSX+10.11.3+build(15D21)&version=11020201&pass_ticket=jAM9k6Rtc9CucGvee7sd%2BH0eytKQL6dUfjl11PvbhhCAZPu64od%2B1kxnb4wpBjYN) — Chen Yuxin, the head of Kans's WeChat-selling operation, claimed at the time that it was "about to launch a new WeChat-selling platform," but the "cliff-like" collapse of WeChat selling as a whole drained that hope of all life: this is, in the end, still a story about who its consumers really are.

We asked people in first-tier cities, and few around them mentioned Kans; nor did they have any sense of its claim that it had 20,000 WeChat sales agents at its peak. In fact, whatever changes occurred, the market where WeChat selling existed was the market Kans had consistently served all along.

This story — from who the consumers really are to a brand's comeback — could have been told better. Unfortunately, for a company of such massive scale, Kans still has real weaknesses.

## To Talk About Its Weaknesses, We Have to Talk About the Product

Rather than the infamous "pyramid selling," WeChat selling is better described as an online version of early Avon-style direct selling. One can only hope that the mass-channel nature of WeChat marketing and the whiff of "pyramid selling" will sober Kans up a bit.

But the possibility of it getting "played out" is always there. Take "Han," for instance.

Its success was built almost entirely on that ambiguous character "Han."

Tang Xiaov, who moonlights as a daigou shopper for Korean cosmetics, fields customer inquiries about Kans every month. "Too many people think Kans is a Korean brand," she told QDaily. "Lots of young women see the 'Han' in the name and assume it's Korean." Misunderstandings like this still live on in Baidu Zhidao.

Kans's official explanation, according to Feng Chen, is that "it's called Kans because the founder's surname is Han. (Although) some people associate it with Korea, we are a thoroughly homegrown brand."

But Kans barely lifts a finger to correct such bias. And who would? The Korean Wave is in full swing. Korean entertainment and Korean idols alike have created new market demand — look at the whole chain of consumption, from sneakers to coats to gradient-lip makeup, almost all of it driven by Korean drama stars. A connection this tenuous only helps the brand.

In terms of product mix, apart from Red BB and the Mega Hydration line, which appear constantly in TV commercials, the rest of Kans's products have little presence. Feng Chen revealed to us that hit products like the Mega Hydration series account for nearly 40% of Kans's total sales.

Red BB launched in 2012 and did ride the BB-cream wave in the market. At the time, Missha's BB cream was priced very close to Kans's, and had already spread by word of mouth on the internet; but given its limited reach and sales scale, it never made much of a breakthrough in market share. That became Kans's opportunity to overtake it.

"Chinese consumers are becoming increasingly professional, and increasingly clear about what they need," said Feng Chen.

True enough — except that logically, consumption is the combined result of one's horizons and one's spending power. By aiming its heavy promotion at young people with lower spending power, Kans is by definition boosting its own sales base.

Kans said it was not in a position to disclose to us the proportion of R&D spending to sales, but noted that its parent company Chicmax has already set up two companies this year, in Japan and South Korea, with the aim of attracting outstanding research talent from Japan and Korea.

The move comes a little late. After taking the hit from the WeChat-selling bust, if Kans no longer has sufficiently massive marketing outlays, it will find that the market has no place for its products. It was never a company driven by product innovation in the first place.

![Image from [58pic.com](http://www.58pic.com/shiliangtu/15617185.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607174129ThrmfG17M8QHARdU.jpg-w600)

Kans's first ten years saw it walk, in full, every road a Chinese homegrown cosmetics company has walked; in the three years that followed, despite nearly losing its way when WeChat selling crashed, it still secured its position — and this may yet become a story of a comeback into a true first-tier brand. This is intimately tied to the "increasingly professional" demand Feng Chen describes; it's just that this is the fundamental test of whether a company can do things well: Kans itself needs to get professional first.
