The "10,000 hours" theory holds that a person only acquires mature skills through extensive practice and accumulation. In the final installment of our internet celebrity series, we explain how designers in this industry are made.

Chen Ya is a fashion designer whose clients are two internet celebrity Taobao stores.

One of the internet celebrities has a "relatively mature white-collar style," which requires Chen Ya to visit Lane Crawford to scout styles for inspiration; the other, a "fresh and airy" type, often sends her running around markets that specialize in Japanese and Korean clothing.

Chen Ya works for a clothing company in Hangzhou. The company originally focused on a make-and-sell-what-you-make business, covering everything from design to production. Since 2015, supplying internet celebrities has become its core business.

The company boss basically goes to Korea once a month, bringing back a few sample garments with novel fabrics and distinctive cuts. Sometimes Chen Ya goes with her to buy samples for reference.

A garment that sells over a thousand units in an internet celebrity store generally goes through this process: style selection – design – pattern making – sample – revisions – production – photoshoot – listing.

Chen Ya handles the first two steps of that process.

### What exactly is "style scouting" looking at?

"Body in the urban-rural fringe, heart at Paris Fashion Week" is how many people tease internet celebrity store designers. But this is not just a job of being glued to an office desk, digging through runway photos and sketching.

Aside from occasionally running to the fabric market in Keqiao, Shaoxing, or scouring fabric exhibitions in Shanghai for the latest materials, the "style scouting" described above has become an important way for Taobao internet celebrity designers to build up their professional skills.

Take the current cycle as an example: most fashion brands released their looks for fall/winter 2017 back in April 2017, and by May, brands including Prada and Chanel had rolled out their 2018 early spring resort collections one after another — all of them a step ahead of the timetable on which internet celebrities put out their designs. The fashion week calendar, originally created for the fashion industry, hands internet celebrities the time gap they need to catch trends fast.

Womenswear trend websites, fashion runways, and even ready-to-wear in shopping malls are all sources of information for designers like Chen Ya. She borrows from big names like Fendi, but also pays attention to newly hot brands like MSGM or Vetements. Still, she draws a clear line on which parts of each brand are worth borrowing: with Fendi, she "mainly looks at its pattern details — the silhouettes are too stiff"; while "Valentino is too ethereal, so it doesn't really suit the mass market. I only reference small elements sometimes."

Beyond the styles that ordinary people care about most, trending colors, color pairings, details, and fabric workmanship are all elements Chen Ya needs to pay attention to during style scouting. She scouts fast: entering a mall store, she first sweeps her eyes over the store's overall color pairing to gauge which colors might be in this year, then zeroes in on the details.

When trying garments on, she also notes whether the cut has changed from previous years and whether the workmanship shows any new highlights.

But Chen Ya's reference sources shift with the seasons. In fall and winter, she follows European and American brands — winter garments have large silhouettes and place few constraints on the wearer's figure. Besides, fall and winter clothing allows more room for variation: shoulder and sleeve details, garment length, fabric splicing methods, and other details are all things she can "customize" for her clients.

Come spring and summer, she mostly uses Japanese, Korean, and even Shenzhen brands as her templates — because European and American summer styles, with their clean lines and overly straight cuts, are "not liked by young women in third- and fourth-tier cities."

For this segment of customers, Chen Ya often needs to nip in the waist of a basic T-shirt slightly, cut a slit in a sleeve, or add a shorter-front, longer-back hem. "That way they feel there's some design sense to it," Shu Ya says.

Applying the right details onto a given style for different customer positions is exactly the core meaning of Chen Ya's work.

### Designing for an internet celebrity with a distinctive style means aiming at the crowd behind her

"I only give suggestions based on experience, but they decide whether to take them in the end," Chen Ya says.

In many clothing companies that supply internet celebrities, the traditional steps of market forecasting and channel analysis have been relatively weakened, replaced by analysis of the fans following an internet celebrity store.

Internet celebrities already have their personas and style plans worked out before they are packaged and presented. Fans treat the internet celebrity as their idealized self — a state of life they "might just reach if they stretch a little." So internet celebrities don't sell a perfect persona; they are simply tagged with a few specific labels.

Some fans purely treat an internet celebrity store as a "buyer's shop" with a distinctive style, offering them affordable references for a personal wardrobe. Once a store offers a garment the fans like, it will very likely land in their favorites list, becoming an effortless option for future clothes shopping.

From the very start, internet celebrity Taobao stores were not known for originality or quality. More people care about buying, at a cheap price, a garment whose style can rival the runway and whose quality passes muster. In fact, in some third- and fourth-tier cities that fast-fashion brands haven't yet reached, internet celebrity stores seem to have more say in "fashion democratization."

An internet celebrity's early positioning has in fact already filtered out the crowd that will buy the clothes, and sketched the consumer profile for people like Chen Ya. As long as she makes sure the styles she designs fit each internet celebrity's own style, she has completed a considerable degree of market forecasting.

For a garment, starting from style selection, the main way she and the internet celebrities communicate is by "tossing a bunch of pictures at each other on WeChat" — the internet celebrities basically only come to the company to talk face-to-face with the designers a few days a month.

She often receives feedback from internet celebrities at one or two in the morning, pointing out where a design needs changes. The internet celebrities also send her clothing pictures, asking her to make similar styles or details.

A few months ago, Chen Ya's company received a request from a "wild" internet celebrity — one signed with no internet celebrity agency and with no fixed supplier. All she asked was to turn the sample garment in the pictures into an actual product — the design step was skipped entirely.

### From the declining Hangzhou school of womenswear to Taobao internet celebrity stores

Chen Ya graduated from Zhejiang Textile & Fashion College (ten years ago it was called "Ningbo Fashion College"), then joined "Qiushui Yiren," one of the representatives of the "Hangzhou school of womenswear." Three years later, she jumped to 3COLOUR, which had 2,000 stores nationwide. Still the same Hangzhou womenswear circle.

The concept of the "Hangzhou school of womenswear" emerged in the mid-1990s. In the early 1990s, China's garment industry gradually shifted from a model of processing supplied materials and exporting for foreign sales to domestic sales. "Mom-and-pop shop" style womenswear stores sprang up in large numbers in coastal cities of the southeast. In Hangzhou, graduates from the fashion departments of universities such as the China Academy of Art, Zhejiang Sci-Tech University, and Zhejiang University of Science and Technology joined in, starting from a few sewing machines and taking the path of entrepreneurship through their own designs.

According to incomplete statistics, at the time there were more than 2,000 womenswear manufacturers in Hangzhou, forming a huge womenswear industry cluster, known in the trade collectively as the "Hangzhou school" of womenswear. Through local wholesale markets such as Sijiqing and Longxiangqiao, they quickly captured the domestic market. These brand companies mainly promoted their products to the market through order fairs held three or four times a year.

Order fairs are the "fashion weeks" of clothing brands and dealers from around the country. To reduce the labor costs of sending salespeople everywhere, traditional clothing brands hold these centralized exhibitions at regular intervals. Buyers examine sample garments, ask prices, and place orders at the fairs, dealing directly with the brand manufacturers.

Basically, before attending each quarter's order fair, a traditional clothing brand company plans out in advance the garment series it will bring to market next quarter, organized by style (trousers, shorts, jackets, shirts, and so on) and divided by style categories such as casual and sportswear.

Around each order fair, Chen Ya would be assigned a few design tasks. But after methodically producing designs according to the number of styles planned by the company, she was also responsible for the display and guidance at the order fair. "I had to work 20 hours a day," she says.

Then the Hangzhou school of womenswear declined; its product styles and production model were both dated, and under the e-commerce onslaught its dealer channels were smashed to pieces. Chen Ya therefore decided to become a second-tier wholesaler — she took a stall in the Sijiqing wholesale market and supplied Taobao store owners.

It was the wrong decision, because what Chen Ya is good at is design, not pulling in customers and selling. Only a few of the garments in the store were her own work; more often, she was overwhelmed by the crowded wholesale market and the chaotic scenes of partner factories sloppily packing orders. In less than a year, Chen Ya retreated to familiar territory and began working for an internet celebrity supplier.

Although she had never had experience making custom clothing for internet celebrities, nearly 10 years of experience made Chen Ya the most senior of the company's six designers, and she even had an assistant.

From an offline brand to a second-tier wholesaler, and then to an internet celebrity store supplier, the focus of Chen Ya's work went through a shift of "order fairs – wholesaler – internet celebrities."

At a brand company, the market-oriented model meant the design department had to study market trends with the sales, planning, and product departments before planning garments.

Once fashion trends were determined, Chen Ya and her colleagues had to spend part of their time brewing up the brand story, and within the framework given by the design director, each was assigned a garment or two to design. After the sample garments were designed, the design department also had to sit down with every department to review the styles and estimate which of them would best cater to mass demand.

Now, Chen Ya revolves around the internet celebrities' demands.

That said, Chen Ya doesn't think she is any different as a designer having gone from a brand company to an internet celebrity store supplier. She has always borrowed in her designs. "Even on European and American runways, big-name designers reference the work of small designers."

### Plagiarism, the "original sin"

Chen Ya says the internet celebrities she knows are far from just pouting and taking selfies in front of the camera. "They also keep an eye on fashion trends themselves — after all, it's their own store."

But the same garment can still be sold by Chen Ya to different clients. Internet celebrities of different styles can occasionally reach this kind of tacit understanding.

The "style" designers talk about is not the clothing style we usually understand, but the basic silhouette of a garment. For a garment with the same silhouette, a designer can change the details, fabric, and even workmanship to give it a completely new look: a simply cut coat with little decoration can be sold to a relatively mature-style internet celebrity store; if its cuffs are made into "lantern sleeves" and a "lettuce-edge" trim is added, it can be sent to a sweet-style internet celebrity; if a piece of mesh or lace is spliced with a woven fabric, it becomes a coat with more distinctive workmanship. This splicing method comes from MSGM. This Italian brand shot to fame last year, characterized by large-scale prints and fabric colliding against fabric. "This new trick catches your eye without feeling jarring — the masses can accept it."

Selling the same style to multiple buyers is a way many clothing companies use to improve efficiency. And internet celebrity stores have even shorter supply cycles. The stocking period has been compressed from "quarters" to "months," so Chen Ya needs higher design efficiency.

"In the old days, before summer had even started, you'd already be preparing the runway garments for the winter order fair." Now, she is designing 20-plus garments that will hit the shelves within the next month or two. When it's more urgent, Chen Ya gets a picture from an internet celebrity and has to send her the sample the very next day.

The samples she designs can, a few days later, travel across oceans with the internet celebrities and appear amid the brilliant weather and scenic spots of the United States, Korea, or France. This is the state of life the internet celebrities need to present.

Rapid knockoffs have become the reason many people accuse internet celebrity store designers of "plagiarism," or dismiss them as mere "style scouts."

Chen Ya doesn't feel moral guilt about the work she does. But she also admits that some internet celebrities, "due to resource constraints," do indeed end up copying originals wholesale.

In most cases, sizeable clothing companies rarely take orders from "wild" internet celebrities, because their sales usually can't meet a large factory's minimums. The latter can only look for smaller workshops to directly imitate styles, making it hard to guarantee fabric quality and workmanship — a model that deepens people's impression of internet celebrity stores as poor-quality and highly repetitive.

Chen Ya's take on this is: "Small orders can't compete on price, and copycat styles have no differentiation. In the long run, that kind of business can't grow big."

She designs in a way she considers restrained. A hoodie she designed this year uses the hugely popular metal ring element from Vetements, with the sleeves connected by several rings. But "I won't use it for summer wear. When creativity is overused it becomes oversaturated and worthless." The trend will probably carry on into fall and winter, though — only the size of the rings and where they are used will change.

"They still end up looking quite different," Chen Ya says.
