**On the one hand, we look back at the stories of these legendary books. More important, of course, is this: why do we have the society we have today? What have we been through? What have we gained and lost? What wealth and what consensus have we accumulated? How did we come to be where we are.**

**This is the 5th book.**

At 49, when many people today are already starting to think about retirement, Shen Changwen began his singular publishing career: director of the SDX editorial office and head of *Dushu* magazine.

Five years later, Shen Changwen published the best-selling work of futurist Alvin Toffler, *The Third Wave*. But then, on the advice of Li Shenzhi — the former deputy director of Xinhua News Agency and a famous "big Rightist" — he decided to "look backward." Li Shenzhi believed that a book like *The Third Wave* was fine, but "too new." That book was said to be the first time Chinese people after the "Cultural Revolution" saw just how far foreign technology had advanced; the concept of the "global village" was popularized by it. Compared with unheard-of things like the "global village" and "electronic mail," "looking backward" was more about "laying a foundation for readers" — spreading the fundamentals of liberal democratic thought.

After reviewing the catalog of the publishing house that Ba Jin had previously translated for and directed, and then the translation catalogs of Shanghai as a whole from the 1930s and 1940s, Shen Changwen settled on Hendrik Willem van Loon's (1882–1944) *Tolerance*. The translators were SDX editors Ze Wei and Jin Cuiwei.

"In this book, the author narrates the history of the development of human thought, advocates freedom of thought, calls for tolerance of dissent, and denounces reactionaries who suppress new ideas," the SDX Joint Publishing editorial department wrote in the book's afterword.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505001812L4UI3PKgalT5dikc.jpg-w600)

*Tolerance* became a product that shook the publishing world; as soon as it came out, it sold, with "500,000 to 600,000 copies printed at once." A book that was later even selected into Chinese high-school textbooks, it now has more than a dozen translations. Wang Yan, a former editorial department director and executive editor of *Dushu*, told QDaily: "*Tolerance* is a mark of civilized bearing. In the history of thought, many people believed they had grasped absolute truth, and it later turned out not to be absolute truth at all. We need to maintain a certain tolerant bearing toward all kinds of different opinions. In fact China has already become a society of plural interests, and plural interests bring pluralism of thought; that kind of unitary political thinking does not suit the situation to come."

Shen Changwen had not anticipated the book's success. Back in 1985 he was far more cautious: "The Cultural Revolution emphasized intolerance; now we were for tolerance — that was in fact criticizing the Cultural Revolution. But of course, we didn't say so explicitly."

Shen Changwen, now 86, sums it up this way: "One very important rule of the 'publishing world' is to make your point and stop there — never spell it out."

Shen Changwen spent most of his life in publishing. At the time, SDX Joint Publishing had not yet been re-established (that happened in 1986) and was subordinate to People's Publishing House, so it was called the "editorial office." China had just emerged from the "ten years of turmoil" of the Cultural Revolution, had only recently adopted the strategic decision of "reform and opening up," and had not yet entered the era of the market economy. Shen Changwen, his SDX publishing house, and *Dushu* magazine happened to catch — and together shape — the tide of thought emancipation and "culture fever" of the 1980s.

"Introducing democratic and liberal thought" became one of the guiding themes of the SDX editorial office's publishing list. In 1986, after *Tolerance* came out, the Austrian writer Stefan Zweig's (1881–1942) *The Right to Heresy* was also published in China. The book's subtitle was "Castellio Against Calvin: A Documented Account," and it tells the story, from more than 500 years ago in the West, of the scholar Sébastien Castellio opposing Calvin, Geneva's supreme ruler, with the courage of "a fly fighting an elephant." Calvin had been a hero of the Reformation, but by then had degenerated into a tyrant.

![Source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505002211kNVsjRiPrWyKnXFO.jpg-w600)

Later, Shen Changwen went on to publish Zweig's *Decisive Moments in History*, *Joseph Fouché: Portrait of a Politician*, and *The World of Yesterday*. The selection of books for translation grew ever more ambitious — "my scheming even extended to units like foreign embassies in China." In this way, Shen Changwen published the Library of America (10 titles), the American Culture Series (27 titles), the German Culture Series (12 titles), the Japanese Culture Series (6 titles), and more, bringing in classic works from countries around the world.

Among them, translations of American classics also came from Hong Kong's 1950s World Today publishing house. The publisher's director was the Hong Kong writer Tung Chiao; its editors and translators included famous writers such as Eileen Chang. Shen Changwen came to know the press through the writer Lo Fu, who had been chief editor of the *New Evening Post*, under Hong Kong's *Ta Kung Pao*, and who had helped Jin Yong and Liang Yusheng write their martial-arts novels — he was called "the midwife of the new school of wuxia fiction" — and who was also close with many Hong Kong writers, including Cao Juren, Ye Lingfeng, and Xi Xi.

Shen Changwen was a Shanghainese who began apprenticing at a Shanghai gold-and-silver jewelry shop at 14; he also once studied radio telegraphy at a Shanghai night school, and his final degree was two years in the reporting program at Shanghai Minzhi Journalism College, from which he did not graduate. When the political campaigns of 1950s China began, he was nothing more than a proofreader at People's Publishing House in Beijing.

But such a background earned him the recognition of being "politically reliable." In the 1960s, Shen Changwen joined the work of translating "imperialist, revisionist, and reactionary materials" under the organization led by the central "anti-revisionism group," and through this came to know many famous intellectuals — Li Shenzhi, Dong Leshan, Lo Fu, Liu Renjing, Feng Yidai, Pan Guangdan, Fei Xiaotong, and others. (Dong Leshan, among others, later translated works such as *Nineteen Eighty-Four* and *The Rise and Fall of the Third Reich*.) At the time, most of these men were "Rightists" and other "black five categories" who had been brought down, and Shen Changwen was one of the very few people permitted to associate with them — and their thought greatly influenced his later work.

![Source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505002334aQ40WRdN3MZj6DyX.jpg-w600)

Later, Shen Changwen compiled these translated books — *Tolerance*, *The Right to Heresy*, *On Love* — into a series called the Culture and Life Translation Series (46 titles), which also included García Márquez's *The Fragrance of Guava*, Milan Kundera's *The Art of the Novel*, Virginia Woolf's *A Room of One's Own*, André Maurois's *Five Great Questions of Life*, and more.

Of course, whether it was *The Third Wave* or *Tolerance*, *The Right to Heresy*, *On Love*, and the rest, the times being what they were, these translated books could not in the end escape the fate of abridgment. What was cut was, for the most part, content opposed to Marx, Engels, Lenin, Stalin, and Mao, as well as "explicit" sexual content.

Still, the impact of these books on Chinese people at the time was already enormous; the new ideas greatly liberated people's thinking. The 1980s are also regarded as China's second enlightenment movement, following the May Fourth New Culture Movement. "The unofficial and semi-official enlightenment movement of the 1980s echoed the thought-emancipation movement within the system and laid the ideological foundation for China's reform and opening up. Without the thought emancipation and enlightenment of the 1980s, today's rapid economic development in China would be unimaginable," scholar Jin Guantao said in an interview with the *Economic Observer*. Jin Guantao was himself one of the important participants in the New Enlightenment movement of the 1980s; the "Toward the Future" series he edited published 74 titles in all and was one of the series with an important enlightenment influence on young people at the time. And among that series' editorial board members were SDX's Dong Xiuyu and Wang Yan.

Clearly, from the moment he took charge of SDX in 1980, Shen Changwen — together with the hundred-plus titles he published, *Dushu* magazine, and SDX Joint Publishing — joined this New Enlightenment movement of the 1980s.

In 1986, the "Culture: China and the World" editorial board — as renowned as the "Toward the Future" series editorial board and the China Culture Academy — published Nietzsche's *The Birth of Tragedy* at SDX Joint Publishing. Thereafter, this "Culture: China and the World" editorial board, headed by Gan Yang, focused on Western humanities and social science, and made up of young scholars, oversaw the publication, over three or four years, of more than a hundred publications: the "Modern Western Academic Library" (35 titles), the "New Knowledge Library" (78 titles), the "Humanities Research Series," and the *Culture: China and the World* journal (5 issues), among others. These included famous works such as *Being and Time*, *Being and Nothingness*, *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, *The Cultural Contradictions of Capitalism*, and *Political Order in Changing Societies*.

"Books like *Being and Time* were enormously hot — the first printing sold 70,000 copies, and *Being and Nothingness* sold 100,000. These books became fashionable at the time; owning them was fashionable, a badge of the young," recalls Dong Xiuyu, who was then deputy editor of *Dushu* magazine and succeeded Shen Changwen as general manager of SDX Joint Publishing in the 1990s.

![First issue of *Dushu* magazine; photo by the reporter at SDX Joint Publishing's Taofen Library](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505004025Yl6gfADpE3dc9aFM.jpg-w600)

*Dushu* magazine, meanwhile, not only published important essays such as "Reading Knows No Forbidden Zones," "The Sun of Man Will Surely Rise," and "Freedom of Publication Is an Important Question," but also ran the writings of a large number of scholars, old and young — Lü Shuxiang, Jin Kemu, Zhang Zhongxing, Shu Wu, Lao Zude, Gu Zhun, Wang Meng, Ye Xiushan, Tu Weiming, Gan Yang, Zhang Rulun, Liu Dong — influencing a cohort of intellectuals and young students, among others. Each issue had a circulation of over 100,000 copies.

Of course, the emergence of these books and essays also owed much to the thought-emancipation movement within the system. At the time, the ideological realm was itself championing a "three leniencies" trend — tolerance, generosity, and relaxation.

Shen Changwen published a best-selling Russian-language book, *On Love* (《情爱论》). Published in 1984 with a print run of 1.2 million copies, it was SDX Joint Publishing's best-selling book of the 1980s. In it, the Bulgarian Kiril Vassilev discusses the views of Marx and Engels on sexuality, arguing that "Marx never denied carnal desire, because carnal desire is the materialist basis of love." For Chinese people who had once spoken only of feelings and never of physical desire, who had come through the era of "taking class struggle as the key link," the impact was undoubtedly enormous.

Besides translated books, Shen Changwen also actively brought in books by scholars from Taiwan, Hong Kong, and overseas Chinese communities — among them Tsai Chih-chung's comics, Jin Yong's martial-arts novels, Lin Yu-sheng's *The Creative Transformation of Chinese Tradition*, and Kwang-chih Chang's *The Chinese Bronze Age*.

![Source: [Kongfz](http://book.kongfz.com/item_pic_14532_612171612/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505013325nY3lJTNaoOuLhzMf.jpg-w600)

To sketch this venerable gentleman's résumé briefly: he served as general manager of SDX Joint Publishing for ten years; he was also chief editor of *Dushu* magazine during its most influential period; and he proposed founding *Sanlian Lifeweek*. He is respectfully known as "Shen Gong" ("Old Shen").

In 1996, Shen Changwen retired from SDX at the age of 65 — but he did not slow down in the least.

He first teamed up with Yu Xiaoqun of Liaoning Education Publishing House, going on to organize the publication of *Panorama* (《万象》) magazine, the New Century Universal Library, the Bookish Delights series, and more. Starting in 2000, Shen Changwen began working with Rex How, joining the Taiwan publishing company Locus Publishing. He ultimately summarized this new editorial life of his in twenty characters: "Eat, drink, and have fun; fall in love; embezzle and steal; sell intelligence; sit around waiting for the 'dough'" (the last phrase punning on the idiom for "sitting around waiting for death").

When we met Shen Changwen at the café on the second floor of Beijing's SDX bookstore (the Art Gallery branch), he looked very much like the caricature Shen Fan (Shen Congwen's granddaughter) had drawn of him: a cheerful old man with a big backpack — only, unlike in the drawing, he wasn't carrying two big bundles of books in his hands. That caricature later became the cover of his memoir *Neither Storm Nor Shine* (《也无风雨也无晴》), vividly capturing the old gentleman's temperament.

"I have to go online every single day," Shen Changwen told QDaily. He not only loves the internet; he also subscribes to at least five newspapers, and "insists on spending 5,000 yuan on magazine subscriptions."

Shen Changwen is also that "knower" (知道分子, his own pun on "intellectual") of his own description. "I hide in my own room, but I seldom 'reflect'; I simply still want to 'know' the outside world. I spend several hours online every day, through which I know what people in the world are doing. 'Knowing' is, for now, the whole of my life," he wrote in his memoir.

**We recorded Shen Changwen's story in his own spoken words, in the hope that you can come to know him more directly.**

**Spoken account | Shen Changwen**

### "Looking backward" revealed the *Tolerance* and *The Right to Heresy* that Chinese society and politics most lacked at the time

*Tolerance* now has more than a dozen translations; that came entirely from an idea of Old Li's (Li Shenzhi): we had to "look backward."

Li Shenzhi was a big Rightist, the most famous Rightist at Xinhua. The higher-ups wanted me to keep watch on him, and instead I became his friend. I listened to him a great deal, especially after reform and opening up; the two of us were very close. So after he died, when his books still couldn't be published on the mainland, I got them published in Hong Kong for him. He was my elder. But when we first met, it was the Party Central Committee that had sent me to be a liaison with these Rightists. That's how I came to know Li Shenzhi and Dong Leshan — both of Xinhua. Li Shenzhi was the deputy director, branded a Rightist. Dong Leshan was director of the foreign department, branded a Rightist. Other people weren't allowed to associate with them; the Propaganda Department designated me as the one who could, because I was politically reliable, of worker-class origins. Many of my ideas came from them, especially after reform and opening up.

(After *The Third Wave* was published in 1983,) Mr. Li came to talk to me. He said, the books you publish are very good, but they're too trendy; Chinese people can't keep up yet. He was a liberal; he said Chinese people had to absorb liberalism at the root, so you should find a way. He proposed a wonderful slogan: "look backward." These are America's newest books; you should translate America's past books. That influenced me enormously. Afterward we rarely did trendy books (the kind we had published before) — they were hard to translate and had few readers, while "look backward" books had greater impact. So we changed course. After the one Toffler book (*The Third Wave*), we put out maybe one or two more, and then stopped, turning our gaze to the past.

With "look backward," I went looking for books — searching and searching, I found a great many, and finally settled on Van Loon. So Van Loon's first book, *Tolerance*, was published. Because we had been through the Cultural Revolution, which emphasized intolerance — now we were for tolerance — that was in fact criticizing the Cultural Revolution. But of course, we didn't say so explicitly. One very important rule of the "publishing world" is to make your point and stop there — never spell it out. So I was very interested in this book, and I found the original. The original was strangely found in America. Then I found an old translation, and in the end it was translated by SDX Joint Publishing's own staff. Both *The Third Wave* and *Tolerance* were translated by SDX's own staff — who would have thought the impact would be so great once they were translated.

![Shen Changwen; source: [CNPubG](http://www.cnpubg.com/news/2016/1230/32396.shtml)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505004826UqafAbnt2pNeXC9g.jpg-w600)

*Tolerance* later even became a middle-school textbook. Everyone was reading it at the time; the impact was enormous. Then I became interested in Van Loon and collected many of his books — *The Story of the Bible*, *The Story of Mankind*, *Van Loon's Geography*, and so on — and kept publishing them. This was an important marker of putting Li Shenzhi's "look backward" into practice, and it achieved enormous influence.

Starting with Van Loon, I desperately looked for books related to Van Loon, and people related to Van Loon, and found another writer — Zweig. First, his thought was very advanced for China; second, his language was plain. So following that thread I found Zweig, and Zweig's subject matter was even more cutting-edge — almost intolerable in China — and so *The Right to Heresy* was published as well. *The Right to Heresy* sold quite well. All of these books were abridged, though not by much. That word — "heresy" — could not have existed during the Cultural Revolution; now, heresy had rights. (These books of Zweig's) were likewise thought emancipation, but their impact was a bit smaller than Van Loon's.

At first I found this man Zweig from among the books translated by Ba Jin and old Shanghai, and later from among the books translated in Taiwan, so I translated quite a few of Zweig's works too. I did four: *Decisive Moments in History*, *The World of Yesterday*, *Joseph Fouché: Portrait of a Politician*, and *The Right to Heresy*. *The Right to Heresy* wasn't translated by SDX's own staff, but it was still privately translated. Because these works had to use public language to convey heretical thought, I was always very cautious. Who was the translator? It was my old master's younger brother and his son from my apprenticeship days — he was called Zhao Zhenyao, and his son was called Zhao Tai'an. Zhao Tai'an is still in America today. At the time, because I needed to make cuts, and to smuggle heretical thought under a presentable name, I had to find someone I knew to do the translating. Otherwise, if he had reported me, I couldn't have taken it.

![Source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705050050250mio6zClxnLeJOVu.jpg-w600)

### *On Love*, which discusses Marx and Engels's views on sex, became SDX's best-selling book of the 1980s

(In 1983, the SDX editorial office published *On Love*, with a print run of 1.2 million copies — a huge best-seller.) This book was very special. It was a Russian-language book, and the author was Bulgarian. A gentleman at the Central Compilation and Translation Bureau had a copy at hand and showed it to me; later I found a copy myself. Because after reform and opening up, some books about sexology had already begun appearing in China. All those sexology reports were quite thick. Some of our authors, and the organs above, felt these books were not quite respectable. (So) at first I had my misgivings. But later I looked into it and found that before 1949, SDX had talked quite a lot about questions of sex. So I grew bolder and brought the book out in translation.

What was good about this book? It discussed Marx and Engels's views on questions of sex. We Communists at the time believed that questions of sex could not discuss the physical body, only feelings. But Marx and Engels held that this was idealism; materialism should discuss the physical body. So he used this viewpoint, and cited a great deal of material. Where did that material come from? From the letters Marx and Engels wrote back then, all collected in volume 10 of their collected works. Their collected works are so thick — all translated by the Compilation and Translation Bureau. But, very strangely, the explicit passages about sex in these two men's letters had never been translated; they were in French. The two of them wrote their letters in French. So, fortunately, I had the bureau translate this book — they had a way to translate all of it. Of course there was some abridgment, but not much.

*On Love* had an enormous impact. With *The Third Wave* you could say it was for internal distribution, but who would dare call Marx and Engels "internal distribution" — Marx and Engels on sex! Of course I cut some things. Who was the editor responsible for the cuts? Wu Bin. I simply told Wu Bin: the explicit passages don't come out. Who would have thought that after the cuts, once it was published, the impact would be so great. I changed the title of *On Love* myself; I made it more elegant. The original title (*On the Question of Love*) was too vulgar. This was SDX's best-selling book of the 1980s.

### Translation resources from the "anti-imperialist, anti-revisionism group" led to the enduring "Culture and Life Translation Series"

(I) put the translated books together, and (that became the Culture and Life Translation Series). Many of them were things already translated before 1949 in Shanghai and in Taiwan. At the time I did several things: I took the books that Ba Jin had previously directed the translation of, and Taiwan's as well, brought them all in, selected them, and reprinted them. It also started with Van Loon — I realized this was a great treasure. These books liberated thought, and they were readable.

Why did we publish translated books? At the time, SDX was probably the publisher in China that paid the most attention to translations. The reason was probably, first, that translated books were easier to handle — easier to talk about. And another reason: I personally had a special connection to translated books. Why? After the Cultural Revolution, during the anti-revisionism period, I was transferred to the anti-imperialist, anti-revisionism group, where I worked as its secretary. What was my task? Because I was of worker origins, yet was said to know foreign languages — and said to know many of them. I did indeed know many foreign languages, but only the articles and prepositions of those languages; beyond that I knew nothing. So in front of those Propaganda Department leaders, I could say — they'd take a book, and I'd say, this is Romanian, it's called such-and-such, I can read it, when in fact I couldn't understand a word of the content. But anyway, because I was of worker origins, I got promoted there.

The work I did there was very strange. The job given me from above was specifically to find problematic people to translate books — to have someone politically reliable like me do the finding. So I got to know many problematic people who later became great figures. People like Dong Leshan, Feng Yidai. The biggest figure I managed was Liu Renjing, a delegate to the First Congress of the Chinese Communist Party, and it was designated that I alone could maintain contact with him. Because I was politically reliable, I kept in contact with him — and what did I find him to translate? Trotsky's works. Because he knew Trotsky and could translate. Every month he was allotted 90 yuan; I paid him, and he gave me 15,000 characters.

So by the time I was running SDX Joint Publishing, I had a special interest in translated books, and all the former translators had been rehabilitated — Pan Guangdan, Fei Xiaotong, all of them had been in that situation back then — so my work was very convenient. And why the Compilation and Translation Bureau? When I did secret work, I actually lived inside the bureau. I lived together with the leftists in the bureau, because the Central Compilation and Translation Bureau was trusted by the Party Central Committee. I was inside; I could speak Russian, nothing else. I was on very good terms with the bureau. That's the whole story — so there were an awful lot of translated books.

![Translator Dong Leshan; source: [Renminbao](https://m.renminbao.com/rmb/articles/2017/2/20/65080m.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505005603YJDdb74SBUhvKfIi.jpg-w600)

### "I'm of merchant stock — how could I pass up anything that makes money": how the strange book *Dianetics* came to be published

My relations with the American embassy grew increasingly close because of all this. The American embassy was thrilled to see me publishing these things. They wanted to subsidize me, to give me money. But I had made inquiries: their money could not be accepted.

But I'm of merchant stock — how could I pass up anything that makes money? I quickly found a way. My scheme was this: every time I published their books — the market versions were all paperbacks — I'd give them 1,000 hardcover copies, sold to them at American market prices, and they'd give me several thousand US dollars; in the end it was about the same as if they'd given me the money directly. And where did my hardcover editions come from? They used the paperback interior sheets with a hardcover binding stuck on; the interior was unchanged. When printing, I'd instruct them to print extra interior sheets for me. Even now they display those hardcover books — there's a display room on the east side. That way I still made quite a bit of money off them, and I'm rather proud of it.

It wasn't just the "American Culture Series," but also the "Library of America." Later I found the Germans and did the "German Culture Series." Later I wanted to do a British culture series — very strange, the British ignored me. I wore out my shoes running to the British embassy; they ignored me, and I never learned why. The Germans did engage with me; my German isn't good, but they even hosted me in Germany several times, and that's how that set came out. I made the most money from the Americans, and the books were thick, each one a fat volume. I was very pleased, and to tell the truth, I made quite a lot. Because they probably knew too that this was subsidizing me, but nominally it was called buying books — a Special Edition.

The "Library of America" had the greatest impact; America's historical documents were all in it, one big fat volume. And another reason I made money: besides their purchases, I didn't pay any royalties or the like. Why? Besides the manuscripts I commissioned myself, most came from books World Today had already published. That publishing house was set up in Hong Kong after 1949 by a group of pro-American intellectuals. Tung Chiao was one of its leaders. Who were its editors? I'll tell you: Eileen Chang, among others. So its books were excellent. Eileen Chang's books I later published too — all free of charge.

![Source: [Kongfz](http://book.kongfz.com/item_pic_13534_478836909/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505010455uCjqSkPWvF3gKT0s.jpg-w600)

That way, Tung Chiao and I also became very close. Roughly every week or two I'd go to Hong Kong to talk with them, and talk with the American embassy here. These past couple of days I'm writing a reminiscence of Lo Fu — Old Lo helped me a great deal. Old Lo was very strange: we were old friends, but I wasn't allowed to call him "Old Lo" — it had to be "Old Fu." He lives in Zhongguancun, and I often transferred between buses all over town to go see him.

*Dianetics: The Modern Science of Mental Health* (《戴尼提自我心理调节技术》, 1988) was a major move of mine at the time. Back then, under the Propaganda Department there was a Foreign Political and Academic Works Office, and that office brought me in — I counted as a secretary of sorts. Through this I learned that Comrade Deng Xiaoping and other central leading comrades very much supported the American side's publishing sponsor for this book. So I was very interested. And when I talked with them, they were willing to come to me to do promotion — in one go they sent a dozen-plus people to China, all at their expense.

When selling the book, their American girls all wore miniskirts and stood at bookstore entrances, singing and promoting — the impact was huge. But before long, America's *Reader's Digest* published an article calling it pseudoscience. So Shanghai translated the article and attacked us. At that point I stopped. There was no political content at all; it was entirely about psychology.

### "Taiwan surpasses us culturally," so he published Tsai Chih-chung's comics and authorized editions of Jin Yong

I like running a briefcase publishing house — everything is in my head. When I see a good article, I don't show my hand; later I slowly arrange a book for the author. Turn it into an editorial board, have to propose plans and guidelines — I can't do that; the moment you do it, things get complicated. So my method was a small unit, just me hopping up and down, people thinking I'd gone mad — when in fact there was a guiding idea behind it, a guiding idea I dared not speak aloud, because saying it might have gotten me criticized. Because I've learned from class struggle that what is right today gets criticized tomorrow — so I always said it in other words. (As for the guiding idea,) of course I believed in two men: one was Chen Yuan, one was Li Shenzhi. In fact it was liberalism.

That's why, when I talk about Taiwan unification and cross-strait questions to this day, I still hold this view. Even now, I still believe Taiwan surpasses us culturally. Back then it was because the Cultural Revolution was even more extreme; now, however many years have passed since the Cultural Revolution, things are still managed too strictly. They have many fine things there, so I very much hope for cross-strait unification — we must learn from Taiwan and communicate with it.

I also got on particularly well in Taiwan. Why? I understand old China; I talk with them the old-China way, and it has always worked — the new-China way doesn't work. I'm a product of old China. At the time it was even more pronounced — why? Taiwan's publishing people back then were mostly book dealers from Fourth Avenue in pre-1949 Shanghai, and I got along with them even better, so I had especially good relations with Linking Publishing, San Min Book, China Times Publishing, and the rest.

![Shen Changwen; source: [Sohu](http://cul.sohu.com/20150326/n410350774.shtml)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170505011711k76tEHWcKL9Qxiqh.jpg-w600)

After I retired, I mainly worked with one Taiwan publisher — Rex How. I have a special affection for Taiwan, because I feel its cultural resources are comparatively rich. Whether for works or translations, for many of my books I was willing to look to them first. Tsai Chih-chung was the same. Before Tsai Chih-chung, I looked in many places, including Japan and Hong Kong, and they all felt too Western. Tsai Chih-chung is thoroughly Chinese. Tsai Chih-chung's comics speak of Chinese classics, Chinese canonical texts — Hong Kong and America can't do that. Tsai Chih-chung and I are good friends now. He no longer lives in Taiwan; he lives in Hangzhou, and I've been to his home several times — we hit it off.

So even now I still hold this view: Taiwan is even more Chinese than we are. We're still like me, influenced by the Soviet Union, but Taiwan has handled the question of national essence very well — I admire that — so I'm very willing to seek out Taiwan's books. That's how Tsai Chih-chung was found, and it felt right. Chinese people appreciate him; he understands Chinese things. What he draws now is still Chinese.

(In 1993, SDX published nearly 40 Tsai titles in one go.) This had a big effect on my finances, because his royalties all stayed with me, uncollected. So by the time I retired, there was said to be 4 million yuan. I gave all that money to the staff to buy dormitory housing in Fangzhuang. When the staff bought their dorms, how much was the housing per square meter? Eighteen yuan per square meter. What would it cost now? So on the surface I didn't take a cent of his money; but with inflation, those several million yuan sitting with me all that time — I only handed it over when I retired — I'm deeply moved. He is an authentic Chinese — even more authentic than me.

![Source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2017050502383517m4X2iyB8905EHx.jpg-w600)

Because at the time, if I wanted to publish other comics there were problems: Japan's and Hong Kong's all preached the West, and China's Ding Cong was all political attacks on America, while Tsai Chih-chung purely promoted Chinese thought. Strangely, even I, a Chinese, didn't know those Chinese ideas of his back then. Chinese people hold such views! Only after seeing his comics did I understand. His classics weren't just the Four Books and Five Classics; many were folk literature — I admire that. This man is truly strange; sometimes nowadays he draws calculus and such into comics — an eccentric.

Jin Yong was introduced to me by Lo Fu; in January 1989 I made a special trip to Hong Kong to see him. Later Dong Xiuyu took over my job, and she went on to publish Jin Yong's books. I wanted to publish them at the time, but because... it couldn't be done, so it was shelved. It waited until Dong Xiuyu's tenure to come out. But the introduction at the start was Lo Fu's. Jin Yong was very straightforward — introduced by Lo Fu, and he had boundless admiration for Lo Fu.
