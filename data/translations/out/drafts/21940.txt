@@QD_BODY@@
Why is it so hard to spot disabled people on the streets of China? And why are the ones we do see almost always beggars? Why are baby-care rooms so rare in China? Why does Chinese society seem so cold and cruel compared with the friendly accessibility that developed countries offer their most vulnerable?

When you get off the subway, have you ever noticed the two steel rails mounted on the wall beside the stairs? Do you know what the mirror installed in an elevator is for? — For quite a few people, one could even ask: do you know why some of the floor tiles on sidewalks have raised longitudinal ridges?

The steel rails beside the subway stairs are actually part of an accessibility device that makes it easier for wheelchair users to get down to the platform. Ahead of the 2008 Olympics in Beijing and the World Expo in Shanghai, every subway line was fitted with accessibility facilities. Except they are almost never used in everyday life, because while a disabled person can finally travel from street level down to the platform without barriers, the stretch from their home to the subway entrance is riddled with obstacles.

![**Subway stations do not build accessibility devices at every entrance, so able-bodied helpers remain an indispensable support for disabled people getting around**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115955iA5O4cl6JUr1zn9H.jpg-w600)

——No wonder first-time visitors to China from developed countries are often astonished at how rarely they see disabled people on the streets; some have even inferred that this must be a consequence of China's distinctive family-planning policy. The only place in the world where disabled people are even harder to spot than on China's streets is probably North Korea.

## **Mirrors, Japanese — but No Braille**

Why do elevators have such large mirrors? Most people assume they are there so passengers can check their appearance. In fact, the mirror is installed so that wheelchair users can see the floor-indicator lights reflected in it without having to turn around.

Before the reform and opening-up era, China's tiny number of elevators had no mirrors — and of course no Braille or arrival chimes either. When China opened its doors and selectively absorbed advanced foreign technology, the mirror was kept, while Braille, chimes, handrails and the second, lower-mounted control panel were, in the vast majority of cases, left behind.

![**The Braille markings that should be on every elevator**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115955BPGUmCIFXZLwSnal.jpg-w600)

Similarly, on China's Hexie ("Harmony") trains you can still occasionally spot Japanese characters inadvertently left behind — something critics cite to question the purity of China's homegrown intellectual property. But compared with Japan's Shinkansen, China's high-speed rail has no baby-care rooms and no Braille (which is perhaps understandable, since some of the posted notices on board aren't even grammatical, concise Chinese) — proof enough that it is genuinely a Chinese original.

![**Japanese on the Hexie train, and a "please press switch to close door" notice that defies Chinese usage**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115956cPVKQBHmuWGZja3h.jpg-w600)

China seems to be a country that is not friendly to disabled people going out. "Death trap" tactile paving (guide paths for the blind) is everywhere in major cities; guide dogs and other working dogs are still barred, along with pet dogs, from many public places and public transport systems — in fact, China has no guide dogs at all; the "beep-beep" tones that accompany traffic lights have failed to spread across the country along with the latest pop hits; buses rarely have ramps for wheelchair boarding; staircases and thresholds in public buildings become "door gods" barring disabled people from entering; and most public toilets do not have a dedicated accessible stall.

Walk past the wall of TVs outside an electronics shop and most programs come with neither sign language nor subtitles. Apart from a handful of large shopping malls in first-tier cities like Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou and Shenzhen, commercial venues simply never provide baby-care rooms. Before the 1980s, China had few public toilets even outside the walls of work-unit compounds (**see Elephant Guild's 《The Gap in "Toilet Civilization,"》** reply with the keyword "**Gap**" to read it).

![**Building tactile guide paths with Chinese characteristics, making disabled people even more disabled**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115956EoIgVWJ82bZMixG0.jpg-w600)

## Is China's Share of Disabled People Just Too Low?

According to the China Disabled Persons' Federation, as of 2010 China had more than 80 million people with various disabilities, roughly 6% of the national population. Of these, over 25 million were severely disabled. Yet we rarely see disabled people out on the streets, let alone in workplaces or other social activities.

Of course, in another sense the Chinese are actually the people most likely to see disabled people: every city's public spaces feature disabled people begging in various forms, who trade on cruelty and suffering as the selling points of their "work," to the point that a Chinese person's first association with disability is a beggar in ragged clothes holding up a porcelain bowl.

![**Riding the subway in Beijing, after the first impression of crowding, what leaves the deepest impression on people is the groups of disabled beggars**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115957pihNqx7v3zkRMsB9.jpg-w600)

If the marginalization of disabled people — the largest vulnerable group — and the ignoring of their existence could perhaps be explained by prevailing attitudes in Chinese society today, then the comprehensive neglect of young mothers nursing infants, in an era when children are universally doted upon, can only show that mainstream Chinese society is, as a rule, cruel and indifferent to others.

## **How a Savage Society Became Civilized**

By contrast, developed countries today display services and warmth toward vulnerable groups that are entirely different from China's. Whether in publicly funded facilities or venues built with private commercial capital, friendliness and accessibility for the vulnerable are maximized — and compared with the handful of suffering-and-inspiration stories about disabled people on Chinese variety shows, disabled people in developed countries often live far more brilliant, confident lives.

That said, the societies of Europe, America, Japan, Hong Kong and Taiwan were not "civilized societies" from the start, and prejudice against and neglect of disabled people and other vulnerable groups is hardly a "Chinese specialty."

In the Western developed countries, history includes not only legislation discriminating against the disabled but also, before World War II, state campaigns of mass persecution — even extermination — of disabled people under the banner of "eugenics." The most infamous case in history is Nazi Germany.

The United States was little better. In 1907, Indiana led the way by enacting an Eugenics Law, which prohibited people with mental illness from marrying and having children; similar laws then swept through 27 other U.S. states. In 1927, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the sterilization of Carrie S. Buck by the state of Virginia on grounds of intellectual disability did not violate the U.S. Constitution. To this day, that ruling has never been overturned. Ironically, before being sterilized, Buck had given birth to a baby girl, and the girl's school records show that this daughter of a disabled woman had perfectly normal intelligence.

![**Carrie S. Buck with John Bell, the eugenicist who operated on her**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115957WmHtOh7VQr6e28uK.jpg-w600)

In the United States, it was not until 1974 that the "Ugly Laws," which completely banned people with physical disabilities from appearing in public, were terminated nationwide. Compared with China, the West's past behavior toward disabled people and vulnerable groups can fully be called a model savage society.

Western society's efforts to integrate disabled people began with the resettlement of wounded veterans after the two world wars. But what truly turned society gradually into one friendlier to disabled people was the product of the postwar "civil rights movement."

The "civil rights movement" is often reduced to the fight for women's rights and racial equality; in fact, the disability rights movement was an important component as well. From the 1950s to the 1970s, the Independent Living movement for disability rights aimed to free disabled people from nursing homes and asylums and let them live independent, normal social lives with the help of certain accessibility facilities.

Over the half-century since the disability rights movement began, disabled people's living radius has expanded enormously. More importantly, the movement made people realize that what limits disabled individuals' personal development and contribution to society is not their physical or mental disability, but the isolation, ignorance, discrimination and poverty brought about by social marginalization.

Sentiment alone, clearly, is not enough to make a genuinely friendly society. Before 1958, calls arose in the West for accessibility features in public and private buildings. In 1961, the first U.S. building standard containing accessibility requirements was published. But a survey of 3,000 architects conducted the same year showed that only 35% of respondents knew of the accessibility construction standard. Requirements for accessibility in public buildings did not gain broad acceptance until 1976.

![**A public bus in San Diego, USA, 1981**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115957bHoEUjD4WywQnsSJ.jpg-w600)

Fighting for the rights of disabled people of course required disabled people speaking up for themselves: Dr. Fred Fay, founder of the American Disabled Association; the renowned lawyer Harriet McBryde Johnson; Max Starkloff, one of the founders of the U.S. National Council on Independent Living; and Marca Bristo, formerly of the National Institute on Disability — all became disabled for various reasons. Usually highly educated, they promoted society's understanding of the living conditions of disabled people through rallies, speeches, writing and other means, beyond achieving their own struggles. It was precisely the collective efforts of so many disabled people that brought the disability rights movement its eventual victory.

Judging from Western history, without the civil rights movement, there would be no civilized countries.

![**An accessible children's playground in a British community, where every child can find happiness**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115958yloMQEHdZxUTNbcw.jpg-w600)

## **The Silent 80 Million**

China has of course not been without progress — except that China's progress has been pushed entirely and unilaterally by the authorities.

The change in terminology alone reflects Chinese society's civilizational progress. In the past, Chinese people called disabled people by all manner of "-zi" slurs: cripple, blindie, deafo, idiot... and ultimately lumped them together as "the crippled and useless" (canfei) — even official documents used that word. In the early 1980s, after the Cultural Revolution, as the authorities vigorously promoted the "Five Stresses, Four Beauties and Three Loves" campaign, one item under "stressing civility" was teaching children to change the way they referred to disabled people: "blindie" became "visually impaired person," "idiot" became "intellectually disabled person," and "the crippled and useless" became "disabled person."

The leaders of the China Disabled Persons' Federation also contributed somewhat to the rights of disabled people. For instance, CDPF Chairperson Zhang Haidi enabled some disabled people capable of driving motor vehicles on their own to obtain driver's licenses.

But the overwhelming majority of China's disabled population seems exceptionally silent. In an era when social injustice and stereotypes are constantly being challenged, they still live lives cut off from the world. Neither disabled people nor the general public see anything wrong with them quietly staying at home. Nor do people notice that the vast majority of Chinese disabled people and their families are still struggling at the edge of subsistence.

![**Mobility scooters meant to make travel easier for disabled people have instead become unlicensed "black taxis" for illegal operators touting for business**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201606071159596Z5KqCINxjm7DiMp.jpg-w600)

The restrictions they face in education, employment and other areas are also taken for granted: CDPF data shows that as of 2008, China had only 1,640 special-education schools and 46,000 special-education teachers — completely out of proportion to the country's huge disabled population. The shortage of basic educational resources means the school-enrollment rate for school-age disabled children in China is far lower than that of non-disabled children. The illiteracy rate among disabled people is as high as 43.29% (2006).

Beyond basic education, the physical examination required for the national college entrance exam is another threshold keeping disabled people out of higher education. Before 2014, blind people simply had no chance to take the college entrance exam at all, and the only majors open to them were massage and musical performance. According to CDPF data, between 2008 and 2012, only 35,000 disabled people enrolled in regular institutions of higher education. By comparison, in 2003 alone, China had more than 7 million university graduates.

Insufficient education inevitably leads to difficulties in employment and income for disabled people. Although the Law on the Protection of Disabled Persons, enacted in 1990, explicitly stipulates that employers may not discriminate against job seekers on grounds of disability, the authorities themselves did not set a good example on anti-discrimination: a 2007 survey by China University of Political Science and Law showed that 40.9% of respondents believed discrimination against disabled people existed in the selection of civil servants.

Data from 2014 showed that of China's 80 million disabled people, only 21 million (4 million urban residents and 17 million rural residents) were successfully employed. The 2006 national sample survey of disabled people showed an employment rate of only about 31% for disabled people; the 2013 China Labor Market Report released by Beijing Normal University showed that between 2007 and 2012, the employment rate for disabled people was also only around 45%.

![**March 2014, Mercedes-Benz Fashion Week in Moscow: models with disabilities walk the runway**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201606071159595EnJSmr3sb6LBvZp.jpg-w600)

Employment difficulties inevitably bring economic poverty. According to a report by Disabled Peoples' International, most of China's disabled people still live in poverty. CDPF data for 2012 showed that even under the discounted 2,300-yuan poverty line (79% of the UN's $1.25-per-day standard), roughly 40% of China's rural disabled population still lived below the poverty line.

Has China simply not become wealthy enough yet to care about disabled people?

In 2014, the per-capita real GDP of economically developed regions such as Tianjin, Beijing, Shanghai and Jiangsu had already surpassed that of the United States in the mid-1950s. And in places like Beijing and Shanghai, average years of schooling and the share of the population with higher education already far exceed those of the United States at the start of its civil rights movement.

![**According to reports, Tianjin did not introduce its first accessible buses for disabled people until April 2014**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607115959mOxtIMpYLcQlDBbT.jpg-w600)

In stark contrast, because of the various restrictions they face in education, employment and other areas, Chinese disabled people lag far behind every vulnerable group in the mid-1950s United States in per-capita income and educational attainment.

Given China's national conditions, the well-being of all Chinese people rests mainly on the government's omnipotence and omniscience, and disabled people are no exception. We can also see Chinese society seemingly becoming friendlier toward disabled people and vulnerable groups — for instance, China issued its first regulation on accessibility design standards as early as 1989, 《Design Code for Urban Roads and Buildings Accessible to Disabled Persons (Trial)》, and the latest edition of the 《Accessibility Design Code》 took effect in September 2012.

But we all know a simple fact of life: events like the Olympics and the World Expo have done a great deal to elevate Chinese society's state of civilization. So if Chinese society is to broadly shed its cruel and indifferent face, perhaps the most effective method is for every city to host a round of Olympics or World Expo.
