A game you may still know nothing about: the Super Bowl. What people call "America's Spring Festival Gala" is just the tip of its iceberg.

At 7:30 on the morning of Chinese New Year's Day — 6:30 p.m. Eastern Time on February 7 — San Francisco, California's second-largest city, hosted the 50th Super Bowl, with the Carolina Panthers taking on the Denver Broncos.

Legend has it that nine months after the Super Bowl ends, the winning team's city sees a baby boom, and those babies are called Super Bowl Babies. Think about it this way: a Super Bowl Baby born at the first Super Bowl in 1967 would turn exactly 50 this year.

A sport that "grows up with Americans" — that would be no exaggeration as a description of the Super Bowl. The Super Bowl is the annual championship game of the National Football League (hereafter NFL). By some accounts, at this time every year Americans spontaneously throw parties averaging 17 people; on this day, Americans consume 1.25 billion chicken wings, and toilet water use during halftime reaches 350 million gallons — the equivalent of seven minutes of flow over Niagara Falls.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132359s15x2REzlDiYHFrd.jpg-w600)

Ticketing site aztickets listed ticket prices for different sections of the 50th Super Bowl: the red zone is VIP seating, nearly impossible to buy through ordinary channels; the pink zone is the most expensive, currently averaging around $10,000, and even the green zone at the very top of the stands costs more than 50 times a stand ticket to the Champions League final. According to AC Nielsen, Super Bowl ratings can reach 40–60%; 130 million Americans watched the 2008 Super Bowl — more than the number who voted in that year's presidential election.

Americans love it so much that the all-time rankings of U.S. TV viewership are essentially an internal contest among past Super Bowls. Last year's Super Bowl once again set the annual viewership record, while second and third place went to — the post-game and pre-game shows of the Super Bowl.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132400oZ1GfnNtgPMAJ0ac.jpg-w600)

With that many viewers, sponsors are of course happy to throw money at it. To get airtime on the NFL, Nike pays $220 million a year — roughly 1/15 of its annual marketing budget. Whether a brand can afford to splash out on a 30-second Super Bowl ad has become a symbol of its financial muscle and status.

The Super Bowl is the most profitable sporting event in the world, bar none. You may often hear the phrase "America's Spring Festival Gala," but that is just the tip of its iceberg.

## How Did the Super Bowl Shape American Sports Culture?

Why do so many Americans adore football, a sport that looks so violent? In the view of Liu Zhuxi, a veteran NFL fan, it is precisely because of the violence that football places particular emphasis on restraint — you have to master that to play it well — making it a very modern sport.

This claim is borne out by the history of American football's rule changes. In the mid-19th century, this sport derived from British soccer had no established rules yet. Unlike baseball, American football was a sport of the college elite, and student deaths in games in the early 20th century drove rule reform.

If you know modern football, a 60-minute game is cut into four quarters, each with two-minute pauses; this precise sense of time gives football a distinctly modern-industrial feel. It breaks the offense down into discrete tasks, and each of the 11 players on a team has a strictly defined position and playbook. Lewis Mumford wrote in *Technics and Civilization*: "The clock, as it towered over the town, almost symbolized urban existence." Football, as a form of entertainment then enjoyed by the middle class, was stamped with exactly this trait.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016060713240057sit8fBUEKYHOlo.jpg-w600)

A useful comparison is the NBA, which you may know better. Looking back at how the NBA became popular in the United States, it is not hard to see that this was a realm dominated by Black Americans and subculture — the period from the 1960s to the 1970s, when racial conflict in America reached its peak, was also when the NBA gradually rose to become a top sports league.

Relying on superb physical gifts, Black Americans who gained status in the sports world kept injecting subcultures — originally in a position of weakness — into America's mainstream culture, ultimately producing the current state of affairs in which Black culture is fairly well accepted by mainstream American society.

Football, by contrast, was tagged with "elite" attributes almost from the start. And every competitive sport has its camps; football has its own "distinctly American conflicts." Take neighboring Oklahoma and Texas: the first settlers who opened up Oklahoma were called "Sooners," and the game between the two states' college teams became known as the "Red River Shootout." The same goes for Missouri and Kansas, where, because of the historical friction between slave states and free states, their teams' games carry extra火药味.

When TV broadcasting arrived, you could watch pre-game documentaries recounting the two teams' "grudges and entanglements" — the NFL invented many such variety-show tricks in its history. And the Super Bowl ultimately became a successful commercial event precisely because of the rise of television as a medium.

## How Did the Super Bowl Change TV Broadcasting?

Television was what truly brought football to the masses. *Time* magazine wrote in 1973: "Without cameras, American football would have remained confined to the universities and the notebooks of historians. Because of television, the game became an important part of the entertainment industry."

After World War II, television became a fixture in American households. By 1955, half of American homes had a TV set. Large numbers of veterans returned to the U.S., got married, and had children; television answered their entertainment needs, and broadcasting sports games seemed a good choice. In 1955, the NFL signed its first contract with NBC (the National Broadcasting Company).

But in the early 1950s, football was far from as popular as baseball, the national pastime, and the NFL's broadcast revenue lagged well behind college football. Which brings us to the NFL's seventh commissioner, Pete Rozelle.

After his predecessor died unexpectedly, the 33-year-old Pete Rozelle took office. He saw the huge potential of television — the "new media" of its day — to help the NFL grow. In his second year in office, he persuaded Congress to pass the Sports Broadcasting Act of 1961, a law crucial to the league's development: it allowed the league to sell broadcast rights for the entire season as a package on behalf of all teams, with proceeds shared equally among every team, free from antitrust law constraints.

![Left: the Sports Broadcasting Act of 1961; Right: Pete Rozelle on the cover of Sports Illustrated's 1963 Sportsman of the Year issue](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132401kTIKPjAXoWrsm7Ja.jpg-w600)

The tricky part was that with every team getting the same money, a big club like the New York Giants was losing out. Pete's successful lobbying got the act passed, and the upside was that it gave small-market teams a chance to develop — anyone could win — thereby guaranteeing the games' watchability. In the 2014–2015 season, for example, the NFL made $9.5 billion, and it had to split broadcast rights revenue, national sponsorships, one-third of ticket income, and roughly 10% of merchandise sales equally among its 32 teams — which is why many people say the NFL is a communist league.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201606071324010sNoQWUj2lzMx893.jpg-w600)

The "AFL" ([American Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Football_League), hereafter AFL) also emerged at this time. In 1960, the newly founded AFL began working with ABC — not yet a national network — on TV broadcasts. To compete with the NFL, the AFL adopted many TV-friendly measures, such as designing brighter-colored jerseys and movable camera positions on the field. Before long, though (in 1966), the AFL merged with the NFL to form the new NFL. CBS and NBC acquired four years of Super Bowl broadcast rights for $9.5 million.

To make broadcasts more watchable, the NFL also successively adopted many innovative TV technologies — instant replay, improved on-field audio, and so on. For example, to help new fans understand the sport's rules, they needed more intuitive on-screen graphics, and so the Graphic System was born (also known as 1st & Ten) — it debuted on ESPN broadcasts in 1988, displaying a special-effects marker at kickoff showing the down-and-distance on screen.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201606071324024AGqPkbTsWV7ujcp.jpg-w600)

Beyond its masculinity, football on TV has served in large part as a pivot of American party culture, especially at family gatherings. Compared with broadcasts of other sports, football games are highly fragmented, because they are interspersed with large numbers of ads, plus substitutions, lining up, and other preparation procedures. As a viewer, your attention can stay in a scattered, relaxed state while always ready to snap back to the game. This state is perfect for chatting with friends and family while still feeling engaged.

That is how baseball lost to football. Besides dragging its feet on TV promotion, baseball's televisual product was far less eye-catching than football's trench warfare; its thrills and tension were hard to convey to viewers through the screen. Football, meanwhile, had long since shed its identity as a pure sport and become an entertainment program for Americans. It is far more fun to watch than to play.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132403slK5XwQ4ZpG1MCiS.jpg-w600)

During this period, America's two major public networks, ABC and FOX, benefited enormously; acquiring NFL broadcast rights has always been regarded as the watershed in FOX's growth. Today, NFL broadcast rights are so expensive that no single network can swallow them alone. Last season, beyond the broadcast fees of networks like FOX, CBS, and NBC, the league's earlier four-year, $4 billion deal with DirecTV drove NFL revenue up 21% to $9.5 billion.

If we treat the NFL as a company (in fact it has always been a non-profit institution), this league of 32 teams is worth about $46 billion — roughly near the world's top 100. But by revenue, it would rank only somewhere past 200th. This illustrates the advantage of the NFL's "limited-edition product": a season of just five months each year, capped by the championship game of the Super Bowl, which like a TV series finale has a commercial value assessed by *Forbes* at around $470 million — higher than the Olympics and the World Cup combined.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132403GYhBE5Q0awmFXTto.jpg-w600)

The NFL's core product is the game itself, and from the game it has grown four revenue streams: ticket sales, broadcast rights, advertising sponsorships, and merchandise sales. To hit those numbers, it must keep attracting fans, so Americans have turned the NFL into an extremely entertainment-driven sports business — a quality most pronounced in the Super Bowl.

Consider this set of figures: players on the losing Super Bowl team get $46,000 each; players on the winning team earn $92,000 for one game; but the halftime show's performers are paid exactly zero.

![Bruno Mars at the 2014 halftime show](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201606071324040O2Pv6lMAcj9id8V.jpg-w600)

And these performers are all superstars — last year it was Katy Perry, the year before that Bruno Mars; virtually every giant of American pop music has taken this stage: Madonna, U2, and at the 1993 game where the Dallas Cowboys beat the Buffalo Bills, the King of Pop, Michael Jackson, performed the classic *Heal the World* surrounded by 3,500 teenagers. Bruno Mars's album sales rose 164% after his halftime show, and three of his singles immediately climbed the Billboard charts.

That is why pop superstars are willing to perform for free. The Super Bowl halftime show is hailed as a global barometer of entertainment and a symbol of status — the same appeal the Spring Festival Gala holds for Chinese performers, except that they don't lip-sync. This year's performers are Coldplay, whose lead singer said he would strike while the iron is hot by launching a U.S. tour right after, converting the halftime show's influence into revenue.

![This year's headliners: Coldplay, Beyoncé, and Bruno Mars](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132404DoFcCnUbqWmA6XJ3.jpg-w600)

![Michael Jackson's halftime show performance in 1993](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132405gihIOBzp4vsma2WF.jpg-w600)

"The halftime show was Pete Rozelle's idea," former NFL executive Jim Steeg once said. "He understood that we couldn't count on every Super Bowl being a nail-biter, 20–19. You have to be prepared for 55–17."

In the 1970s and 80s, few companies had the capacity to stage a halftime show at a sporting event, and Disney was a natural choice. After bringing in this new operator, they made the halftime show spectacular. Performing on a stadium lawn with a tight halftime window, every show was a test of props, sound, lighting, pyrotechnics, choreography, and stage design — they had to build or tear down a complete stage within 25 minutes and still make it dazzling. The NFL, though, didn't seem worried about anything going wrong, because Disney treated staging the halftime show as a way to promote its theme parks: look, they can create a show like this, so they can certainly create a dreamland for kids.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132405WZ5dK4e3kHTJguQw.jpg-w600)

## This Show Also Rewrote the Advertising Industry

Another reflection of the Super Bowl's commercial value is its advertising revenue. Super Bowl ad revenue in 2015 reached $350 million. According to a *Forbes* report (link), roughly 50% of viewers watch the Super Bowl for the ads.

The Super Bowl's夸张 ratings on game day make brands willing to pour in enormous ad budgets. This year, a 30-second Super Bowl ad sold for $5 million, about 32.85 million yuan — and with brands now tending to buy 60 seconds to tell a longer story, that figure doubles. AB InBev ran a total of 210 seconds of ads last year; at 2014 prices, it spent $31.5 million on the Super Bowl.

Ad Age, the advertising trade authority, has tracked Super Bowl ads with special coverage every year since 2004. In the ensuing social-media era, most sponsoring brands release 15-second teasers of their Super Bowl ads months in advance to build buzz online.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160607132406MDPbL6zEAlWsr7ev.jpg-w600)

In the old days, a 60-second ad was an oddity. Apple's Macintosh ad at the 1984 Super Bowl was the pioneer that broke this rule; its thought-provoking content defied the prevailing comedic style of advertising. It became one of the most classic ads in advertising history, and the 60-second ad went on to become a standard TVC length.

If the Super Bowl halftime show is the entertainment barometer, then Super Bowl ads are the barometer of American society and economy.

In 2000, a total of 16 companies bearing .com logos appeared on the Super Bowl. The next year that number crashed to three. The most representative was pets.com, which went bankrupt within ten months amid the bursting of the dot-com bubble.

Then came the subprime mortgage crisis. Ameriquest, then America's largest subprime lender, bought Super Bowl ads in 2005 and 2006. But in 2007, it was acquired by Citibank.

Ad prices keep climbing, and every year on the eve of the game, major media outlets rehash the argument over "whether the money is worth it." But as Ad Age reporter Steinberg once told the media, the Super Bowl is "one of the last bastions of stability." Those 2,100 seconds of ad time determine whether a big company is truly rich, whether it is a responsible national brand, and whether a small brand can become a new upstart — last year's Mexican avocados, and the 2014 Goldie Blox toys, caught fire just that way.

Looking back over 50 years of Super Bowl history, it is a microcosm of the extreme entertainment-ization of American sports business. Within it, there is a TV medium that grew with technological development, and partner brands eager to join the business, all making merry together — add half of the American people, and you get this one day of carnival every year.

From a businessman's perspective, you can learn from the league's operation how to keep teams healthy and let everyone get a slice of the pie. From a sentimental perspective, the Super Bowl is an American dream, folding the organizational building of the industrial age, the fervor of the westward movement, and even the hatred within a people into one carnival. So it is far more than what a "Spring Festival Gala" can sum up.
