For most of his life, Fan Meizhong has been pushing back against so-called mainstream values.

**Growing up in a remote village in Neijiang, Sichuan, Fan Meizhong was the first student from his village ever admitted to Peking University, and he carried boundless glory and hope — yet he has always been a marginal figure.**

**He believed he was fit to teach only exceptionally gifted students and deserved a post at a key middle school in Chengdu. Instead, he ended up at a private school in Dujiangyan staffed mostly with foreign teachers, facing every day a group of students he called "industry trash, total dregs."**

**For a long time, Fan Meizhong believed life had no meaning at all, living in constant anxiety — and that included 2008.**

**At 2:28 p.m. on May 12, he abandoned his students and became the first person in the school to flee to the soccer field. Ten days later, Fan Meizhong wrote on his blog *The Moment the Earth Shook — A Firsthand Account of the May 12 Wenchuan Earthquake*《那一刻地动山摇——5·12汶川地震亲历记》: "At a life-and-death moment like that, I would consider sacrificing myself only for my daughter. Anyone else — even my mother — I would not look after, not under those circumstances..."**

**This marginal figure was instantly swept into the center of public opinion. The name "Fan Paopao" — "Run-Run Fan" — has been linked to the Sichuan earthquake ever since.**

**Fan Meizhong may seem cold. He challenged the "goodness" and virtue that the authorities of the day were loudly praising, but he believes he in fact represented the power of truth.**

**For most of his life, Fan Meizhong has been pushing back against so-called mainstream values.**

**He loathed the gaokao and opposed the endless exam regime.**

**He spurned special-grade teachers, regarding them as synonymous with stupidity and mediocrity.**

**In the past, he could barely be said to have stirred small ripples in education circles. But this time his defiance collided with the first time China faced a disaster of such magnitude. Grief had nowhere to go, yet not a single grain of sand could be tolerated.**

**Fan Meizhong enraged everyone.**

**When Sichuan people stood in unison shouting "Sichuan will not let the nation down," "I threw a lump of shit on their beautiful, shining image," Fan Meizhong said.**

**Looking back ten years later, Fan Meizhong is convinced he won this fight. More and more people have begun to understand him — once held up as the earthquake's negative example — because the positive examples have themselves "fallen from grace," one after another.**

**The two kinds of people arrived at the same destination by different roads.**

**But judging from the trajectory of his decade, it is hard to say whether he really won.**

**Compared with others who became famous overnight because of the Wenchuan earthquake, the quake seems to have left no "after-effects" on him. Apart from a brief spell of exposure, it created no further opportunities for him. He remains a label that education circles, Sichuan, and middle schools keep at arm's length; even publishing a book met obstacle after obstacle.**

**Fan Meizhong retreated to the small circle he started from. An even smaller one, in fact.**

**In 2015 he quit his job at Guangya School and became a freelancer, parting ways with the education system once and for all.**

**He left Dujiangyan and settled in Chengdu.**

**Chengdu was the "lowest-grade city" he could accept: far from the political center of Beijing, yet at the center of China's peripheral cities — satisfying his wish to be both on the margins and proudly so.**

**That was his habitual comfort zone.**

**In March 2018, through the introduction of one of his students, we got in touch with him. At 9:30 on the morning of March 9, we met in a café near Sansheng Township in Chengdu. He wore his signature black-framed glasses and a gray sweater, and was reading a literary classic.**

**At 45, he was energetic and spoke without restraint.**

"These days very few media outlets come to me, because those that wanted to interview me already have, and the shelf life of news hotspots is getting shorter and shorter. It was strange that they interviewed me for so long back then, because smartphones weren't yet widespread in 2008. If they had been, news would have updated much faster, and the story wouldn't have stayed hot for so long, been stir-fried for so long. Later it was just that every year around May 12, when everyone did retrospectives, they'd come do an interview with me.

My story ran all the way into the second half of 2008 — mainly those three months from May to August 8, the Olympics — and then it tapered off. Which was perfectly normal. People shouldn't fixate on a news event or a news figure for that long in the first place, because everything worth digging up had been dug up — my life story, my values, my views. I also took the opportunity to fully express my various values. I don't want this society's values to always be shaped by the mainstream class and the powerful class — I can shape this society's values too, right? We can't live according to the values of a small minority; we can also express our values and become part of what shapes this society's values.

At first I didn't want to give interviews. If I hadn't, the whole thing would have passed. But then I thought, no — there were two reasons. First, I had to state my views, because you know how people's views of a historical event settle (my episode had already become a historical event). If you don't fully express yourself at the time to overturn many of those views, the event will set in people's minds as time passes. By the time you try to overturn it, people won't be interested anymore. So you have to take advantage of the moment and fully articulate yourself while people still care about the matter, so they hear your values and your views on these questions.

Second, it was also a chance to lay out my values on all fronts, because I have my own views on Chinese society, on life, on ethics, on politics, on everything. If you write academic-style articles in normal times, nobody reads them. Since everyone was willing to read at that moment, I gave my full lecture, hoping to have some influence on others. Later, out of this consideration, I cooperated fully with the media.

You know, most Chinese people — our culture has a terrible side, though of course our nation also has a very great side; that's a fact. The terrible side is that our nation doesn't much value truth, which is terribly, terribly bad. Why don't we value truth? One cause is the authoritarian society; another is that we love to emphasize 'goodness' — and especially once you single-mindedly emphasize 'goodness,' it stifles people's impulse to speak the truth, because truth conflicts with 'goodness,' with the bright and beautiful.

But without truth, there is no light, no beauty, no 'goodness'

, and people tend to forget this, because they so love to stress 'goodness'

, so any statement of truth may be accused of being 'not good' — in that situation, people are very unwilling to speak the truth. This is a big problem for the Chinese nation, and it's why I wrote that piece in 2008 — it was also connected to my own thinking. I don't like how Chinese people live so falsely. You're clearly like this in daily life, your real thoughts are like this — so why, facing a camera or writing an article, can't you voice what's truly inside you? A teacher complains a lot in normal life, but when the camera rolls says, 'We are engineers of the human soul; we must care for children with all our love' — while privately teachers often grumble, 'We only get paid 3,000 to 5,000 yuan.' Why can't you say that in front of the camera?"

**Regarding Fan Meizhong's remarks, a Ministry of Education spokesperson said in 2008: "We can do without nobility, but we cannot permit shamelessness."**

**The Party secretary of Peking University's history department commented: "Peking University is ashamed to have such a student. If he were expelled, we would approve."**

**The principal of Guangya School once informed Fan Meizhong that, because of his remarks, the Dujiangyan education bureau and the city government had decided that all donations received by Dujiangyan would be accepted centrally by the Ministry of Education — except that none would go to Guangya School.**

"Chengdu's Huaxi Metropolis Daily would definitely never interview me. Because in the eyes of the Sichuan education authorities, the press and publishing authorities, and the propaganda department, my episode is a disgrace for Sichuan. They have this beautiful, shining image of Sichuan to protect — and me? I feel like I threw a lump of shit on that beautiful, shining image. They feel embarrassed, very displeased — that's their mentality. It's very strange, because they never care about truth.

My incident was never such a terrible thing, but because of their values they'd consider it a terrible thing. In fact my incident was precisely a glory for Sichuan, because when everyone is lying, one truthful utterance is the precondition for moving toward the good. But they don't see it that way — their heads are already messed up — so Sichuan media would absolutely never interview me. And if they did interview me, I would definitely say things they wouldn't want published.

The rebellious impulse behind my writing that piece was precisely my disgust with that kind of propaganda. I just wanted you to have something not-so-beautiful. Actually I knew those things were fake, but I also didn't know what the real things were.

I only knew the real situation at Guangya School, not the real situation anywhere else, and I couldn't just shoot my mouth off, so I could only talk about myself. At least I'm not so heroic and glorious — I tore open that curtain of beauty and light. At the time, people had lost their reason; under the enormous shock of the disaster, people lose their reason.

But ten years on, with everyone calmer, raising the matter again may make people more clear-headed, willing to dig into many questions — like why did so many buildings collapse? Was responsibility pursued? At this point everyone has calmed down; if you go back to promoting sacrifice and touching stories, nobody pays attention. What calm requires is calm reflection. It certainly doesn't want the matter raised again, because raising it would dig up many problems that won't be let go — they fooled everyone back then, but it's not so easy to fool people now.

People's attitude toward me has changed a lot. Of course some people will never change. You know, a person's values are formed mainly before high school, or at the latest before the end of college. After college, a person's values are very hard to change, so expecting everyone's views to be changed by this incident is impossible. But a considerable number of people's views did change, which is quite rare.

Actually, when I went on Phoenix TV in June 2008, that already made many people change their minds. Maybe a year or two later, once everyone calmed down, quite a few more changed. For example, later the news about Tan Qianqiu turned out to be fabricated. He was a positive exemplar of the earthquake; I was a negative exemplar at the time. Tan Qianqiu was a teacher at Dongqi Middle School in Hanwang Town, Mianzhu. They dug open the earthquake site, found several students beneath his body, and said he had heroically saved them — but that was pure fabrication. When they dug the site open, he might well not have been protecting students at all; the building may simply have collapsed on top of him, pressing him onto the students. There is no evidence he deliberately shielded them, but later journalists interpreted it as a heroic rescue.

Later the old principal of Dongqi Middle School wrote a letter exposing this, and the Southern Metropolis Daily reported it. Then the word came down: no more writing about this, because the old principal also had personal motives — his personal relationship with Tan Qianqiu was very bad. The story was forcefully suppressed after it came out, but many people already knew. And Lin Hao — later some unsavory things about him came out too.

Later, in the Yushu earthquake and other earthquakes, there were also cases of teachers running out. Lots of people still cursed them, but it couldn't stir much of a reaction, because my incident came first — everyone's tolerance and understanding had grown; people see the problem more clearly."

![In June 2008, Fan Meizhong defended himself on Phoenix TV's Tiger Talk](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180510233024oqxHfrk3W1tVU9S4.jpg-w600)

**Fan Meizhong speaks very fast. You don't need to encourage him to talk; the only challenge is interrupting him at the right moment.**

**When Fan Meizhong speaks, he loves using the word "of course." "Of course" usually fits two situations: one, after stating a fact, as a concession or supplementary qualification; the second, to strengthen the tone and assert something plain for all to see.**

**Of course, Fan Meizhong's usage is usually the second kind — and it is often used to describe his own excellence.**

**"My self-appraisal was very high back then. Of course, it's still very high now."**

**"My moral level far exceeds that of ordinary people — and of course, I still think I far exceed ordinary people."**

**"I don't think my overall state is as high as imagined; but of course, objectively speaking, I'm still much better than most people."**

"I don't think the earthquake changed my life trajectory much. Without the earthquake I would still have resigned from Guangya School, because Guangya's students are, overall, industry trash — basically total dregs; you can't even teach a class.

Although Guangya School gave me privileges — for example, if students wouldn't listen, I could simply refuse to teach, and my pay was relatively high among the Chinese teachers — for a person, taking pay and not teaching makes me feel I don't deserve the job. I took the money and did nothing.

Second, I work not just for the money but for achievement, for the joy of work. People need the joy of work; it is the main source of human happiness. So I definitely would have resigned, and then I would take care of my child and do academic research, because the main direction of my life is lecturing and studying the classics. I'm a genius at academic research — not just ordinarily capable, a genius — definitely stronger than the vast majority of Harvard professors. I could do better than them with 1% of the effort; I'm really not bragging. Take philosophy, literary studies — with just a bit of effort I'd do better than them. I can brag about this; I can brag like this in front of anyone.

Precisely because of this, I know what I should do, so I definitely would have resigned, because Guangya School drains my energy. But right now I basically have no time to read. My wife's home school is nearby; I drove them there today. At noon my kid needs to come home for a nap, and from when she wakes up until she goes to bed at night I have to watch her. I only have two hours each morning; I often can't even finish an article, let alone read. When the child is older, my main energy will go into academic research. So my life trajectory is the same either way.

I'm not someone swayed by the external environment; I'm someone who listens to the voice inside. In one sense I'm an ordinary person, but in another sense I'm not. My inner self is quite strong; I don't much care about others' comments and opinions.

So with or without the earthquake my life trajectory wouldn't change — but my views did change.

First, the earthquake made me rate China's intellectual circles, its thought circles, and the quality of ordinary Chinese people even lower. I didn't expect them to be that stupid. Ironically, that put me in a better state, because the reason my state used to be so bad was that I thought China was terrible and I was desperate to change it, yet couldn't. After this happened, I became more at peace: if China's intellectual circles, its ordinary people, its thought circles were all that stupid about my incident, then my heart wasn't so anxious anymore. You realize that Chinese people's state of awareness and capacity for thought is even worse than you imagined — and you gain a clearer understanding of the long-term nature of China's transformation. After that, I actually felt more at peace.

Second, my understanding of myself. Before 2008, my self-appraisal was also very high — and of course, it's still very high now. Not just talent — for example, that I'm a genius at academic research. I even felt my moral level far exceeded ordinary people — and of course, I still think I far exceed ordinary people.

But after 2008 I discovered that, in a critical moment, in a sense everyone returns to an amoral state — not an immoral one, but the most instinctive, most authentic state of a human being. In that sense, I'm not a bit more elevated than ordinary people. It's not that I'm worse than ordinary people; it's that I originally believed I was far above them — but it turns out I'm not so terrible either.

In other words, when one day everyone is starving and has no food, they'll all grab things — that's the state. I wouldn't be the one saying, 'I have plenty, you all eat, I'll starve.' When there's no food, I'll grab bread like everyone else. Fundamentally, we're all about the same, though in daily life there may be some differences between us.

These two things both made me better. The second gave me a sober self-appraisal, and my anger toward other people diminished. For a long time, my anger at ordinary Chinese people — even intellectuals — was very strong: moral anger. Why are you all so awful? In daily life — take teachers and civil servants — you live inside lies and masks. I felt everyone had some moral problem, and it made me furious.

But later I realized my overall state isn't as high as I imagined. Fundamentally, back at the level of instinct, the differences between people aren't as big as I thought. The more you recognize your own limitations, the more understanding you have of others, and the calmer you become inside."

![Guangya School in Dujiangyan](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180510233421aSkm6u07y9qXi3AZ.JPG-w600)

**In June 2008 there were reports that the education authorities demanded Fan Meizhong's teaching certificate be revoked; Fan Meizhong said in an interview that he had never had a teaching certificate at all.**

**He holds the evaluation standards of the education system in complete contempt.**

"No public school in China will come to invite me now, and I wouldn't take a full-time job either. If I were willing to teach, I'd actually be suited to teach students with high talent. In Chengdu they're mainly at Chengdu No. 7 High School, Chengdu Foreign Languages School, places like that. But if I went to teach there, they largely wouldn't accept it, so I wouldn't go asking — and I never have.

If I went asking, I'd be passive. If I were to teach at a famous school, they would have to come invite me. Only if you come invite me will you recognize my value — I'm very capable. If I went looking myself, they'd treat me as an ordinary teacher, which is meaningless. At a school I would definitely demand certain privileges. These privileges are not special treatment; by privileges I mean not keeping office hours, teaching my way, choosing the teaching content.

At the same time I don't accept you testing students every few days, and I don't accept you judging me by gaokao results — that's the kind of privilege I mean. If I submitted a résumé, I couldn't make these demands, but if you come to me, I can lay them out.

If they came to me on their own initiative, those Chengdu principals would be too impressive. But you should know: don't expect principals of Chinese public schools to be outstanding — they're destined to be mediocre. I have no expectations of them.

Even without the earthquake they wouldn't invite me, because they're just a bunch of mediocre principals. The earthquake didn't make my job hunt harder, because finding a job was already somewhat difficult for me — I don't care about the gaokao. In their minds, before and after the earthquake, I was never a good teacher. It's just that before the earthquake I was already well known in online education circles, because I'd written many articles about education — but those had nothing whatsoever to do with their gaokao. And I never enter teaching competitions; I take none of the in-system routes. I don't even have a teaching certificate, let alone being rated a special-grade teacher — while they sometimes care about 'poaching a special-grade teacher.' Special-grade teacher status doesn't represent ability at all.

Overall my life is much better now, because in the internet age I can teach online classes, write articles, receive tips. At my age, I still have some influence in the field — in the circle and even in academia. Adults are willing to listen to me lecture on Zhuangzi and willing to send their kids to hear me. So the road gets wider and wider, and my income gets better and better. Of course it's not like them (Lin Hao, for example) who got so many benefits. My income has nothing to do with the earthquake; it's the natural result of my own level provided to society. I'm not saying I'm rich, but a basically comfortable life is relatively assured."

![Rebuilt downtown Dujiangyan](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180510233912QHGraUDhTBMwZCfb.jpg-w600)

**After the earthquake, 20 provinces and cities across the country paired up to assist the hardest-hit areas of Sichuan; Shanghai's paired city was Dujiangyan. By the end of August 2010, all 117 projects Shanghai had undertaken in Dujiangyan were completed, at a total cost of 8.25 billion yuan.**

**There is a saying that after the earthquake, "the disaster area leapt forward twenty years."**

**In return, from September 8, 2017 to May 12, 2018, Shanghai residents could visit the Dujiangyan scenic area for 15 yuan (regular ticket 90 yuan per person) simply by showing their ID cards.**

"'Twenty years of progress' is a fact in terms of infrastructure, in terms of street construction, but it doesn't mean the earthquake was some beautiful thing. However great the leap in infrastructure, it's only material; it cannot bring back lost lives, cannot soothe the wounds in the hearts of those who lost loved ones. How are people to weigh material progress against the loss of lives, the trade-off between psychic wounds and material progress?

Second, this twenty years of progress was not itself the result of quality development. It was the massive use of our centralized system to forcibly reallocate resources from elsewhere. So it's not the result of normal economic or productive development; it's purely accumulation-type, consumption-type investment.

Taking Shanghai's money to invest in Dujiangyan proves nothing; it only shows something being forcibly taken from one place to another — and the process caused enormous waste.

Economically speaking, Dujiangyan built lots of houses and infrastructure, but Dujiangyan's industrial and commercial development can't keep up with such artificial input. This isn't the result of natural economic expansion, so many houses sit empty. Infrastructure nobody uses, houses nobody lives in.

A considerable share of Dujiangyan's housing sits empty. Some people moved out of the mountains, but those people certainly can't fill this many houses. The resulting waste of resources gets very little attention. Shanghai aided Dujiangyan to the tune of hundreds of billions of yuan — that's Shanghai taxpayers' money, forcibly taken like that, right? This is something people rarely consider. Call it the whole nation as one chessboard if you like, but Shanghai taxpayers' money was taken without their opinion being solicited. And after it was taken, it wasn't used effectively. So we cannot sing the praises of infrastructure progress paid for with such a catastrophe.

By that logic, we could also have one big bombing, level a city entirely, then rebuild — it would definitely be much better than before. Should we praise that? Obviously not — it would kill millions of people. That kind of thinking is clearly absurd.

Second, how are we to understand the claim of 'twenty years of progress'? For a city — for our values — after decades of China's rapid catch-up modernization, an idea has formed that the newer and grander the infrastructure, the better. Do you think Americans believe the more old buildings demolished, the better? Sometimes an old house, a city's natural development — why must it be torn down to build very tall buildings? Are very tall buildings necessarily progress?"

![A panoramic view of Dujiangyan](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180510235153boQExjsMyLgk90r2.jpg-w600)

I now live near Sansheng Township in Chengdu. I don't have a house in Dujiangyan; I never considered Dujiangyan home, because Dujiangyan is too small. Not just because of its small population — its cultural foundation is too poor. In Dujiangyan I usually can't find a single person to have tea and chat with, can't find a single person to have an intellectual exchange with. Chengdu is the lowest-grade city I can live in in China — I don't mean material modernity, I mean spiritual needs. For instance, being able to chat and exchange with people — Chengdu is my lower limit. If I were in Neijiang, Mianyang, Deyang in Sichuan, I couldn't stay. That's my floor.

When I left Guangya in 2015, Dujiangyan's 2.5th Ring Road was already built, built very glossy, all connected to earthquake reconstruction — but it meant nothing to me, because those people still only know how to play mahjong. And what I care about — what they call 'infrastructure leaping forward twenty years' — what about people's ideas, people's quality? Did they advance one iota? So their kind of evaluation is itself very low-end, a very bottom-tier view. You say China's high-speed rail, China's railways are built better than America's — so China must be more developed than America? That's a very bad way of thinking.

I don't deliberately go back to Dujiangyan. My going back isn't about nostalgia; it's just that sometimes friends come to visit, or the kid wants to go to Mount Qingcheng to play, to have tea — not that I taught there for ten years and want to go see Guangya, see Dujiangyan. I have no such thoughts. I have no attachment whatsoever to Dujiangyan or Guangya School.

After the earthquake, I feel everyone became more pragmatic; the hoped-for moral sentimentality, the inflated stuff, grew less and less, including among intellectuals. Before 2008 I was fairly involved in intellectual circles, including attending some events in Chengdu — that utopian kind, utopias pinned on ideas in one's head, is decreasing. That's a good thing. Chengdu used to have mostly Alto cars; now the cars get better and better — ordinary neighborhoods are full of Mercedes and BMWs. I feel people care more and more about their personal lives; life gets better and better.

I think Chengdu has changed a lot in these ten years. Chengdu's general image has always been a city of leisure. But for me personally, Chengdu has two changes. Life in Chengdu has become busier and busier — why? Because China's development moves from east to west. Isn't Chengdu the top of the new first-tier cities? Including the development of south Chengdu — it's actually made the pace of a considerable share of Chengdu people very fast and busy, not as leisurely as before.

Second, a considerable portion of Chengdu people keep playing their mahjong, but some have begun moving toward self-learning, lifelong growth — signing up for classes here and there, taking online courses. I think this is a good thing. As Chinese society grows ever more materially prosperous, after the brief state of having nothing but money, some people begin to search for the meaning of life, to seek spiritual fulfillment and self-growth. This is what I've strongly felt over these ten years. For Chinese society to truly get better, it must be premised on the spiritual improvement of every individual. So just chanting 'China moves toward prosperity, democracy, civility, and harmony' is useless. The growth of each individual, including changes in education, is very slow growth.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180510234146KXqJm2vTHjsewSGZ.jpeg-w600)

**In 2012, the Central Radio and TV University Press published Fan Meizhong's book *Folk Wild Grass*《民间野草》, in which he interprets Lu Xun's *Wild Grass* in 15 essays.**

**It was the only book Fan Meizhong formally published in the past ten years.**

**He began studying Zhuangzi in 2011; Fan Meizhong describes Zhuangzi as the turning point of his life. Although he long ago finished the manuscript on Zhuangzi, he has been unable to publish it "legally." He believes the book deserves far more attention, because the Zhuangzi interpretations on the market are simply not up to par, and he represents "the highest level of Zhuangzi scholarship today."**

"These days, as soon as I print a book, the publisher can't put it out — so I printed two books myself, one on education and one on Zhuangzi.

I very much want these books to reach readers, since I believe I represent the highest level of Zhuangzi scholarship today, and they could help many people. The Zhuangzi studies already out there completely failed to understand him, right? So I'm certainly not convinced, and I want mine to be seen, to expose the fakes.

Some publishers feel my books are a bit niche, but some people really want to publish them — one publishing editor thought I wrote very well and really wanted to publish. Originally there was no risk, because after 2008 I formally published a book and nobody made trouble for the publisher, and nobody made trouble for me. But the leadership of this publisher, pre-estimating possible trouble in case it came — unsure of the propaganda department's attitude toward me — wouldn't take any risk.

Freedom of speech is declining, but what fills me with confidence is that I studied history, and I think this is a countercurrent. History is the tide — as Sun Yat-sen said, 'the tide of history rolls on, mighty and vast; those who ride with heaven prosper, those who defy it perish.' No countercurrent can be stopped. The Qing government back then didn't want to hand over power either, and in the end it fell. Mao Zedong wanted to run the Cultural Revolution forever, on his terms, but when Deng Xiaoping came to power he had to change course — if he hadn't changed it, China would have collapsed. In this respect, historical development advances through the struggle of various forces. The Communist Party loves saying this: the future is bright, the road is winding. Now it's my turn to say it.

I studied history, so I look at the whole long river of history, and I'm very confident. Moreover, China's dependence on openness is already indispensable. The maintenance of Chinese rule depends on the economy, but China's economic development depends on its trade and connections with the world — raw materials, markets, technology. Once it gets too closed and those things are cut off, the economy collapses and rule cannot be maintained.

And with internet technology today, you can always break through the blockade; anything you want to see, you can see. I'm not worried about this at all. Also, the Little Pinks can easily be converted (to our side), but we could never be converted into Little Pinks. When I taught in Hangzhou before, I had students from grade nine to grade ten read Li Shenzhi's 《风雨苍黄五十年》("Fifty Years of Storm and Change") — he was a Communist Party elder who wrote a piece of reflection — and they were converted. Why do I have such power? The power of truth. So it's easy for Little Pinks to convert over, but hard for us to convert into them.

Second, are there really that many Little Pinks online? I doubt it. Some are just wumao — the 50-cent party — who want to speak and put on the appearance of Little Pinks. So I don't think it's that scary. And slightly well-off Chinese often take their kids traveling all over the world, all exposed to things abroad. People are used to every family having a car, vacationing all over the country and the world. In a few years — less than ten — they'll trade up to a better car and a better life. No matter what you do, only an open society can be prosperous, right? A closed society cannot be prosperous. Once you let their material life decline (Little Pinks or not), dissatisfaction arrives. 'I used to drive a Mercedes or BMW; now I can't even drive.' However much of a Little Pink he is, he'll start opposing. I think this is a very key factor."

![Sansheng Flower Township, Chengdu](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180510234558h3QaRTHkFxIfl2Py.jpeg-w600)

**Fan Meizhong's family now lives near Sansheng Township in Jinjiang District, Chengdu — also called "Sansheng Flower Township," the city's main flower-growing area, known for its natural scenery.**

**He lives with his wife and two children in an 80-square-meter apartment, making a living by teaching online classes and tutoring his children in Zhuangzi at home every week. The income is hardly stable, but in Chengdu it guarantees him a life of modest contentment — "not what you'd call rich, but a basically comfortable life," Fan Meizhong said.**

**Material things don't mean much to him, because even if he were penniless, he would still consider himself a cut above others.**

**Sansheng Township is also seen as an "earthly paradise" within Chengdu's urban area.**

"I've only really been in a good state these past few years. My bad state before had nothing to do with the earthquake. I used to be in a very nihilistic, very dark inner state, feeling life was meaningless — and that included 2008; I was still in that state, because my metaphysical questions were unresolved. Even when I was teaching at Guangya School I wasn't in a good state; I just felt there was no point in living.

I think my state changed mainly for two reasons. One is family and children. Although I married in 2006, after my second child was born I watched him every day, and family life had a big influence. The other reason is that I read Zhuangzi through — it completely resolved many of my metaphysical questions.

My earlier inability to answer was a failure to know the truth; speaking as a Christian would, it was not knowing Christ, not knowing God. For me it was being without the Dao. I used to be entirely owned by the self, trapped in self-conceptions and the inner self; the relationship between you and the Dao, with truth, with heaven and earth and the universe, hadn't been reached.

This sounds very abstract, but it's a real life process. Without this inner experience, we're like a butterfly that never breaks out of the cocoon, trapped in a web of its own making. I'm a person with a very strong self-awareness. Self-awareness is a very important stage of human growth, but the self itself closes off and binds the self. The health of the human psyche is built on connection with all things under heaven — say, your relation to others, your relation to nature.

Human beings became independent from nature, yet came from nature; if a person severs the relation with nature, the spirit develops problems. Moreover, humanity as a whole is also a spiritual community; if a person loses a genuine relation with others — not a surface relation (working at the same company makes us an interest community, not a real relation) — the spirit also develops problems. So why do Christians go to church? To become a community under the guidance of the Holy Spirit — healthier, fundamentally walking out of the self.

I've already written some Zhuangzi interpretations. In 2013 I wrote over 100,000 characters, having interpreted only a tenth of it, and then I stopped, because my thinking and my life accumulation were exhausted. Zhuangzi's thought is exceedingly broad and profound; it is the core classic of Chinese culture. From 2013 to now, five years, I still haven't written, so I had to set it down. This isn't something I can continue by reading a few monographs on Zhuangzi; studying these depends on the growth of my soul and my life experience. If my life experience hasn't grown to a certain height, I can't keep writing, so I put it aside.

Five years is certainly not enough. In today's fast-food era, anyone can write an article every day, but for me — I insist on high-quality writing. If you write a poem, you may spend ten years waiting for that poem. Truly great poets spend ten years practicing, experiencing, and thinking for that poem. I dislike this era's fast-food, low-quality tendencies, so I insist on the quality of writing. In my view, writing one book in ten years is not low output at all. Some people write one book in twenty years, and it is truly worth reading, or it remains after the din fades."

![Fan Meizhong studying Zhuangzi](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180510235418Vm6ZGPqlMvO3Uypt.jpg-w600)

These three years since quitting, my main work has been taking care of the kids. Twenty-some days after I resigned, my second child was born.

My wife runs a home school, teaching our eldest at home, busy every day. One of the parents has to take charge; my wife basically never has.

Now when I have time I read a bit and write a bit, but that time is scarce, because the kids take too much energy, and my eldest is ten now — I often take him skating. Occasionally someone invites me out to give a lecture, but not often. Many people want to invite me but don't dare, because lots of people in education circles have long-standing concerns. Even a certain principal who wanted to invite me worried that someone would use it against him. Many people in education circles are still very stupid. Teachers are among the stupidest groups in this society — especially primary and middle school teachers. It's ridiculous.

Normally I give a weekly Zhuangzi lecture — I round up a few kids myself and teach them literary classics at home. And of course I also teach online; in the second half of last year I gave twenty Zhuangzi lectures online. This year they've asked me to lead a reading club, to guide everyone in reading — *A Dream of Red Mansions*, Tang and Song poetry, Lu Xun's works, Milan Kundera's *The Unbearable Lightness of Being*.

I don't feel financial pressure in Chengdu. I think doing what I like doing is pretty good.

Honestly, my income is even a bit higher than at Guangya School. Back at Guangya it was maybe nearly 9,000-plus yuan a month, and maybe 100,000 yuan a year. Now taking on a few kids brings tens of thousands of yuan a semester, plus going out to lecture and teaching online — making this money is easy.

Of course income is a personal secret. At least in Chengdu I live fairly comfortably, because the apartment is bought. But with two kids I may have to upgrade — I'd originally planned on a two-bedroom, two-living-room place, and then surprise, the second child came, and suddenly it doesn't feel big enough.

We now live in a place of 80-plus square meters. In two or three years, my plan is to trade up to a four-bedroom, two-living-room place of about 120 square meters. But it's not a must — at least I have a place to live, and upgrading isn't big pressure. Worst case, I sell this apartment and buy another, which is more than sufficient.

For me, as long as basic living is maintained, it should be very easy.

I have no plans for this year. Probably for the rest of my life I won't take any job — I definitely don't want to do full-time work again, because I've tasted freedom. I won't do the kind of work where you must show up today.

I'm 45 now; my second child is three. When he turns 18, I'll be 60. I had planned to drive through every county in China, combining China's historical geography with a road trip, but after he was born the plan was shelved; I may not finish it before I die, but it's still my plan.

There's also traveling the world — the ruins of the Roman Empire, the Amazon jungle, the North and South Poles — I still need to see those. I haven't been to the United States yet; I should still take a look at American imperialism. Although we know a lot from books, we're not like your generation — few of us went to college, and few have been to America."
