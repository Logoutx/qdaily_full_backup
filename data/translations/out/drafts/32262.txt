Guess how McDull ends up saving the world? (Spoilers ahead)

When *McDull: Rise of the Rice Cooker* (《麦兜·饭宝奇兵》) was 90% complete, Alice Mak phoned animator Wong Ping: "Help!" She simply couldn't crack the outer-space scenes in this McDull film — the outer space she drew never looked right.

In the past, the farthest McDull had ever been was probably Wudang Mountains. He was born in a tiny flat in Tai Kok Tsui, Kowloon, Hong Kong, where he lives with his mother, eats at cha chaan teng diners, occasionally dreams of the Maldives, and has a female friend named May, with whom he wants to twist his way to New Zealand. From the drawing style to the story, it has almost always been the tale of an ordinary Hong Kong underdog. But in this installment, McDull must defend the Earth and find a way to stop a green-furred monster from outer space.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160917031545EA6BW7sCrDwdap9m.png-w600)

[Mak was worried as soon as she got the script.](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzI3MjI4MzgwNA==&mid=2247484181&idx=1&sn=f6af48f0c94bbf9dc1755e9cf1a3b8d8&chksm=eb35a2e6dc422bf0b0521d7c8c19a4b00a0f37fb50b939f08d57a7d5f0ab8c1cddb0dbf94035&scene=1&srcid=0916ux6w49cgQZiXwXAZsJLO&pass_ticket=sY%2B1IL%2BkfUNoVeMIfs9UeDf%2BgSb7LTeDIiWBheuHJVLq%2BMTOBMFBoee7PtLz1QAX#rd) She has serialized the "McDull" comics in *Ming Pao Weekly* since 1991. The first McDull feature film was released in 2001, and she went on to do the artwork for seven McDull films, always inhabiting the character and rendering him just right — the warmth between him and his mother, the way he hides nothing of his true feelings from May, Mr. Chan and the school principal, how he fails again and again, and his understanding of what changes in the world and what never does, like that dialectical ten-thousand-year clock.

In the words of her husband Brian Tse — the screenwriter and director of the McDull films — this is a story about "how a person can avoid distorting himself while still facing a rather distorted world."

But in this installment, "the world" shows up as all of humanity. McDull and the school principal fly off to the United States to build a robot that can fight the monster.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160916224346mulQG2rgDhZvfFH0.jpg-w600)

[Mak doesn't understand it either:](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzI3MjI4MzgwNA==&mid=2247484181&idx=1&sn=f6af48f0c94bbf9dc1755e9cf1a3b8d8&chksm=eb35a2e6dc422bf0b0521d7c8c19a4b00a0f37fb50b939f08d57a7d5f0ab8c1cddb0dbf94035&scene=1&srcid=0916ux6w49cgQZiXwXAZsJLO&pass_ticket=sY%2B1IL%2BkfUNoVeMIfs9UeDf%2BgSb7LTeDIiWBheuHJVLq%2BMTOBMFBoee7PtLz1QAX#rd) what is McDull holding on to? Her guess is that the story turned out this way because her husband, like all boys, wanted to make a monster-fighting story. Still, she knows well that, unlike superhero movies, there will be no villains in McDull's story.

She drew the monster's "poop" pink.

This is the seventh film in the McDull series. Like *McDull, Kung Fu Kindergarten* (《麦兜响当当》), *McDull: The Pork of Music* (《麦兜当当伴我心》) and *McDull: Me & My Mum* (《麦兜·我和妈妈》), it is a mainland–Hong Kong co-production. At a press conference a week before release, the producer, Xinhua Zhanwang, said it believed the film's box office would exceed 300 million yuan — a number far above the 70.58 million yuan record set by the previous McDull co-productions, not to mention the 14.55 million yuan that [the original *My Life as McDull* took in](http://www.qb5200.com/content/2015-12-11/38527.html) on its Hong Kong home release back in the day.

The film was originally slated for the July 22 summer slot, then announced it was moving to the Mid-Autumn Festival. During the summer, the film was promoted by comparison with superheroes, and the posters were cheerful and energetic. For the later slot, the publicity shifted: the film began to sell itself as heartwarming family fare.

Either angle was a guaranteed draw at the box office. The producer also released two promotional songs on the mainland: one, *Soul Pork Soup — Invincible Edition* (《心灵猪汤·无敌版》), sung by Pan Binlong, and the other, *Soul Pork Soup — Innocence Edition* (《心灵猪汤·童真版》), sung by Selina.

On September 15, opening day for *McDull: Rise of the Rice Cooker*, the film took in 4.95 million yuan. By the early hours of September 17, the total had passed 14 million. That showing is not good.

[A Douban reviewer](https://movie.douban.com/subject/25889476/comments) who goes by "Europa" thinks McDull has "flown too high," voicing the incomprehension a slice of the audience feels toward the film: "After milking all that motherly love, McDull spends a long time away from that little island, that neighborhood, that school — he's flown too high. And the most speechless, awkward part is that McDull is suddenly talented, successful, and even loved!"

On Douban, the film holds a 6.4 rating. [Of the 979 people who rated it, 47.3% gave four or five stars — a share that overwhelmingly exceeds those who gave one or two stars. But another 39.9% gave it three stars.](https://movie.douban.com/subject/25889476/)

It isn't only mainland public opinion and media saying so — even the Hong Kong press has commented that *McDull: Rise of the Rice Cooker* has drifted away from its Hong Kong roots and ended up awkward.

Mak spoke to QDaily in an interview after a preview screening on September 7. She explained how McDull came to be connected with "fighting monsters," why Superman is sent up, and why there is always a fierce girl at McDull's side.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160917031519NfziRjx31Ieb7d95.jpg-w600)

She also noted that many people feel McDull represents the Hong Kong spirit, but she has never felt that way herself — even though anyone can recognize at a glance the cramped housing blocks of Tai Kok Tsui, the greenery particular to Hong Kong, and the fishing village that looks rather like one of the outlying islands, and even though 90% of a McDull film, to this day, is completed by Hong Kong people.

Other things Mak revealed suggest that this was a new production experience not only for McDull: making *McDull: Rise of the Rice Cooker* was also a first for Brian Tse, Alice Mak and their team. As McDull's story has developed, the core creative team has genuinely been changing. Mak realized she could hardly break through on her own, and decided to bring in outside help.

She brought in an independent Hong Kong animation director to make the outer-space scenes in *McDull: Rise of the Rice Cooker*. Mak knew Wong Ping's work had strange colors — garish and jolting — and once Wong Ping took it over, he finished it in two weeks. Hong Kong comics artist Yeung Hok Tak then joined the project again: he drew the village's oddball grown-ups — the loan shark Brother Long, Sister Shrimp and company.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160917031416Cno2bghTWw8OQu4k.png-w600)

[This is what Mak can't do herself; she has asked Yeung Hok Tak for help since *McDull: The Pork of Music*.](http://www.timetimetime.net/renwu/3396.html) The film needed some small-time thugs who look genuinely menacing, but what she draws never has any menace. In this film she was responsible for the children of the fishing village — that she is better at.

Yet against all those jolting images, Brian Tse and Alice Mak still held on to the warmth of the whole McDull story. So — guess how McDull ends up saving the world?

The robot he designs, "Rice Cooker," tickles the monster. Knowing what the monster likes to eat, he wins it over: in the end the monster gives up its attack on Earth, takes the giant rice cooker, and heads back to outer space.

**Q: QDaily**

**M: Alice Mak**

Q: Some people, after watching *Rise of the Rice Cooker*, came away with this feeling: when McDull leaves his home island and is transformed into a hero, achieving success in the traditional sense, he is no longer McDull.

M: Really? I think if McDull didn't fight properly, they would say they just wanted him to fight properly and get famous. Now that he really can't become a hero, they worry again. No — to me, McDull has always been right. I've already put myself in Mrs. Mak's shoes. Is McDull really dumb? He's not dumb; he's thinking about other possibilities. The monster comes to invade Earth, and everyone is busy figuring out how to beat it, or how to make it say sorry. But McDull doesn't think that way. He only cares about the monster's problems at home — whether it came to Earth because it needed something, and whether he can satisfy that need and make its life a little better.

Q: Why make a monster-fighting film?

M: Because Brian Tse always wants to make something different. I think boys always hope there'll be one monster-fighting film, or a wuxia film, or a romance — at least once or twice you make one of those. I think Brian Tse gave McDull his greatest difficulty yet: before there was Wudang Mountains, the problems with his mother, the singing — this time he's run into every difficulty in the whole world, so how does he face it?

Q: Does the way mainland company money comes in interfere with the choice of subject matter?

M: I've never met them. When Brian Tse writes the script, he never knows — he just writes the story.

Q: You've said before there would be no villains in McDull's stories, but actually the earlier stories had small-time thugs and such. Is the antagonist this time especially thorough?

M: Who is the villain this time?

Q: My understanding is, it's the strongest one in the robot competition.

X: Oh. But it turned good later — it saved the giant rice cooker in outer space, so for me there is no villain to be found. I still think they're all good people. Everyone thinks the monster is the bad guy, so they have to beat it and make it say sorry. So it's just doing its job too; it's not some villain.

Q: McDull has grown up. Did you deliberately design a change in his personality?

X: His basic personality is simple, innocent, kind — and this time I think kindness is what matters. I seem to have heard Brian Tse say that kindness is the most powerful weapon.

Q: In this film, McDull is in elementary school.

M: Every time he runs into difficulty, he happens to be the right age for it. Because this time he had to make some inventions, he knows a bit more. For me it's a marvel — I've never seen him in elementary school before; it's all fresh in terms of his temperament.

Q: How is it fresh?

M: The audience probably thinks it's about the same, but I find it very different. In this animated film his skin color is a bit darker, and the other kids — his classmates — are no longer the little animals from before; they're real children, all sun-tanned and dark. While we were making it: ah, he's grown up — should his voice be a bit bigger too? Should his mother's expressions toward him be different?

Q: In the previous McDull-and-his-mother story, wasn't McDull already in his teens?

M: Right — sometimes he's even older, with a girlfriend. In the previous film he was a detective, an ancient man, an inventor. So he has grown up before, but who he becomes when he grows up, who his girlfriend is — it's different every time.

Q: So the time and space of each McDull film don't carry over?

M: Right — it depends on when he lives. Say the 1960s: he might have long hair. Sometimes he's a kid running around a fishing village, so he's sometimes sockless, that feral-kid look.

Q: McDull's girlfriend used to be May (a cow); now she's been swapped for a pig, the same species as him. Was that deliberate? I don't know — does this even count as his girlfriend?

M: What girlfriend in kindergarten. There's always a very fierce girl by his side. April is a wild boar — she has a tusk. But we still wanted her to be cute, not genuinely mean.

Q: Why does she have to be fierce?

M: I think you have people like this around you too — you feel like you can be a bit fiercer with them. Maybe you're not that fierce with other people, but with them you always end up a little fierce. McDull is that kind of character. You'd find the person kind, you'd feel closer to them, you'd be fiercer with them — but amid the fierceness you'd love them too, anxious for them, worried they'll be bullied by someone else.

Q: Why so much 3D animation?

M: There was 3D before too, but this time it's almost entirely 3D. First, the rhythm demands it — the monster moves to a faster beat, there are fights, and it can't be slow like McDull used to be. The sensory jolt — monster-fighting can't be handled too flatly. Plus we've gotten to know quite a few 3D talents in Hong Kong; they did a lot of the design for us — the robot, the monster, and the backgrounds. We look at what talent Hong Kong has and get together to figure out what we can make.

Q: So everyone making the animation is from Hong Kong?

M: A small part of the backgrounds and animation got help from the mainland, but about 90% of it was done in Hong Kong.

Q: Which parts did you draw yourself?

M: Less this time. We brought in Wong Ping — the outer-space backgrounds are his. Yeung Hok Tak did those small-time thugs, the littler characters in the village; the kids are mine, and the grown-ups are all his — Brother Long, Sister Shrimp. His stuff has a special vitality — Hong Kong's underbelly.

Q: How many people worked on this film? *McDull: The Pork of Music* had over a hundred people join.

M: Ah, did it? People keep joining, really — once it gets to a certain point, if someone sings well, you invite them. People keep joining, so you can't keep count. For instance, Wong Ping came to help when it was about nine-tenths done.

Q: Why did you think of asking him to help at that point?

M: Because I couldn't crack it. The outer space still wasn't strange enough. Wong Ping's colors are wonderful — very outer-space, not the real outer space; the colors are so vivid, so jolting.

Q: Who came up with the "SORRY" bit?

M: Brian Tse, of course — it's wildly funny. But to me it's just ordinary funny, nothing of great significance.

Q: Why poke fun at Superman?

M: It's the needs of the genre film too. It's also that we grew up watching Ultraman, the Masked Rider, and "Salted Egg Superman."

Q: How did you come up with the way the monster walks?

M: That's the 3D animation company's credit. We kept demanding that every monster and robot move in its own way. I had also bought some Superman toys from the '60s and '70s — they walk just like that: hit a wall and spin around. McDull's inventions are all not-very-high-tech.

Q: Is one film every two years a deliberate rhythm?

M: One film every two years — so tiring. I think with a bit more time we could do it better. Animation is that kind of marvelous thing. Our company doesn't have many people — just a dozen-odd, twenty-odd. And you can keep improving, keep building the same story. Animators are marvelous people: they work hard for two years without saying a word, asking the audience to digest it all in 90 minutes — it seems like such a bad deal, but that's also where its romance lies. It strains to tell you a good story, and hopes you'll watch with your heart.

Q: Why is it three months short? Who is rushing this time?

(Promotional staff interject)

Q: Did the production of the "Soul Pork Soup" songs involve any communication with you?

M: It wasn't made by us, so I only listened to it once and didn't get to hear it clearly. I won't comment.

Q: Was there a different promotional song for Hong Kong?

M: Yes — Liu Xiaohua is his stage name. His real name is Liu Haonan. He's adorable; he sings in a "natural high." His mom is in a band too, and she taught him to sing gently. In the end we encouraged him and he sang it happily. Why not control this part... (promotional staff interject)

Q: Do Hong Kong's changes still unsettle you?

M: I was unsettled in the earlier years; now I'm used to it.

Q: Were adult jokes deliberately inserted?

M: Were there? Which ones?

Q: The design of the monster, the monster's interactions, that sort of thing.

M: This one is a bit different — we call it a genre film. At the start, when you see the monster, the superman, you know you've seen this kind of film before; you don't know there's some affection in store, so you take it for granted that McDull will bully the monster and beat it. That's what a genre film does: it lures you into that trap, then lets you discover it isn't that way at all.

I didn't answer the adult question, right? Then I can let Brian Tse answer. (Brian Tse's reply: Adult jokes? Not really — I don't know which ones you mean. Anyway, everyone jokes around sometimes, and it just got written in.)

Q: Are you more like McDull or more like Mrs. Mak?

M: Both. The worrying part of McDull is like his mother; I don't love McDull as much as Mrs. Mak does, but the dumb part is like McDull, and the kindness isn't enough yet — I have to learn from both of them.

Q: What about Brian Tse?

M: He's the real one. If it were a record, he'd be the producer — he handles things more comprehensively, and I think he doesn't bring emotion into it. He's actually a very simple person, very close to McDull, yet able to see all the other angles.

Q: Brian Tse has said the McDull stories set out to solve a problem: how can a person avoid distorting himself while still facing a rather distorted world. Has that problem been solved?

M: Probably not. Even now, when McDull saves the Earth, he still does it his own way — nobody understands the robot he builds out of a rice cooker, an iron and a washing machine; only in the last five or ten minutes does everyone start to get it. So for me, McDull still has endless difficulties to deal with, and the next problem may be even harder than saving the Earth.

Q: Have you already started the next one?

M: I know the next one is resting. I don't know... For instance, this film was made together with the mom one (*McDull: Me & My Mum*, released in 2014) — that took three years. There are already several scripts ready for the next one; we can't say yet which one we'll make.

Q: *Rise of the Rice Cooker* and the mom film were simultaneous. But was there no next film happening simultaneously with *Rise of the Rice Cooker*?

M: That's a very good question. Creators are very bad at controlling time. Doing two films at once in the past was so we wouldn't take too long to put out one. If we had finished the mom film and then started, I'd now be rushing *Rise of the Rice Cooker* in Hong Kong — so it's already a little faster. Next we hope to push two films together. I don't know how long the next one will take either — hopefully two years. Otherwise the kids will forget who McDull is.
