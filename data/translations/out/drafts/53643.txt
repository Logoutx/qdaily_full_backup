“(I) hope to twist my body with all my might. And often it’s a cliff, and down I go again.”

With a new show about to premiere, Zhang Yimou has recently been scheduled for many interviews. Two things keep coming up whenever he sits down with reporters: why he doesn’t “cherish his own feathers,” and why he always seems so “full of energy” when he works.

*Dialogue · Apologue 2047* (《对话·寓言 2047》), directed by Zhang Yimou, launches its second season in June. The show is defined as a “conceptual performance,” made up of seven acts. Each act runs under ten minutes and creatively combines “ancient craft” with “powerful technology.” Put more plainly, it’s a bit like a theatrical, scaled-down version of the opening ceremonies Zhang Yimou has directed.

After the first season ended, the online commentary on *Dialogue · Apologue 2047* was mostly positive. But the negative reviews were merciless. One said, “Beautiful it may be, but the body and soul are at odds.” The show’s promotional copy talks about “dialoguing with the inner self while parabolizing the future,” so one commentator wrote: “A folk-culture hodgepodge, with no sense of the future whatsoever.”

The audience for live performance is small, the total number of reviews is small too, and the bad ones are more easily drowned out.

In truth, for a Zhang Yimou work, *Dialogue · Apologue 2047* hasn’t drawn much attention or discussion. But the show’s producer, Buick, is pleased with the collaboration. The combination of Zhang Yimou plus China plus technology is, for them, enough to count as speaking to their audience.

![Dialogue · Apologue 2047 used Zhang Yimou's portrait directly to make its poster](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180528113016FWLp6jbh8d7P0sn9.jpeg-w600)

Back when the first season was being produced, Zhang Yimou was still working on *The Great Wall*. Bao Ye, head of Buick’s marketing department, approached Zhang Yimou about collaborating on the show and promised him it didn’t need to turn a profit. Zhang Yimou felt that gave him creative room, and he took the job. When it came time to start the second season, he was also filming *Shadow* and, after that, the “Beijing 8 Minutes” segment for the PyeongChang Winter Olympics. As of now, *Shadow* is finished and the “Beijing 8 Minutes” is over, *Dialogue · Apologue 2047* hasn’t yet premiered, and he’s already preparing a new film.

He Lulu, the show’s executive director, said the early preparation for *Dialogue · Apologue 2047* involved a huge number of creative meetings. Once rehearsals began, Zhang Yimou was no longer on site, but he watched rehearsal videos every day and made phone calls every evening, each call running well over an hour.

After the press conference for *Dialogue · Apologue 2047*, a reporter asked whether every film he’d made over his career represented some kind of breakthrough. Zhang Yimou’s first sentence in reply was: “I’m not someone who cherishes his own feathers. So my body of work shows a rather splintered picture.”

In 1987, Zhang Yimou’s directorial debut, *Red Sorghum*, won the Golden Bear at the Berlin Film Festival — the first Asian work to take that prize.

Between 1987 and 1999, he directed *Ju Dou*, *Raise the Red Lantern*, *The Story of Qiu Ju*, *To Live*, *Not One Less*, and *The Road Home*, earning three Oscar nominations and five Golden Globe nominations.

![A still from The Story of Qiu Ju](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527021753YQEha3SqNbel0n8y.jpg-w600)

In 2002, Zhang Yimou shifted to directing commercial films with *Hero*. He followed with *House of Flying Daggers* and *Riding Alone for Thousands of Miles*. The reviews were generally weaker than before, but audiences flocked to these films.

It wasn’t until *Curse of the Golden Flower* in 2006 that audiences began to question him heavily. Then came *A Simple Noodle Story* in 2009 and *The Great Wall* in 2016 — plus the various visual-spectacle projects he took on after the 2008 Olympic opening ceremony.

[“Up and down, high and low. One moment the feathers are full, the next they’re all gone.”](https://weibo.com/tv/v/G9wiO4FAn?fid=1034:e4aacf20c1532441930d41138aa34027) Zhang Yimou said, “Some people think it isn’t worth it, some think it’s careless, but I’m quite happy.” He said: “Our generation grew up in the environment of the past, we were already well-disciplined, and there was plenty of pressure and restriction. In my own work I often hope to be restless, to twist my body with all my might. So often it’s a cliff, and down I go again.”

The fuller version of this thinking is in the interview below. These were Zhang Yimou’s answers during a hurried group press interview. They may be off-the-cuff lines, or they may be how he genuinely understands the world.

**Q: Questions from reporters after the press conference**

**Zhang Yimou: Born in Xi’an, Shaanxi, in 1950; considered one of the representative figures of China’s “Fifth Generation” film directors**

**Q: In earlier profiles you’re often described as someone afraid of wasting time. Is there something that others find like a waste of time, terribly boring, but that you find interesting and want to do?**

Zhang Yimou: Talking through scripts. Sometimes we talk for a year or two, two or three years, every single week. Anyone else would have a hard time sitting through it — just like our talks for “2047,” which went on for ages. Who comes on? Who do we use? How do you add one plus one? How does it get presented visually? Which part is the high tech? Which part is the ancient tradition? How do they combine? How does it appear on stage? What about the music? The dance? The sound? What’s the content of the stage? And so on…

We talked for a very long time. Every meeting drove everyone crazy — searching online, watching things, and if it didn’t work, vetoing it. These vetoes were national-level: America’s out, Germany’s out, Britain’s…

Talking scripts is the most tedious. A lot of the time it put everyone to sleep. Outsiders sitting there would say, what does Zhang Yimou do all day? It must be so, so boring. But I think that part is the most crucial for me — it’s not the actual hands-on stage; like making a film or doing a show, it’s the creative stage.

I always say: three elements — creativity, creativity, creativity — just like real estate says location, location, location. That’s how creativity comes out. Only later do we talk about how much money was spent, how much time was used. The original creativity comes early on. People watch the behind-the-scenes footage: so hard, all of that. But that’s the realization stage. That’s the most tedious, the most oppressive — you’re oppressing yourself.

It goes on and on and on, it devours time. Sometimes you can’t get past a single point and no one can save you. You think about it constantly; it doesn’t leave you alone when you eat or sleep. Suddenly you think of something in the middle of the night and write it down, and at breakfast you overturn it. Sometimes the team discusses it, and you take a bathroom break and overturn the whole thing.

That process is the hardest. Originality is the hardest, and the most tedious. But for me — it’s not that I like it; it’s that the job is sitting right there and I took the order. It opens tomorrow, time waits for no one, and that’s where your pressure comes from.

So you save yourself; you have to deal with it. And you know you have to leave time for production, for external coordination, for the team; you have to subtract the holidays; you can calculate the schedule — with experience you know this whole process. When you know there’s one week left and you’re still at zero, how could you sleep? You drive everyone crazy, you drive yourself crazy first — there’s no way around it.

So, finding joy in it — the so-called pain mixed with happiness is probably something like this. It seems terribly boring.

**Reporter: You kept mentioning creativity, creativity. Across all your years in film, has every movie shown a breakthrough?**

Zhang Yimou: Right, I’m not someone who cherishes his own feathers, so my body of work shows a rather splintered picture. Of course I could think differently — carefully polish my own posture, make my work show a coherent overall flow, keep it consistent, make myself feel complete. I’m not too willing to do that.

Our generation grew up in the environment of the past; we were already well-disciplined, and there was plenty of pressure and restriction. In my own work I often hope to be restless, to twist my body with all my might. So often it’s a cliff, and down I go again.

When choosing material I deliberately let it be a bit random — don’t overthink it, don’t consider the consequences. If you’re interested today, do it today; maybe tomorrow your heart changes, and then we’ll talk again. So my work shows all kinds of possibilities. Some people think it isn’t worth it, some think it’s careless, but I’m quite happy. I work hard at everything, even if others say it’s garbage — I work hard at things I’m interested in. Nothing is perfect; if you were really interested in that thing at the time, go do it. Maybe the consequences are serious.

Working this way also made me cross into many fields, do many different things, and take on many projects — often three or four big things on my plate in a single year. Besides making films there are other things weighing on me, but I’m willing to do them. You could also turn them down and be a pure film director, spending three years honing one sword, but I’d rather keep myself busy.

My contemporaries were all workers; they’ve all retired. I’m lucky to still be a director, still doing creative work. Be grateful for this era — stop strolling; run if you can. As long as your body allows, do more things, and don’t worry about good or bad.

I’ve also found that thinking hard about original work is good for the brain — you have to learn by analogy and apply it to film.

But film is the hardest. No director today can say every one of his works is amazing. Film is truly a high-end thing; it fuses all of it, everything. Even though it’s a simple story, it embodies a whole worldview, outlook on life and values, thought, emotion — it’s hard. A film recognized as great worldwide is hard, so we’re still learning.

**Q: At the press conference just now, a reporter mentioned that you received an honorary doctorate from Boston University a couple of days ago. How many doctorates have you received now — do you still remember?**

Zhang Yimou: Just the foreign ones number quite a few — the big ones include Yale, Moscow University, and one in Korea. For me it’s an honor, and everyone’s way of affirming you. But really, I think it’s the era. China today is no longer the China of the past — Chinese culture and Chinese people are valued, national strength has risen, the country’s standing has changed, the look of the people. I benefited from the era. The era made me, the so-called first generation. Without this era — I came through the Cultural Revolution — we caught the last train and didn’t waste a single moment after that.

When you talk to the post-90s and post-00s about the era, about how things were back then, they feel nothing. But I know — our parents’ generation was wasted, and the third- and fourth-generation directors too.

China today is wonderful — the barrier to becoming a director is so low, anyone can be a director, and out of nowhere a dark horse appears. That’s great. Today’s China suits young people starting businesses very well, especially in the cultural industries.

Film, TV and web series keep producing new talent. In my own view, when it comes to large-scale live productions, to the stage, young people aren’t growing as fast as they are in film and television — especially mega-events like the Olympics, where basically no really young people have come in. I did a large-scale performance program at Hunan University, training master’s and PhD students professionally. I’d like to mentor them myself, and when I look for teams I look for people like them.

So in China’s cultural industries, the next ten to twenty years will be a golden age, concentrated in this area. I think it’s a good era.

**Q: (Omitted) In college, to learn from your seniors, you even borrowed books from Chen Kaige?**

Zhang Yimou: Of course. Back then we borrowed books. There were 26 students in the cinematography class; I was ten years older than all of them, younger than my two younger brothers.

So in my class I felt very inferior, very sensitive, thinking I was just too old. The old-generation cinematographers who lectured us put it this way: after you graduate, you start pushing the dolly track as a third assistant, then second assistant, first assistant, associate cinematographer, camera operator, and finally cinematographer — it will take you at least ten years to become a cinematographer, so cherish it. I graduated at thirty-two, did the math, and realized I’d be forty-two before I became a cinematographer. I thought that was too slow.

It felt like a hassle. Then I looked at the directing class next door — their incoming age was older than ours, and that cohort, Tian Zhuangzhuang, Kaige and the rest, were all older than me. So I thought about switching to directing. That was my sophomore-year idea — mainly so that making a living wouldn’t be so awkward. I asked Kaige and some classmates to get me some books to read. I was too embarrassed to say I was switching to directing.

I said I just wanted to look and broaden my knowledge. Each of them gave me a list of ten or twenty titles, and I borrowed and read them all. It was only because my age was awkward that the idea sprouted; I didn’t think much about it.

**Q: These large-scale performances and the use of the “shadow” element?**

Zhang Yimou: I care a lot about visuals, about light and shadow. This time there’s one thing that’s different from last time. Last time it was directly a show of technology and traditional art. This time I have a transformation: I let everyone see a small robotic arm, filmed once, filmed twice, rendered in real time, magnified — transmitted through filming and light and shadow. It’s a kind of visual, dual-layered creation.

My sensitivity to light and shadow also comes from my training, and from visuals — that’s the most important. We often say the simplest thing: does it look good? It has to look good. When it looks good, everything follows. Whatever point you’re making, no one understands it.

It’s a one-sentence thing: looking good matters most. Back to film it’s the same — does it look good? Good-looking is good-looking; if it’s not good-looking, it’s all over.
