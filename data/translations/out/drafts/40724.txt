There's no doubt it was a show of force — though it benefited both the brand and science.

At the 30-kilometer mark, Eliud Kipchoge's pace was 1 second behind.

His muscles began to loosen, his legs quietly losing energy, and his mind could already calculate that he had likely missed his shot at "breaking the 2-hour marathon" — yet a smile still flickered across his face from time to time.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512090203WavtpgelwToE0AOZ.png-w600)

The 32-year-old Kenyan track star holds the third-fastest marathon time in history, and since 2013 has won six of the world's seven major marathons. Kipchoge has a habit: he likes to smile at critical moments in a race, he says, because running is a joyful thing.

Even through a screen, it's hard to grasp what it means to run at 5.83 meters per second for two hours straight (roughly the speed of your all-out sprint), but viewers watching the livestream on Twitter, Facebook, and Sina Weibo couldn't help crying out: "Is that a smile or a muscle twitch?" "I swear he smiled — unbelievable!" "He looks like he's out for a stroll in the park!"

The "Breaking2" marathon organized by Nike was nearing its end.

Unlike the city marathons people are used to — the gun fires and a tide of runners surges forward — this race featured only 3 elite athletes: Rio Olympic men's marathon champion Kipchoge, half-marathon world record holder Lelisa Desisa, and three-time Boston Marathon champion Zersenay Tadese. Their goal was to break the 2-hour barrier for the marathon.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512090635GKXqAmx8nZygvOJi.jpg-w600)

The current world record for the fastest human marathon is 2 hours 2 minutes 57 seconds, set by Kenyan runner Dennis Kimetto at the 2014 Berlin Marathon. To shave off those three minutes would mean cutting roughly 4.2 seconds per kilometer — the equivalent of finishing the race while Kimetto still had a kilometer to go. And the last time the marathon world record was shortened by a full 3 minutes, it took about 19 years.

At 5:45 a.m. local time on May 6 in Italy (11:45 a.m. Beijing time), the race officially began. Fifteen minutes later, the sky was only just beginning to lighten.

Most marathons start at 9 a.m. or later, but Breaking2 began more than three hours earlier — which to some extent conflicts with the commonly held theory that "marathons run at night are more efficient." But "in our planning, environmental factors mattered more," Dr. Philip Skiba, who oversees the athletes' physiological health and training system, told QDaily. The point was to get the temperature and humidity available only in the early morning.

Wind speed 0.0–0.4 kilometers per second, temperature 11.3 degrees Celsius, relative humidity 79%. In Philip's words, "very suitable for running."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705120904481GB5cX7yTS3R4iVm.jpg-w600)

Beyond the meticulous planning, poetic and ambitious messaging appeared in every corner of the event. The livestream camera occasionally cut to Nike's prepared video footage, where you'd see images from May 6, sixty-three years earlier, of Roger Bannister attempting to run a mile in under 4 minutes, along with the word "Moonshot." In a pre-race interview with QDaily, Nike chairman and CEO Mark Parker described the goal of breaking the 2-hour marathon as "one of many holy grail challenges."

In fact, the Breaking2 idea was not Nike's first. In 1991, Mayo Clinic researcher Michael Joyner predicted that a human could run a full marathon in 1 hour 57 minutes 58 seconds. The idea truly became enticing in 2008, when Ethiopian runner Haile Gebrselassie became the first person to break 2 hours 4 minutes; in 2011, the *Journal of Applied Physiology* published an article titled *The Two-Hour Marathon: When? Who?*; and in 2014, shortly after Dennis Kimetto set his new world record, Yannis Pitsiladis, professor of sport and exercise science at the University of Brighton in the UK, announced to the world his Sub2Hur project, claiming he would achieve the goal before 2019.

![The projected curve of marathon record declines predicted by Michael Joyner in 1991, image from Sub2](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512090728t3hUWwfCskrGbJFv.png-w600)

Crowdfunding on websites, recruiting talent. As the first person to genuinely and publicly attempt to break this limit, Pitsiladis's project needed to raise $30 million. He tried to win sponsorship money from Nike and other companies, but failed. As it turned out, Nike wanted to do it itself.

In December 2016, Nike announced its own Breaking2 project, and two months later, word also leaked that Adidas's two-hour-marathon project, called "Sub2," had been "in preparation for years."

Judging by speed of execution, Nike had temporarily become the leader in this "moonshot" race.

On the eve of the race, Andy Jones, an applied-physiology professor and external consultant Nike had hired specifically for Breaking2, wrote his prediction on a slip of paper: 2:00:59. The actual result (2:00:25) was somewhat better, and better than most sports scientists had predicted beforehand.

The only problem: the result of this race would not reset the existing marathon world record. The IAAF, which sets the rules of the running world, believed Nike had "cheated" on Kipchoge's behalf.

### Like Tuning a Race Car

Nike set numerous conditions for this race, run on the morning of May 6, to ensure a satisfying result.

First, the special venue: the Monza F1 racetrack in Italy. Sitting northeast of Milan, the "Temple of Speed," which can hold 115,000 spectators, has roared with engine noise since it was built in 1922, and the notorious "death corner," the Parabolica curve, served as the starting point of the race.

The track, responsible for the deaths of nearly a hundred people, now became the perfect marathon venue. A nearly turn-free loop eliminated unnecessary energy expenditure, and its marked 2.4-kilometer length allowed perfect control of pacing, hydration, nutrition, and the movement of support teams.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512090956T5kcUfXd3iRWytFa.jpg-w600)

"What we're doing is something quite unique: finding a window of time in which we can choose a day when all the conditions — humidity, temperature, environment — are most suitable for the attempt," they said. After analyzing six years of historical weather data and comparing it with the climatic conditions of the fastest marathons in history, Monza won out thanks to its temperature of around 12°C and mostly cloudy weather (which reduces the runner's heat load), as well as a stable airflow thanks to the track's just-right distance from the coast. More importantly, a circuit far from a city center offers far greater controllability.

"The ideal course should be flat. So far, the fastest world marathon records have come in Berlin, which is considered one of the flattest courses." Brett Kirby, chief physiologist at Nike's Sport Research Lab, said they finally settled on Monza — "flatter than Berlin" — in January this year. It encompassed all the necessary environmental elements; some analyses hold that the Monza circuit and the environmental edge it offered were worth 20 seconds.

Berlin's Brandenburg Gate, Boston's Heartbreak Hill, the Thames views of London, New York's Verrazzano Bridge, and the wide expanse of Lake Michigan — in Andy Jones's words, "no major city's marathon course is perfect."

But the unusual course was not the main reason the IAAF refused to recognize the race. What they found even harder to accept were the 30 elite pacemakers surrounding the 3 athletes, along with the swarms of research teams appearing everywhere — like pit crews around an F1 star.

And Kipchoge, Desisa, and Tadese were supercars, carefully selected for their biggest engines ("VO2 max") and power ("running economy").

Anyone who has ever run along a beach or a riverside will tell you that a kilometer can differ by several seconds between a tailwind and a headwind. Starting in the 1970s, a tactic called "drafting" appeared in marathon running. It usually means following another runner's rhythm early in the race, where wind resistance is lower, saving energy for a finishing kick.

The 30 pacemakers — including world champion and American middle- and long-distance star Bernard Lagat — would form a wind-blocking barrier in groups of 6, each responsible for 2 laps, with rotations of 3 runners at a time.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512091039PuJz4aFhqWTc3GlI.gif-w600Gif)

At a pre-race press conference the day before, Nike Breaking2's research team proudly unveiled this pacing strategy, and when the GIF diagram on the electronic screen showed the pacemakers rapidly adjusting their formation into an "arrow," the assembled media gasped.

"We had many alternative pacing strategies — you can go ahead and imagine them wildly," said Matthew Nurse, vice president of the Nike Sport Research Lab.

On top of that, a Tesla Model S would drive the entire race directly ahead of the runners, displaying in real time the current pace, elapsed time, and projected finish time, and shooting lasers at the ground behind it to guide the formation.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2017051209521247Z5FSKf2dVrD1E9.jpeg-w600)

"An 85% wind-resistance shielding effect requires you to be tucked exactly behind the windscreen," explained *Runner's World* columnist Alex Hutchinson, who was also one of the race's commentators. "That almost never happens in normal running — until last Friday."

The fueling strategy was another key point. Nike set up a supply station in the middle of the circuit, and at 2 a.m. the scientists prepared a whole row of bottles for each athlete containing varying ratios of carbohydrate, caffeine, and water — some liquid, some jelly-like. Every lap, a motorcycle courier would hand one bottle to the athlete, replenishing energy and easing the stomach discomfort caused by intense exercise.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512091221uRLBHqvKmYPF6CEw.jpg-w600)

"2,300 calories." After the race, Andy Jones pulled out his phone and calculated the energy the athlete had burned. "We gave them roughly... 15 grams of carbohydrate per hour, so about 500 calories total. The rest will have to be made up with champagne!"

Environmental scientists, medical doctors, biomechanists, designers, R&D directors... According to Nike CEO Mark Parker, collaboration on this scale across disciplines was a fresh experience for Nike. The team, totaling around 400 people (including some external consultants), had one job: to analyze what breaking 2 hours in the marathon requires, and to optimize every factor that might be worth a few seconds.

"Since we've already cut 6 seconds per mile from 2:00:25 to 1:59:59, we just need to find that next 1 second. There must be something we can do," Andy said.

Although the amount Nike paid the three athletes will not be made public, for reference: for Breaking2, the three gave up the recent London and Berlin marathons and trained separately in Kenya, Eritrea, and Ethiopia. The thin oxygen at altitudes of 5,000–8,000 feet increases the number of red blood cells in the athletes' bodies, allowing the blood to carry more oxygen to the muscles, and this higher red-blood-cell concentration can be maintained for up to two weeks after leaving high altitude.

"In the end, we neither pushed nor pulled the athletes — it was all human power," said Matthew Nurse, who holds a PhD in biomechanics and a medical degree, in an interview. "Talk science if you want, but in the end they still have to run."

### "It's Gotta Be the Shoes!"

Even so, running under two hours remained brutally hard. It amounts to cutting 3% off the world record, and no human track record has ever been broken that quickly — women's marathon record holder Paula Radcliffe improved the record by 1.4% across two runs, and Bolt's 100-meter world record was less than 1.6% faster.

For a company that sells running shoes, Nike's answer was obvious, echoing the old Jordan shoe ad slogan: "It's gonna be the shoes!"

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512092121a48y9JWFpYXvudiI.jpg-w600)

In June 2013, Nike quietly launched a new secret project internally. Sandy Bodecker, vice president of special projects, was the soul of the plan. The 35-year Nike veteran and "problem solver" went to the CEO's office and told Mark Parker what he wanted to do; the two agreed Breaking2 was a great idea. "My part was saying yes the whole way through — being the cheerleader," Mark Parker said.

Around the same time, Dr. Geng Luo, who grew up in China, joined Nike's Sport Research Lab as a biomechanics expert, aiming to improve the running economy of running shoes by 3% — one link in the Breaking2 plan.

The first several hundred pairs of test shoes were all designed around the same idea: the lighter, the better. But problems followed.

Based on the theory that "every 100 grams added to a running shoe increases energy expenditure by 1%," ultra-light shoes are the design direction for most marathon shoes. But when the target is set at a "two-hour marathon" — requiring the athlete to sustain a very fast, even pace — Geng Luo and his team found that soles as thin as cicada wings could not withstand the forces of Breaking2's more than 20,000 steps, equivalent to three times body weight.

In 2015, they made a test shoe that, because of its unremarkable looks, the team internally called the "Cinderella shoe." It used two layers of very low-density blown rubber (named Zoom X by Nike) and an unusual forefoot thickness of 21 millimeters for a marathon shoe. Geng Luo compared the energy return from the thick rubber sole to a big battery.

And sandwiched between the two layers of rubber, a spoon-shaped carbon fiber plate weighing less than 20 grams that reduces calf exertion would become the focus of later controversy.

This March, Nike unveiled the Zoom Vaporfly 4%, the shoe that would officially go on sale in June at a retail price of $250. The version used in the Breaking2 project would be a customized Zoom Vaporfly Elite. The biggest difference between the two is that the carbon fiber plate is fine-tuned to each athlete's foot and running style.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512091944kX3RKizDJNV59gfe.jpeg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512091714CVzAe45x9HOQtwjW.jpg-w600)

Yannis Pitsiladis, initiator of the Sub2Hur project, somehow got hold of a pair of Zoom Vaporfly 4%, and images of the shoe from a CT scanner were published by *The New York Times* in an article titled "Will Nike's New Marathon Shoes, With a Carbon Fiber Plate, Give Runners an Unfair Advantage?" The carbon plate in the images looked like some dangerous contraband spotted at airport security.

The article mentioned the carbon plate in the same breath as track athlete Oscar Pistorius and his J-shaped carbon-fiber blade prosthetics. "We're entering a really interesting area of sports technology ethics," South African sports physiologist Ross Tucker said in an interview with *The New York Times*.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512092509irCTAIofvnml1dM5.jpg-w600)

![A CT scanner reveals the carbon fiber plate in the midsole of the Nike shoe. Image copyright: Yannis Pitsiladis](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512091955FCrGshbiQWjqJ8IX.jpg-w600)

Ross Tucker offered some of the sharpest commentary on the Breaking2 project, and he is also the author of the Science of Sport blog; immediately after the race on May 6, he published a post calling for any carbon-plate-equipped gear to be banned from competition.

"Runners wearing this shoe have already won London, Berlin, New York, and Chicago; Kipchoge's 2:03:05 at the London Marathon and the gold, silver, and bronze medals in the Rio Olympic men's marathon were all won in this shoe," Geng Luo said, unable to hide his delight in his introduction. Before Nike unveiled the Zoom Vaporfly 4%, these shoes had all appeared disguised as ordinary models, and athletes and researchers were required to keep them secret.

Nike even issued a dedicated press release interviewing many athletes who had raced in the shoes. They reported that the new shoe reduced prolonged fatigue, sped up recovery, and was like a three-course feast for the calves.

But obviously, for a runner like Kara Goucher, who finished fourth, the feeling must have been exactly the opposite — she had been bumped out of Rio Olympic qualification by two opponents wearing the Zoom Vaporfly 4%.

In fact, carbon fiber plates have long been used in athletic shoes. As early as the turn of the millennium, Adidas had used a carbon plate in a product called Pro Plate, and the scientist who helped develop it, Dr. Darren Stefanyshyn, was Geng Luo's PhD advisor.

The Zoom Vaporfly 4% was sent to the University of Colorado lab for testing, and the "4%" name comes from one parameter in the results: 4% less energy than Nike's current best marathon shoe. Rodger Kram, the lab's director, said in an interview with *Runner's World*: "Almost all modern running shoes actually have springs. We now call it a foam midsole."

On these discussions, Tony Bignell, Nike's vice president of footwear innovation, offered this:

"First of all, I don't think it's illegal. It's a bit like tennis — it's only cheating if the ball bounces back higher than the force you hit it with. All we're doing is minimizing the energy wasted when your foot strikes the ground."

### Who Is the Next Kipchoge?

Compared to Breaking2, the Zoom Vaporfly's sales pitch is obviously aimed at people whose marathons take more than 6 hours.

During the Sina Weibo livestream of the race, peak viewership reached 1.28 million; according to Brandwatch tracking, mentions of Nike on Twitter, Facebook, and Instagram topped 584,000 over the past seven days; the #Breaking2 hashtag was used 400,000 times on Twitter, 87% of the mentions positive. The hashtag's exposure exceeded 2 trillion — a Brandwatch senior data analyst said it was the largest single-hashtag exposure he had ever seen.

Even CCTV, invited to Milan, livestreamed the entire race on its mobile app.

Brand-marketing firm Red Peak said in an interview that Breaking2 would be one of the most outstanding marketing precedents of the coming years. "They grabbed everyone's attention with something novel and elusive, then used social media to propel it further."

"A marathon challenge secretly prepared for years was about to be livestreamed in an extremely risky way." Although the event itself was not open to the public, anyone with a phone and Wi-Fi could watch the whole race. And that so-called risk had in fact been reduced to a minimum: whether all three broke 2, one or two broke 2, or all three failed, Nike had prepared contingency plans for each scenario.

All signs indicate that Breaking2 was a thoroughly prepared project that had to be executed as quickly as possible.

The sporting-goods industry has been deeply shaped by fashion trends over the past two years. Nike, best at crafting polished TV commercials and mass-market sports products, repeatedly lost growth momentum to its rival Adidas, which sold staggering numbers of sneakers thanks to the social media influence of trendy celebrities.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170512092449PEjqY0hMCQ6s9Uen.jpeg-w600)

The era of Prefontaine — who broke the American record in Nikes in 1972 — when an athlete was both endorser and director of public affairs, is over. Today most people are far less attuned to a champion than to a candid street photo of an influencer. Which also explains why, even as so many athletes won titles in this brand-new marathon shoe, Nike stayed tight-lipped. In Tony Bignell's words, Breaking2 was the result of "collaboration among the running brand team, the business team, the PR team, the research team, and the innovation team."

"A courageous attempt — congratulations to Eliud Kipchoge." After the race, Adidas Running posted this reply under Nike's Weibo post.

The IAAF tweeted: "Great run by Eliud Kipchoge in Monza."

"Given that the sporting world, under the cloud of doping scandals, is in the midst of a push to annul all world records set before 2005, getting excited about a race that won't be recognized as a world record seems, frankly, a bit odd," BBC sports commentator Ed Harry wrote after the race.

Sports freelance writer Sarah Barker commented: If Nike's Breaking2 was meant to showcase human potential, I think it showed precisely the opposite — it made unambiguously clear just how far we are from breaking the 2-hour barrier.

"Even if this race isn't officially recognized, it will still be called the fastest marathon ever run," Professor Martin Polley, director of the International Centre for Sports History and Culture at De Montfort University, said in an interview with *The Guardian*. "The real challenge lies ahead: if Nike's runner can break 2 at Monza, can they do it again at the Tokyo Olympics?"

Who will be the next Kipchoge? Everyone who followed the race is asking the question, but one man may have more of a say than most: Kipchoge's sports agent, Jos Hermens.

This silver-haired 67-year-old former Nike employee represents many of long-distance running's most famous stars and is also one of the investors in Pitsiladis's Sub2Hur project. Every year he travels to East Africa to scout new local running talent. Like many people involved in the project, Hermens loves to talk about genes, red blood cells, and the declining curve of marathon world records. He once said, "African runners aren't good at drinking [supplements] during races. They don't understand the importance of hydration, and they don't practice drinking water while running."

Yet like Kipchoge, many athletes from Kenya and Ethiopia seem to appear out of thin air. At 16 he burst onto the international scene, winning the 5,000-meter world championship in 2003, and from then on Kipchoge won everything from 1,500 meters to 42 kilometers. No scheduled hydration, no heart-rate monitor, no custom shoes, no army of scientists.

"If I'm not happy, I don't run," he said.
