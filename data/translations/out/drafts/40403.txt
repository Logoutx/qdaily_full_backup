What did the first wave of foreign investors, hotel operators, and architects who "dared to eat the crab" go through?

In 1982, I. M. Pei's experiment at Beijing's Fragrant Hills Park did not go smoothly.

At first, the Beijing municipal government chose a site for this Chinese-American master architect — the man who had single-handedly created the Hancock Tower — beside the Forbidden City, hoping to build a skyscraper-style hotel for the foreign tourists flooding in after China opened its doors. But Pei said he had no intention of disrupting the old city's urban fabric, and instead chose a site at Fragrant Hills Park, far from downtown. The client was not sold on the final hotel with its white walls and gray tiles — it didn't look "modern" at all.

Dale Keller was the interior designer of the Fragrant Hills Hotel, and his recollections were equally unhappy. The placement of the air conditioners was one of the sticking points in the standoff with the client. The client made it clear that all air conditioners had to be placed in conspicuous positions, so that anyone could see at a glance that this was a high-spec establishment. Dale, meanwhile, insisted on hiding these traces of technology and human intervention as much as possible, so that they would harmonize with Pei's overall Suzhou-garden design style.

All of this history became research material for Cole Roskam, an associate professor in the Department of Architecture at the University of Hong Kong. Four years ago he began a study tracing the international hotels that sprang up across China in the early reform-and-opening period and the people who created them.

As early as the beginning of the 1970s, as China regained its seat at the United Nations and Nixon visited China, signals of opening were released, and Chinese officials traveling abroad marveled at modernized living spaces. An ideal international hotel was a matter of building height, number of guest rooms, form, facade, modern appliances, or advanced construction techniques.

But more importantly, they were a signal. "The more modern and large-scale a project looked — the more it proved that this land had emerged from the shadow of the Cultural Revolution's catastrophe and begun the Four Modernizations — the more government support it received," Roskam told QDaily.

![Interior of the Fragrant Hills Hotel (Photo / Cole Roskam)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503110115wkNKoBzW5cmDSOLZ.jpg-w600)

### What should a modern "face" look like

In 1978, the number of inbound tourists to China reached over 1.8 million — more than the total for the previous twenty years combined — and in 1979 it surged to over 4.2 million. At the time, Beijing had only seven hotels authorized to receive foreigners, with just 1,000 beds meeting reception standards. People realized there was an urgent need to build a batch of hotels meeting international standards, but few knew what such a hotel should look like.

In 1979, the first three pilot projects introducing foreign investment were approved: No. 001 was China Aviation Food Company; No. 002 and No. 003 were the Beijing Jianguo Hotel and the Great Wall Hotel. The State Council set up a "Leading Group for Building Tourist Hotels with Overseas Chinese and Foreign Capital."

The international hotel was a relatively self-contained, controllable small space. Roskam called them "mini special economic zones," where commercial trade could proceed quietly in a comfortable, modernized, even cosmopolitan space — while ordinary people stopped at the gate had no idea what was happening inside. What they saw was only one modern marvel after another, towers so tall that looking up at them would send your hat tumbling off.

"What you have is, at best, a guesthouse, not a hotel!" Clement Chan Jr., the creator of the Jianguo Hotel, bluntly told Hou Xijiu, then deputy director of the Beijing Municipal Tourism Bureau, during an argument over management rights. The Chinese-American pointed out that Beijing's hotel facilities were backward and that there was no management experience to speak of. Roskam collected design sketches of the Beijing Hotel showing that domestic designers at the time had not even planned an independent bathroom for each guest room — the communal bathrooms were placed at the end of the corridors.

In the 1970s, Chan's San Francisco Chinatown hotel with 566 guest rooms had already become a port of call for mainland Chinese officials visiting the United States. "The Jianguo Hotel can almost be seen as a replica of the San Francisco one; in fact, what the client back then wanted was exactly that replica," Roskam said. The building's structure was not novel, and compared with the Beijing Hotel's east wing, the Jianguo Hotel was much smaller in scale.

But the Jianguo Hotel had first-class facilities for its time, including a swimming pool, fountains, and Justine's, Beijing's first French restaurant; every guest room came with a color TV and a dial telephone. "Chan knew very well the level of China's construction capability at the time, and he understood what Chinese officials wanted. The Jianguo Hotel's success lay in satisfying an entire set of imaginings about 'modernity.'"

The Jianguo Hotel was a huge success, with occupancy staying above 95% for a full year after opening. Representatives of multinational banks lived here and even set up offices on the first floor, and it was for a time called Beijing's "Wall Street."

Orville Schell vividly described the world behind the Jianguo Hotel's doors in a 1983 article for [The New York Times](https://www.google.co.jp/url?sa=t&rct=j&q=&esrc=s&source=web&cd=2&ved=0ahUKEwiC-7LH9dLTAhWBa7wKHW7sAoAQFggtMAE&url=http%3a%2f%2fwww%2enytimes%2ecom%2f1983%2f11%2f27%2ftravel%2fan-oasis-of-privilige-in-china%2ehtml%3fpagewanted%3dall&usg=AFQjCNGfqLRcFJNcWYIVL4njtJliewnsyw):

"Tapestries covered every wall; the guest rooms had matching curtains and bedsheets whose botanical prints made you think not of China but perhaps of distant Greenland. The bathrooms were clad in black-and-white marble mosaics from China — possibly the only trace reminding you that you were still in China. The king-size beds were imported from the United States; there were color TVs, individual air conditioning, dial telephones, and American-style chandeliers with golden chains. Sliding open the door onto the balcony, you could see the glinting spray of the fountain in the hotel garden's pond."

In Schell's words, when a doorman pushed open the hotel's glass door for you, "it was as if China had been left behind; you entered a completely new world." Blocked outside the door were not just China's streets, traffic, or air — but also people. "Private car drivers stood by the fountain waiting for tips, while nearby stood ordinary citizens, gazing through the floor-to-ceiling windows at the bright lobby within."

Another strand of Chinese officials' imaginings of modernity, besides skyscrapers and glass curtain walls, was the revolving restaurant that could complete one rotation per hour.

China's first revolving restaurant was realized at the Jinling Hotel in Nanjing — "Xuangong." It was the third building of its kind in the world, following the Hyatt Regency Atlanta designed and built by John Portman in 1967 and Hong Kong's Hopewell Centre in 1981.

Roskam tracked down a Hong Kong architect who had participated in the design. At a design coordination meeting, he noticed several "cooperation representatives" who had come from Beijing. During the meeting, the representatives tried hard to persuade everyone to lower the height of the Jinling Hotel and to drop the idea of a revolving restaurant. "They probably hoped that the most advanced technology and the record of China's tallest building would be born in Beijing, not Nanjing."

Xuangong sat on the 36th floor and was equipped with the country's first high-speed elevator; it took only 29 seconds to reach Xuangong from the ground floor. At first, like all international hotels, the Jinling Hotel was not open to the public. People queued to buy admission tickets for Xuangong, then were led by waiters onto the elevator to overlook the city from a height of 36 floors.

One of the more recent typical witnesses of this history is the Shanghai Centre, also known as the "Shanghai Portman."

The creator of the Portman was John Portman. When US-China relations were normalized in 1979, Deng Xiaoping, during his official visit to the United States, stayed at the Atlanta Westin Peachtree Plaza designed by John Portman — the latter being the tallest building in the world at the time. After that, John Portman opened his first international office in Hong Kong.

Roskam found the project's original design drafts in Portman's company archives. At first it was simply a hotel. But in later versions it became a "city within a city." After many rounds of revision, the final plan was a 152.1-meter-tall building in the shape of the Chinese character for "mountain." The eight-story podium contained a 1,500-seat theater, a three-level shopping plaza, 30,000 square meters of upscale office space, and an atrium exhibition hall. Without stepping out of the gates of this "micro-city," one could access almost every function a city offers inside the building itself — from residence, dining, retail, entertainment, commerce, and culture to offices. This string of "condensed urban experiences" was how modernity was defined for the Shanghai Portman.

The Shanghai Centre was originally planned for the Bund, but that site was dropped because of the area's relatively soft soil. In the end, officials and architects chose a site north of the Sino-Soviet Friendship Building, which is today's Shanghai Exhibition Centre. That building, begun in 1955, has a "Cold War" style, and the Shanghai Centre stands on a north-south axis with it.

"Officialdom was highly uncertain about this batch of international hotels, unable to judge to what extent these mini special economic zones would affect their neighborhoods and the wider urban space. Would these quiet changes catalyze urban transformation? It also posed a challenge to official management — no one could predict it," Roskam said.

### The architects' challenges and confusions

The Fragrant Hills Hotel was I. M. Pei's first design project after returning to China; when describing how much importance was attached to it, a team member said, "It was the first time I saw him take part in discussions at the sketch stage."

Pei, who had left China at 18 and only returned in his fifties, wanted the Fragrant Hills Hotel to pursue a "modernity" with value drawn from traditional Chinese architecture.

The Fragrant Hills Hotel consists of three- to four-story wings arranged around a central hall, neighboring cedar, cypress, and spruce trees that had grown for centuries. Roskam praised his courage in attempting "localness": "For Chinese people who had just been through the Cultural Revolution, talking about history and tradition was sensitive; anyone who had lived through the 'Destroy the Four Olds' campaign would reflexively associate this with feudal remnants."

Pei's borrowing from traditional Chinese architectural style sparked heated debate in architectural academia and the industry at the time, especially because he transplanted the architectural style of Jiangnan — the region south of the Yangtze — to the north. Some considered Pei's borrowing of imperial-era architectural language a breakthrough, a return to architectural history and traditional culture. The other camp argued that China's modernization needed technically more advanced and visually more striking architectural solutions, requiring the contribution of internationally renowned architects like Pei — though from today's vantage point, choosing to build a wholly different building in Beijing using the aesthetic system of a Suzhou garden can hardly count as "local."

"You have to remember, this batch of buildings was born in a very sensitive period, the early days of reform and opening-up, when the Party had its reformists and relative conservatives arguing endlessly over the speed, scale, and methods of reform," Roskam said.

![I. M. Pei in front of a model of the Suzhou Museum (Photo / PBS)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503123340iV5LNehdCDRo8a4s.jpg-w600)

But the more practical challenge may have been whether the architect's vision could be realized at all.

Language was one problem. Roskam interviewed an American who had worked on the Great Wall Hotel and was employed by one of the investors, Unison Group; his fluent Chinese made him the translator between the American architects from Becket and the Chinese construction crews. At the time, there were many people like this American serving as dedicated "translators" for international hotel construction communication.

Language barriers could be overcome, but cultural differences and different construction techniques also posed obstacles. Roskam cited the process of laying wiring: the American side would first lay the conduits and then cover them with concrete, whereas Chinese workers at the time would first pour the cement, then dig open a channel to bury the wiring when it was time to install it, and finally seal the cement again.

In fact, the original design for the Great Wall Hotel, built between 1981 and 1984, had a revolving restaurant on its top floor — long before the Jinling Hotel's design — but Chinese workers at the time lacked the technical capability, and after repeated failed attempts it was ultimately abandoned. Both sides were deeply frustrated and could only give up.

"Bringing American workers onto construction sites in China would have been far too sensitive," Roskam said.

### The world behind the doors of the international hotels

The international hotels of this historical period were, rather than a service industry, a proving ground. These modern interiors, hotel facilities, and the business activity conducted within could all happen quietly indoors, unknown to ordinary people — the international hotels of this era all adopted strict access control; those who could pass through the gates were foreigners and officials, or investors from Hong Kong and Singapore.

Roskam once interviewed a journalist who was born and raised in Nanjing. She recalled that as a child she made a special trip with her parents across the city just to see this tall building; they knew there was a revolving restaurant at the top, but they could not enter the hotel's lobby.

![Clement Chan Jr., founder of the Jianguo Hotel (Photo / Shanghai Center of Photography)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705031248033lkB7ypA5KRtUvTW.jpg-w600)

Schell called the Jianguo Hotel, so tightly guarded by its gatekeepers, an "oasis of privilege" ("An Oasis of Privilege in China"). He interviewed Clement Chan Jr., who admitted frankly over the phone: "If I opened the doors, thousands of people would pour in just to drink a cup of tea, and then they would sit around idly, enjoying the free air conditioning all day. There's no way to run a first-class international hotel like that."

Roskam once cited a 1981 report in Beijing Daily, "Beijing Hotel persists in anti-corruption education," which recorded that while training Chinese staff, the Beijing Friendship Hotel reminded employees to strengthen their "immunity," particularly with regard to items left behind by guests in their rooms, such as "pornographic publications" like *Playboy*.

![Pierre Cardin on the cover of Time magazine](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2017050312574185LBjA0Fz6tDHPd2.jpeg-w600)

On one hand, the government tried to create visual symbols of modernity; on the other, it hoped to hide the negative effects brought by reform. "But the Chinese people could hardly fail to notice the spatial and social changes amid the thunderous clamor around them, and many gradually grew dissatisfied at being shut out of these new colossi."

The Jianguo Hotel was promoted as an ideal model; in 1984, People's Daily reported on the hotel in a front-page headline story, analyzing its operating model and praising the hotel's creator for successfully inviting Hong Kong's Peninsula Group to provide professional staff training and hotel management. The National Tourism Administration then launched a massive nationwide "hotels learn from Jianguo" campaign.

International celebrities were the earliest guests of the international hotels. Nixon, Andy Warhol, Elizabeth Taylor, and Gregory Peck all stayed at the Beijing Hotel. In 1981, Pierre Cardin held his first public fashion show here. Two years later, he set up a "branch" of Paris's Maxim's restaurant on the second floor of the Chongwenmen Hotel. Before that, Beijing had only Russian-style restaurants such as "Lao Mo" and the Xinqiao Hotel. A full French dinner at Maxim's cost over a hundred yuan, while an ordinary citizen's monthly salary at the time was only a few dozen yuan.

![Pierre Cardin at Maxim's restaurant (Photo / Shanghai Center of Photography)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503105954vrQUSOBMjiRsxecg.jpg-w600)

Beijing's international hotels were the sounding ground for rock musicians of the era; someone quipped that "rock doesn't reach the people; rock only reaches Maxim's." The "Cui Jians" of those early years performed here, and foreign tourists were their earliest audience.

The Guangzhou White Swan Hotel, invested in by Henry Fok with US$50 million, was an exception — it was described as "close to the people."

Its 1,000 guest rooms sat within a diamond-shaped structure containing a complex three-story atrium with an indoor waterfall, plus pavilions and ponds in imperial-era traditional style. The first hotel of the early reform-and-opening period designed entirely by Chinese architects, it also introduced what was then an advanced computerized hotel management system.

The White Swan Hotel opened to the public relatively early; some people even made special trips just to see its brand-new bathrooms. Roskam curated an exhibition, "Space Reform: International Hotels in China 1974–1990," at the University of Hong Kong's Shanghai study center, where he showed a 1986 White Swan Hotel wedding invitation he had scavenged from channels like Kongfz. By the mid-1980s, the hotel had already opened to the public and rented out its venues for events.

![The White Swan Hotel in Guangzhou (Photo / Cole Roskam)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503111437fT5gBO0r9WHAqeYi.jpg-w600)

![The "homeland landscape" in the atrium of the White Swan Hotel (Photo / Wikipedia)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503111458gETvktX1ORxGVSPB.JPG-w600)

In 1991, American citizens were allowed to adopt children in China. Because the White Swan Hotel was close to the US Consulate General in Guangzhou, many adoptive parents chose to stay here for the convenience of handling the paperwork. The White Swan thus came to be known as the "Baby Hotel." The hotel also partnered with the multinational toy company Mattel, placing baby strollers in adoptive parents' rooms and setting up a children's play area in the hotel's public spaces.

### Later, they were forgotten

Thirty years later, the international hotels that once kept people queuing outside their gates seem to have been forgotten. You can still book these hotels on sites like Trip.com Group and TripAdvisor, but the most frequent words in their reviews are "old-fashioned," "dated facilities," and "arrogant service."

The flaws of early construction technology often brought high maintenance and renovation costs. I. M. Pei's signature Hancock Tower once gave rise to a peculiar maintenance crew because of its falling glass curtain walls: workers stood on the street, using binoculars to "monitor" the color changes of each glass panel in order to predict and promptly replace problematic glass.

![The damaged glass curtain wall of Boston's Hancock Tower (Photo / The Globe Collection)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503130647MFpdJhwbfrAtIB3E.jpg-w600)

The first skyscraper-style international hotels were born in China before most cities had issued fire codes for high-rise buildings; the White Swan Hotel was at one point ordered to make fire-safety rectifications. Some international hotels rated as immovable cultural relics could not easily add underground parking garages, and when renovation and reconstruction came, they found that the original drawings had not been preserved, or that the drawings differed enormously from the final built result.

The international hotel boom was accompanied by the training of hotel management talent. In its first five years, the Jianguo Hotel was managed by Hong Kong's Peninsula Group, which also trained its staff. As early as 1980, the first group of five government-sponsored students majoring in hotel management went to a tourism management college in Hawaii to study. In 1982, the first hotel-run training school in Chinese hotel history, the "Lido Training School," was officially founded, enrolling 530 students in its first cohort. After two years of training, the first graduates went out to "break new ground" for hotels nationwide.

The Lido Hotel was built with an investment of 80 million yuan by Luo Xinquan, a wealthy Singaporean Chinese businessman, with additional investment later bringing the total to 200 million yuan. In 1994, Luo Xinquan transferred the Lido to Li Ka-shing. This was the template for almost all international hotels: foreign or overseas Chinese capital brought the money, the Chinese government provided labor, land, or part of the funds, and architects provided the technical support.

But on the whole, vocational hotel training in mainland China remained neglected and undervalued for a long time. The Fragrant Hills Hotel was managed by the Chinese government; the Jianguo Hotel invited the Peninsula's professional management team to handle staff training and management. The Great Wall Hotel was the same.

As China joined the WTO, the first batch of Sino-foreign joint-venture international hotels were nearly defenseless against the proactive international hotel giants and domestic brands. More than thirty hotel management groups — Holiday Inn, Sheraton, Hilton, Accor, Shangri-La, Peninsula, and Hyatt among them — had entered the Chinese market as early as the late 1980s; they were more flexible, and more aggressive.

From then on, the international hotels were constrained by the system. Most were joint ventures, roughly 51% Chinese-owned and 49% foreign-owned. The joint-venture contracts ran 10 to 20 years, after which control would be brought back under state ownership through negotiation. Whether they could keep pace with the market varied from one hotel to another.

![The Ritz Bar & Lounge designed by Gensler for the Shanghai Portman (Photo / Gensler)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503131002lcoZrYpxaekm1KS6.jpg-w600)

The former Shanghai Centre, now The Portman Ritz-Carlton, Shanghai, invited Gensler in 2016 to design the "Ritz Bar & Lounge" in the hotel lobby, hoping this internal public space could attract visitors — yet on Trip.com Group you can still see people criticizing the dated guest room facilities beneath the glamour of the public spaces.

The Fragrant Hills Hotel's fate was more desolate. Because of its historical background, it still hosts large numbers of conferences; the Beijing Tourism Bureau lacked proper management and failed to maintain it well, and Pei never set foot again in the hotel he had followed from the sketch stage onward.

Such was the end of the international hotels that once represented openness and controversy.
