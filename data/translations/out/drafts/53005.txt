ACE Hotel, One of the World's Trendiest Hotels — How Did This Business Come Together?
@@QD_EXCERPT@@

@@QD_BODY@@
"Look at how the most interesting people in different cities live, and whether anything in it can be turned into a business idea."

Ryan Bukstein, the 38-year-old director of marketing and public relations, stepped onto the 10-meter-wide MINDPARK stage, ready to talk about a topic verging on the metaphysical: how culture can make money.

He wore a black MUJI jacket, black straight-leg jeans and black canvas shoes, the collar of his white shirt casually unbuttoned. The outfit bore some resemblance to that of his late former boss, Alex Calderwood, co-founder of ACE Hotel. People used to say of Calderwood's attire at industry forums — a necktie at most — that it looked both cooperative and cool: just like the secret of ACE's success.

ACE is one of the trendiest hotels in the world. It has 9 locations globally (Seattle, Portland, New York, Palm Springs, Los Angeles, London, Pittsburgh, New Orleans and Chicago), 8 of them in the United States, the country where it was born. In sheer scale, ACE is not a big brand. But its fame far outstrips its size. ACE has more than 150,000 followers on Instagram. In 2016, Monocle magazine ranked ACE third among "favorite hotel groups," behind only Four Seasons and Mandarin Oriental — both hotel groups founded in the 1960s, with Four Seasons alone operating more than 100 hotels worldwide.

The price of this "number three" hotel is not cheap, but not outrageously expensive either. At the New York location, a bunk-bed room costs US$169 a night, while a suite runs about US$800 a night. [New Yorkers consider that an acceptable price.](https://www.nytimes.com/2011/01/30/fashion/30ACE.html?pagewanted=all&_r=0) In London, ACE's most basic single room costs £254 a night.

[The Guardian offered a wonderfully precise description of its guests](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2014/mar/07/alex-calderwood-cool-hunter-death-ace-hotel-london): young, or at least quite young, carefully dressed in casual clothes.

Although Alex Calderwood always insisted that ACE was not just for the coolest, most fashionable people, it could hardly escape labels like these: retro minimalist design, live pop bands playing in the lobby, a lobby that doubles as an open-plan workspace where you can also order a drink, and, incidentally, a shop selling the most independently spirited local small brands. That is what ACE is.

When Ryan Bukstein threw a batch of meticulously shot photos onto MINDPARK's giant screen, it was, in the end, to prove exactly this — the ACE in Los Angeles has a 600-seat theater — in Chicago, people love the ACE Hotel's rooftop — startups and freelancers have occupied the lobby of the ACE in New York — the Kyoto ACE, opening in 2019, will house three restaurants. The photos flipped by quickly, and the audience was submerged in a new kind of urban life. And ACE is the guide to that new life.

1.

People often mistakenly believe that ACE started in Portland, Oregon. This mistake is not without reason.

The first ACE opened in Seattle in 1999, a small inn. When the founders bought the building, it still stank, its walls yellowed and covered with nothing but stains and profanity. After being converted into the ACE, it still retained a welcoming, budget-friendly air, with bunk-bed dorms sitting next to luxury suites, the cheapest going for just US$65 a night. Bands passing through Seattle to perform sometimes chose to stay here. The rooms were furnished with well-chosen secondhand pieces, and Shepard Fairey decorated them — back then he was still a nobody; he only became famous in 2008 for his Obama campaign poster blending the styles of Andy Warhol and Che Guevara.

![The ACE Hotel in Seattle, [image from the ACE Hotel official website](https://www.acehotel.com/seattle)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180509154353Nno01UJCQ4ORyezk.jpg-w600)

Although a number of travel and design magazines covered this ACE and praised it for breaking the boundary between star-rated hotels and cheap motels, the other two partners besides Alex Calderwood — Wade Weigel and Doug Herrick — never really wanted to get into the hotel business. They just thought it would be fun.

Eight years later, a proper boutique hotel, the ACE, appeared in Portland, offering 79 guest rooms. It was also there that ACE acquired its widely reported turntable and record library. ACE was different from what it had been — more refined, more accessible to the general public. "At the start we knew nothing about the hotel business; we just went on instinct and invested very little money. When we did the second hotel in Portland, we started paying attention, as if we were building a global brand," Ryan Bukstein said in an interview with QDaily this past April.

The globalization of traditional hotel brands owes to standardized operations and an asset-light model. The former means fast replication and lower costs. The latter can be understood as third-party companies investing in and owning the hotels, while the hotel brand provides standardized, trustworthy service and management, profiting from management fees and a share of performance.

The new player ACE followed the second of these hotel-industry conventions. ACE holds almost no property of its own, though it always manages to find buildings that are already full of stories. ACE's third hotel sits in a 1900s-era building in New York, the result of a partnership between ACE and the veteran hotel investment developer GFI. [GFI is keen on converting well-located old buildings in Manhattan, New York, into trendy hotels; an 1883 historic building on Beekman Street, near the World Trade Center and the Brooklyn Bridge, was also their handiwork.](http://gficap.com/development-news/gfi-development-gb-lodging-secure-350m-in-financing-for-manhattans-the-beekman) Immediately after the New York project, it was again GFI that partnered to open the ACE resort in Palm Springs. The resort has 179 guest rooms and 5 acres of outdoor grounds.

In cases of renovating old buildings, ACE usually does not have to handle the hotel's facade design. But in the newest Kyoto project, ACE brought in Kengo Kuma, a Japanese designer famous around the world, who has not done many hotel projects — his first hotel project was The Opposite House in Beijing. "Kengo Kuma and we have been friends for more than a decade." Indeed, ACE likes to work with friends. "He is a designer who can create both the new and the old at the same time. At first it feels very new and very special, but ten years later it still feels like part of the city, as if something in it is eternal," Ryan Bukstein said. ACE's founder Alex Calderwood had always tried to do the same.

"But you haven't seen the hotel's interior design yet. It's very Japanese, but also very modern."

This is ACE's strength, and the thing people look forward to most about it. It completely breaks the other rule of the hotel business: standardized operations. Ryan restated this conviction of the ACE team: "If every location repeats itself, you lose the brand. I know it's funny, because other brands do exactly the opposite." "Our definition of ACE is that it is special and ever-changing," [Alex Calderwood said in a 2012 interview with HYPEBEAST.](https://hypebeast.com/2012/10/the-ace-hotel-share-their-philosophy-and-preview-their-new-reigning-champ-project)

In its two most recently opened hotels, ACE tried two diametrically opposed styles. In New Orleans, ACE worked with the New York architecture and interior design firm Roman and Williams to try out a classically ornate Art Deco style, with retro oversized refrigerators and wardrobes faced with oil paintings.

In the guest rooms of the Chicago hotel, by contrast, ACE teamed up with the Los Angeles studio Commune Design to recreate a modern, functional Bauhaus style — clean lines, large blocks of color, huge floor-to-ceiling windows that were originally meant to show off breakthroughs in modern building technology. Bauhaus originated in Europe, but Americans embraced it first, and Chicago was one of the important places for it — home to the block-like Willis Tower and John Hancock Center.

"We create a new story based on history," Ryan Bukstein said.

2.

Some people love it, while others are skeptical of the buzz ACE puts on display. [The New York Times interviewed people who hate ACE](https://www.nytimes.com/2011/01/30/fashion/30ACE.html?pagewanted=all&_r=0) — though they, too, were ACE guests.

One pair of guests at the New York ACE complained that on weekend nights there were so many people in the place that they had to show their room key just to get in. Some guests were even mistakenly turned away. This was a complaint that the overly open and inviting lobby culture was encroaching on the space of actual paying guests — but ACE has always been proud of the crowds at its New York location, claiming that startups and freelancers all stay put and won't leave, held fast by its long communal tables and its bar drinks.

Another couple who complained to The New York Times said they hated ACE — too trendy — yet they happened to love its special whiskey, mixed with a dash of house-made pickle juice; no pickle juice anywhere is better than ACE's. Data provided by Ryan shows that 35%–60% of ACE's revenue comes from drinks and food.

Still, some people worry about ACE's service. After the aforementioned MINDPARK talk, an audience member asked: "ACE Hotel focuses on local culture, art and lifestyle, values design and product merchandising, and does event marketing — as a hotel, won't it end up emphasizing design while neglecting service?"

Ryan answered: "For us, everything is connected. From the moment you walk into our hotel, everything is designed. Service is the most important thing, but it is both important and not important." "Having only beautiful rooms is not enough; we also have to fill the space, make people want to stay in it, and give everyone in it a sense of connection. That is our standard, and we can apply this standard in different spaces," Ryan added in a subsequent interview.

ACE's distinctive service is indeed reflected in the creation of its spaces rather than in basic hardware and facilities. Although the hotel industry has talked for years about the concept of "destination travel" — hoping people come for the hotel itself rather than for some attraction outside it — very few actually pull it off. The usual practice is to build the hotel in a tourist city dense with attractions, and inside the hotel, fabricate an acceptable locally styled resort, plus a few modern touches — say, a bar, or a jazz band performing in the lobby in the evening. That is exactly what Sheraton did when it opened a resort hotel in Xishuangbanna a few years ago. Homestays do much the same.

Urban life itself, in their view, seems to hold little fun for travelers. In this respect, some of ACE's methods, though old-fashioned, still make a major difference. First of all, they differ in site selection: they want the hotel to be a "node" that makes it easy for people to explore the city. At the New York ACE, guests are thus able to achieve what Ryan calls "flowing between work and play" in the lobby, coming and going, stopping and starting.

In Los Angeles, ACE opened on the site of the former United Artists Theatre — an old Gothic Revival building. ACE reactivated the theater, programming shows with ticket prices ranging from free to expensive. "This helps us attract more people," Ryan said.

The London project is the best example. The lobby of the ACE in Shoreditch, London, sells locally made products. But what moves people more easily is the hotel's entrance: you have to pass through a flower shop. [Alex Calderwood once lavished praise on the flower shop's owner in an interview.](http://www.coolhunting.com/travel/ace-hotel-london-shoreditch) He could recite her name. "Hattie Fox," Alex said. "She is a truly wonderful woman. She is eccentric, has a special take on life — what to do in which season, which flowers to use. She is just so very British."

Before taking on the London project, Alex would stay in this very building whenever he came to London — it used to be a Crowne Plaza hotel. Alex had a habit of wandering around cities, exploring shops. "Look at how the most interesting people in different cities live, and whether anything in it can be turned into a business idea," [Lovejoy, a friend of Alex Calderwood's for many years, said in an interview with The Guardian.](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2014/mar/07/alex-calderwood-cool-hunter-death-ace-hotel-london) He would scribble on sticky notes, shoot a pile of photos, and toss them all to Atelier ACE. This is the in-house creative team ACE established after opening its New York location; they are responsible for participating in the design of ACE hotels, the design of merchandise, and the search for partners. In 2012, the renovation of the London project formally began.

Alex could almost name where every product was bought. And these sellers and companies were not anonymous names on some mature hotel supply chain — they all had some distinctive quality: a century-old local brand, for instance, or a firm that used to mainly supply bolts and gears to factories, with no one ever imagining their products could add freshness to a hotel. Take the cork ceiling in the London ACE — cheaper than other ceiling treatments, but just as striking. Freshness is not always expensive. ACE found local craftspeople to make special rolled-skin copper handles.

![The ACE Hotel in London, [image from the official website](https://www.acehotel.com/london)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180509160509dMOG2Ll9DtAk38qv.jpg-w600)

![[Image from CoolHunting](http://www.coolhunting.com/travel/ace-hotel-london-shoreditch)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180509160611SJUZMhe0YTanfVm6.jpg-w600)

When ACE combines all these things, it makes the big companies' stylized intentions look insincere — or, to be precise, ACE has found a crowd of people who are even more demanding about style.

3.

ACE was Alex Calderwood's childhood nickname, and he applied it to the hotel business among his many ventures.

Alex Calderwood was not someone who only wanted to succeed in the hotel business. He grew up in Seattle and did many things when he was young. Working as a manager in a clothing store, he would give the store's interior a peculiar style. In 1993, he opened a men's barbershop called Rudy's, a traditional barbershop — even then he showed an aesthetic of "young old-school": he bought traditional barber chairs, laid tile floors, but played loud music and covered the walls with bar flyers. Rudy's now has more than a dozen branches across the United States. In 1999, before opening ACE, he also opened a bar in Seattle and became well known in the Seattle music scene. This helped promote ACE's first location.

![The Portland branch of Rudy's, [image from the Rudy's official website](https://rudysbarbershop.com/blogs/locations/pearl-district-portland-barbershop)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180509161026lqyHvK0kdp9U7fc6.jpg-w600)

Ryan Bukstein, today's director of public relations, met Alex in 1999, when he was a 19-year-old intern who, like Alex, loved music and wanted to make it his career. Alex was 33. He told Ryan that if they ran a hotel, they could also do music. Later, ACE rendered that sentence into their now-familiar slogan: the hotel is a platform. When asked about MUJI and IKEA both planning to get into hotels, Ryan answered with exactly this slogan: "They are going from lifestyle brands into hotel brands; our path is different. We were a lifestyle brand from the start — we just use the hotel as a platform."

Regrettably, Alex Calderwood died in 2013, not long after the London ACE Hotel opened. It was not a natural death; [he had previously disclosed to The New York Times that he was addicted to alcohol.](https://www.nytimes.com/2011/01/30/fashion/30ACE.html?pagewanted=all&_r=0) [Some of his former colleagues and friends said in later interviews](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2014/mar/07/alex-calderwood-cool-hunter-death-ace-hotel-london) that ACE was growing so fast that the staff were all exhausted — Alex most of all. Alex's early partners Wade Weigel and Doug Herrick had already sold their shares to Alex in 2012. "We have a lot of big bosses now; the life of ACE has already changed," [Weigel said in a media interview.](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2014/mar/07/alex-calderwood-cool-hunter-death-ace-hotel-london)

They also mentioned the pressure developers were putting on ACE — a warning, as it were, to industry disruptors: ACE, though popular, might still have very little say.

When Ryan Bukstein was asked about developers' and investors' attitude toward ACE, he said: "I think a lot of developers have seen that the future emphasis is on getting people involved. They come to ACE because they feel we can do that. But as for whether they understand how we do it — sometimes they do, sometimes they don't. Both." Ryan believes they still have a lot to learn in business, and that they have to learn to convince their backers.

But some more aggressive businessmen may only want to borrow ACE's trendy reputation. ACE's plans in Japan were therefore shelved for many years. Alex had wanted to open a hotel in Japan more than 20 years earlier. "Since the ACE Portland, we've been very popular in Japan; a lot of Japanese people came to us and invited us, but it still took that long, because we wanted control," Ryan said. But most Asian developers only wanted ACE to license its brand. That went on until they found NTT, Nippon Telegraph and Telephone.

The new ACE Kyoto project is located in the Shinpukan mall in central Kyoto. The building's predecessor was the former Kyoto Central Telephone Office, completed in 1926. In 2001, NTT's urban development division converted it into a mall, but it closed in 2016 for renovation.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180509161326eVlqcoyhJNwQ3zKp.jpg-w600)

![Rendering of the ACE Hotel Kyoto, [image from Fashionsnap](https://www.fashionsnap.com/article/2018-04-07/kuma-acehotel/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180509161345ZJ9vo72khAQTUGpq.jpg-w600)

Ace Hotel will occupy the second and third floors of the existing Shinpukan building, as well as floors 2 through 7 of a new building on one side of it, with a total of 213 guest rooms, and will take on the responsibility of reactivating the building and the surrounding businesses.

4.

In the future, ACE plans to open two or three hotels a year. This will be no small challenge: even excluding the first Seattle location, ACE has opened only 8 hotels in the past 11 years. And a new hotel usually takes two or three years, or longer, to turn a profit. Ryan said in an interview with QDaily that ACE's investment today is several million dollars more than in the past. For now, though, no investors have been brought in.

ACE's other challenges include whether, after Alex Calderwood's death, it can remain what it was. ACE's emphasis on experience — could it ultimately degenerate into just another kind of shallow consumption? "We have to be very careful."

ACE also cares a lot about being copied. Nowadays more and more hotels want to build an urban social hub, and they all seem to have effortlessly learned on Instagram what counts as trendy design. Ryan mentioned "copying" many times in the interview, but in the end he concluded: "It is something intangible that sets us apart from other hotels. It's like those bands that cover the Beatles — you can tell at a listen that they are not the Beatles."
