Farewell, Knight-Errant Jin.

On the afternoon of October 30, 2018, Louis Cha — the wuxia novelist known by the pen name Jin Yong, founder of Hong Kong's *Ming Pao*, and a member of the committee that drafted Hong Kong's Basic Law — died of illness at the age of 94.

Bao Shu (whose other pen name is Xingyuan Ping), the author who once paid tribute to Jin Yong with *A Cambridge History of the Heaven Sword and Dragon Saber* (《剑桥倚天屠龙记》), felt "very surprised." Recalling many of Jin Yong's novels, he said he was "at a loss." "Jin Yong, to me, is youth. He is my boyhood."

Hong Kong writer Ma Ka-fai was in class when he heard the news. "Just hearing it, strangely, I immediately thought of Ni Kuang, Uncle Kuang. They've been old friends for so many years, and Uncle Kuang isn't in good health either; he's getting on in years. He's very carefree, but I think he must be sad inside."

Wang Xiaolei, author of the column "Liu Shen Lei Lei Reads Jin Yong," was "a bit at a loss" when he learned the news. Phone calls came in one after another, but he didn't dare answer, feeling he hadn't thought things through. "I paced back and forth, not knowing where to sit, and in the end plopped down on the toilet. I felt the toilet gave me a sense of security."

Nick Frisch, a PhD student in East Asian studies at Yale's Graduate School, had conducted Jin Yong's last-ever interview four years earlier. Responding to the news, he said Jin Yong's works count among the most treasured products of twentieth-century Chinese culture. "Jin Yong was a uniquely gifted genius, who on one hand revered the aesthetic sensibilities of traditional culture, and on the other created a very fresh, very modern narrative style."

Fu Guoyong, author of *A Biography of Jin Yong*, was "taking a walk in the night" at the time. He recalled a sweltering summer fifteen years earlier, when Jin Yong designed his own epitaph ("Here lies a man who, in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, wrote dozens of wuxia novels that were loved by hundreds of millions of people"). "Now, he is truly gone. There is no doubt this is something worth grieving. This is the end of an era."

Hong Kong Sanatorium & Hospital — the place where "an era ended" — was where Louis Cha passed away. Local Hong Kong reporters had long gathered there, followed by mainland correspondents stationed in Hong Kong; some arrived still in their workout clothes, heading straight over as soon as they heard the news. Then, in batches, mainland reporters who happened to be passing through Hong Kong — for family visits, vacations, or other assignments — also showed up. After 9 p.m., the crowd pressed in. Testing lights, testing sound, the scene was chaotic.

The media got no further news out of the hospital, and on social networks information keyed to "Jin Yong" began to explode. People recalled the first time they read Jin Yong's works and thanked him for laying a solid, sound set of values for them; artists who had appeared in Jin Yong wuxia adaptations thanked him for giving them an indelible role in their careers.

Jin Yong spent nearly his whole life living on one cultural island or another, yet harbored the deepest "Greater China" complex; the success of his newspapers and novels was owed to Hong Kong's commercial society, yet single-handedly he built a world of swift vengeance and chivalric joy utterly at odds with that society.

Throughout his life Jin Yong always showed the world two faces, shifting between conservative and radical, engagement with the world and withdrawal from it, serving all under heaven and cultivating oneself, between a Guo Jing-style devotion to nation and home and a Wei Xiaobao-style drifting through a fleeting world. He built an extraordinarily rich repository from which anyone could draw, yet each person could read a different world out of it.

Seen across his life, most of Jin Yong's achievements were built during Hong Kong's strange, fast-growing era, Fu Guoyong said: "There is no doubt he was also part of the Hong Kong myth, part of the Hong Kong miracle."

### **The Mainland and Hong Kong**

Jin Yong was born in 1924 in Haining, Zhejiang, into a family of scholar-gentry. But he didn't enjoy a stable life for long; the whole family was swept up in social upheaval. After the War of Resistance against Japan began, his mother fell ill and died while fleeing. His father died in the class struggle after 1949. Jin Yong had already been transferred to work in Hong Kong in 1948; he did not witness his father's execution and thereby escaped the catastrophe.

Before leaving the mainland for Hong Kong, Jin Yong had been punished at least twice. Once, in 1941, he satirized the school's director of studies as a capitulationist collaborator with Japan, writing a piece titled *Alice's Travels* (《阿丽丝漫游记》) for the wall newspaper, and was expelled. The other time, in 1944, he was kicked out of the Department of Diplomacy at the Central Political Institute in Chongqing for complaining about the misconduct of student party members.

Keen sensitivity to current affairs and forceful responses remained one of Jin Yong's defining traits for more than thirty years afterward.

Fu Guoyong believes these early experiences also made Jin Yong conversant with both Chinese and Western traditions. And he was very lucky to have encountered "an environment of press freedom full of possibility."

Jin Yong first went to Hong Kong as a newspaperman. In 1946 he joined Shanghai's *Ta Kung Pao* as an international wire translator. It was his first formal newspaper job; before that he had worked as a temporary field reporter at Hangzhou's *Southeast Daily*. In 1948 he was transferred to the *Ta Kung Pao*'s Hong Kong branch.

In 1952 he moved to the *New Evening Post* to edit its supplement. In Hong Kong newspapers of the 1950s and 1960s, the supplement was an important tool for boosting circulation, yet it also became the place where many Hong Kong writers shot to fame. Liu Yichang then edited "Repulse Bay" in the *Hong Kong Times*; his novel *The Drunkard* was adapted by Wong Kar-wai into *In the Mood for Love* and *2046*, and Tony Leung's Chow Mo-wan is a Hong Kong newspaperman of the 1960s.

It was during the *New Evening Post* period that the pen name Jin Yong was first used. Jin Yong's *The Book and the Sword* and Liang Yusheng's *The Dragon and Tiger of Beijing* caused a sensation. When Liang Yusheng died in 2009, Jin Yong wrote a mourning couplet: "A senior colleague of the same year, both madcap and chivalrous and literary — a fine friend."

In 1959, when Jin Yong founded *Ming Pao*, he carried on this model of running a paper. The supplement and serialized fiction mattered. He serialized *Return of the Condor Heroes* in *Ming Pao*, a huge success. He personally selected the writers, and no one else had the final say. It proved that, although he had many criticized aspects of management, he recognized talent and knew how to use people. Writer Chip Tsao described this in a video program a month earlier: "When he stopped writing, he immediately found Gu Long. Pushed him to the top-right corner of the page, found Yunjun, the best illustrator. Wen Rui'an was first put in the bottom-left corner, to see how it went; if it worked, then pushed to the top-right corner. He kept looking for successors."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181031091516oBELhZr8T3qdGs7F.jpg-w600)

These writers wrote novels but also essays and editorials, all of which he read personally, though he usually offered no revisions. Writers including Eunice Lam and Ni Kuang, author of the *Wisely Series*, were deeply impressed by this. In an article in 2010, Eunice Lam wrote that even for editorials on major current affairs, Jin Yong gave writers complete freedom. He was happy to publish all kinds of viewpoints in *Ming Pao*, but he could not accept total silence on them.

His close friend, 83-year-old Ni Kuang, said on a video program a month earlier, chatting with Chip Tsao about Jin Yong, that Jin Yong was extremely cautious when playing cards — he could sit for half an hour without touching a card. Ni Kuang was the opposite, known as "Ni the Follower." Jin Yong did things fearing no trouble, meticulous, by the book. He not only revised his own works over and over but also widely solicited others' opinions. Two Taoist priests came down from Mount Qingcheng to question Jin Yong: why do you always write Mount Qingcheng as the bad guys? Jin Yong said, I'll change it, I'll change it. Ni Kuang said, if it were me, I'd say I just like writing Mount Qingcheng as the bad guys. Does Mount Qingcheng have no bad people?

"He wasn't the rash sort of person," Fu Guoyong said.

Wang Xiaolei believes there are two dimensions to understanding Jin Yong: one is the pragmatist, who did not support overly radical, unrealistic things and would not meet force with force. This was determined by his identity. He was part of the upper strata of society, a decision-maker, and his words had to be responsible. But he was at the same time a humanitarian, and therefore, in the 1967 riots, he did not hesitate to debate the leftists. Freedom mattered greatly to Jin Yong. "Only a humanitarian can write first-rate wuxia. Only a humanitarian opposes totalitarianism, sympathizes with life, and pities us mortals," Wang Xiaolei said.

Jin Yong was of course also a very successful novelist. *Ming Pao* became the first Hong Kong media outlet to secure an exclusive interview with Deng Xiaoping, reportedly because Deng enjoyed reading Jin Yong's novels. Jin Yong's fiction brought everyone together, though each person read something different out of it. Ma Ka-fai believes Jin Yong's novels were one of the earliest sources of national feeling for a segment of Hong Kong people, and also a source of emotional education in adolescence. Ma Ka-fai lives in Wan Chai, and he says he deliberately restrains himself from becoming lost in Jin Yong's world.

Hong Kong's famous female politician Regina Ip is a Jin Yong fan, because she loves the love stories in his works. American journalist Nick Frisch, in a piece for *The New Yorker*, pointed out that *Legend of the Condor Heroes*, written in the late 1950s, resonates completely with Jin Yong's generation of Hong Kong people — whether to flee south and become emigrants, while enduring the pain of losing their homeland.

It is the same for those who were forbidden to read Jin Yong. In the 1970s, Jin Yong's works were banned on the mainland, because some believed the novels were full of political allegory. The same ban was in place in Taiwan at the time.

After the ban was lifted, Jin Yong became hugely popular on the mainland, and he and Qiong Yao became the popular novelists young people of the time had to read. These novels were suffused with traditional elements and morality that had been fiercely suppressed in the mainland's political campaigns, along with deft modern fiction technique. The story of the Wan Chai boy reading Jin Yong keeps repeating: some let themselves go and failed their exams; those who hoped to exercise restraint read a few volumes and dared not continue.

After countless film and TV adaptations, no one doesn't know what a smooth-talking, love-struck fool Wei Xiaobao is, and when people describe a man as being like Yang Guo, everyone knows it is not entirely a compliment — it may be an accusation of being sentimental, of constantly tangling himself in dilemmas. Ma Ka-fai calls this phenomenon Jin Yong as a linguistic symbol of Chinese culture.

![The 1995 TV series adaptation of Return of the Condor Heroes](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181031091559P7uQowaGAW6ZXbe4.jpg-w600)

Employees at Alibaba are required to pick a character from Jin Yong's novels to represent themselves and share one name as their codename within the company. Jack Ma's own name is Feng Qingyang, an unrivaled master in *The Smiling, Proud Wanderer*.

Jin Yong stopped writing new novels from 1972 onward, but he kept endlessly revising his past works. Wei Xiaobao was an important target of revision, because he had been criticized too often as morally deficient.

Jin Yong's characters became different from before. Before entertainment stars all marched into astronomy to name asteroids, Jin Yong's name in 2001 became that of minor planet No. 10930, granted by China's National Astronomical Observatories, honoring his "outstanding talent and great achievements in carrying forward traditional Chinese culture and advancing the development of contemporary Chinese literature, as well as his contributions to the nation's development and the world's cultural undertakings."

He had a stint teaching at mainland universities. But the experience was not happy; critics argued he lacked academic and teaching ability. He later left the History Department of Zhejiang University. Dong Jian, dean of the School of Humanities at Nanjing University, believed Jin Yong's resignation "ended a mismatch."

Ma Ka-fai believes this experience should not be mocked. "Because he felt underrated, and he had the ability and the opportunity to let everyone uncover the deeper meaning of his works. I think that was perfectly proper." What Ma Ka-fai finds regrettable is that Jin Yong could have done much more on the mainland, but he didn't.

Later, Jin Yong appeared as a tireless old man forever eager to learn. He reportedly refused to accept an honorary doctorate conferred directly by Cambridge University, choosing instead to take courses like an ordinary student, and completed master's and doctoral theses.

![Image from [Ming Pao video](https://www.mingpao.com)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181031091700oUb9HCvkF4cqrwsj.png-w600)

Wang Xiaolei believes that Jin Yong is the mainland, the West, and Hong Kong combined. The mainland gave him learning, experience, and a grand vision; the West gave him nourishment — he loved Victor Hugo and Balzac, and there was something modern in his outlook; Hong Kong gave him the freedom to create and the soil in which to exercise it.

### Popular and Serious

As reporters waited outside Hong Kong Sanatorium & Hospital for the latest news, the people gathered began recalling the couplet Jin Yong devised for his 14 works: "Flying snow shoots the white deer; the laughing divine hero leans on the blue luan." It refers to the wuxia novels Jin Yong serialized in *Ta Kung Pao*, the *Hong Kong Commercial Daily*, *Ming Pao*, and other publications from the 1950s to the early 1970s, until Jin Yong announced in 1972 that he was laying down his pen and would write no more wuxia. Those 14, plus *The Sword of the Yue Maiden*, which is not included in the couplet, make up the side of Jin Yong people know best.

![A bronze statue of Jin Yong on Peach Blossom Island in Zhejiang, flanked by the famous couplet "Flying snow shoots the white deer; the laughing divine hero leans on the blue luan." Image from [Wikipedia](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bb/Sculpture_of_Jin_Yong.JPG)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181031090307vk9U1REYelyHWZzp.JPG-w600)

From the very beginning, the birth of wuxia fiction was a matter of market calculation, above all the "new school" wuxia represented by Jin Yong, Gu Long, and Liang Yusheng. In 1954, the two great schools of Hong Kong's martial arts world — the Tai Chi school and the White Crane school — fell into dispute and agreed to settle it in a ring. After the affair blew up, the editor-in-chief of the *New Evening Post* suggested that Liang Yusheng, whose real name was Chen Wentong, try his hand at writing a wuxia novel, and it made him an overnight hit. To meet the demand for a wuxia novel serialized every day, Liang Yusheng egged Jin Yong into writing *The Book and the Sword*.

Compared with the old-school wuxia of the Qing and Republican eras, Liang Yusheng, Jin Yong, and the others incorporated Western fiction-writing techniques into the traditional wuxia subject. "For example, A'zi gives Qiao Feng a bowl of water to drink, and after drinking it Qiao Feng can fall in love with her. That's a device from Greek tragedy," Wang Xiaolei told QDaily. "Jin Yong's novels contain many Western-style techniques — devices borrowed from Balzac, from *The Count of Monte Cristo*, and from ancient Greek tragedy."

Wang Xiaolei believes the value of Jin Yong's work comes down to two sentences. One is "The greatest of knights serves the nation and the people," carrying national feeling — for example, Guo Jing defending Xiangyang, or the Heaven Sword and Dragon Saber resisting the Yuan armies. The other is "Pity us mortals, for sorrow and suffering abound," full of humanist compassion. "He has compassion for all his characters — people twisted by power, like Ren Woxing and Dongfang Bubai; people twisted by love, like A'zi and He Hongyao; and people twisted by vengeance, like Lin Pingzhi and You Tanzhi..." he said.

Xingyuan Ping believes Jin Yong's works shaped a generation's values. Ma Ka-fai, meanwhile, says quite a few young Hong Kong men learned Chinese well by reading Jin Yong, and "Jin Yong's works are not only classics of literary reading, but also a repository within the cultural symbols of the Chinese diaspora waiting to be explored and used. Whatever kind of Chinese you are, from whatever era, you can see in Jin Yong's works your shadow, your friends, your lovers. This is what makes him a common language of global Chinese. And then there is the presentation of certain core traditional Chinese values within historically fictional settings. What is a knight? What is righteousness? What is love?"

Jin Yong himself believed the greatest value of the wuxia novels he wrote lay in their sense of right and wrong. In a conversation with The Paper, Jin Yong said: "The value of my novels lies in stressing the idea of right and wrong — in emphasizing that a person should have a chivalric spirit, stand up when they see injustice, sympathize with the weak, act bravely for what is right, and not do what should not be done."

In that era, the Chinese revolution had only just settled, a new social order urgently needed to be built, and the standard for what a good society might be was unclear. In a manner close to a fairy-tale world, Jin Yong restated the simplest rights and wrongs and gave good people happy endings, offering readers of the time a measure of spiritual comfort. This is why, when people mourned Jin Yong on social networks, they always mentioned that Jin Yong's wuxia was the foundation on which they established their worldview and values.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181031084000054fMGtKNxYSzeA3.jpg-w600)

A question that follows is how to understand the value of Jin Yong's wuxia in the history of literature as a whole. Hsia Chi-an and his brother Hsia Chu-ch'ing, both literary critics, had a huge divergence precisely over how to view Jin Yong. Hsia Chi-an was a die-hard Jin Yong fan, while Hsia Chu-ch'ing always refused to read him: "If you're going to read wuxia novels, how many novels would you have to read? If you're going to read Jin Yong, you'd have to read early stuff like *Legend of the Strange Heroes of the Rivers and Lakes* too — the very idea! Wuxia novels — I don't read any of them."

Critics always felt Jin Yong's wuxia could not escape the clichés of genre fiction and sniffed at it. In Jin Yong's stories there is always a male protagonist who starts out unremarkable, obtains a miraculous encounter and masters divine martial arts, happens to become entangled in the affairs of state and national righteousness, and makes his own choices. In the late novel *The Deer and the Cauldron*, Jin Yong created a complete opposite of this in Wei Xiaobao — but the anti-cliché was still a generic choice built on the foundation of the earlier cliché.

"Those stories and characters of his — I can't recall any of them today; only an impression remains: repetitive plots, verbose prose, fighting the moment people meet, never saying plainly what one sentence could make clear, and no one ever able to kill anyone — just when someone's about to die, a meddler drops from the sky. All the characters have some random blood feud, and the whole plot is driven by that. What's fresh about it? All those old Chinese novels, whether romances or pornography, follow this pattern — in the end it's just karmic retribution." That was Wang Shuo's verdict on Jin Yong.

On the opposite side, people who love Jin Yong's wuxia can never wait to vindicate it, elevating him to the halls of literature. What Fu Guoyong values is Jin Yong's place in literary history and his exemplary language. He told QDaily: "In the colonial environment of Hong Kong from the 1950s to the 1970s, he carried the tradition of this new wuxia novel further and created its highest peak. His prose is so clean, so vivid — it can fully be called a model of the mother tongue. Even set within a hundred years of Chinese vernacular literature, Jin Yong could unquestionably rank in the top hundred."

As for the accusation that Jin Yong's works are merely popular, unfit for refined company, Ma Ka-fai, Xingyuan Ping, and Wang Xiaolei all consider it a prejudice — Shakespeare and Jane Austen, after all, were also very "popular" in their day, which in no way diminishes their greatness. There is no clear gulf between so-called serious literature and popular literature. "Our minds have been formatted by many fixed ideas. In the 1980s and 1990s, whenever many people mentioned Jin Yong, mentioned literature, they got tangled up in one question: are you new literature or old literature? Back then, many people criticized Jin Yong by saying you're old literature, not new literature. What is new literature? Only post-May-Fourth literature is new literature, and therefore good. Old literature needs to be criticized; new literature needs to be promoted. In those days, people were bound by these ideas. Now we are bound again by 'classic or popular.' This is normal. Writers must accept doubt; it doesn't matter — leave it to time," Wang Xiaolei told QDaily.

Chen Shih-hsiang, a literary critic and close friend of Hsia Chi-an, tried to excavate the depths of Jin Yong's wuxia. Discussing *Demi-Gods and Semi-Devils*, Chen Shih-hsiang said that as long as one grasped the theme of "grievance and karmic entanglement, and salvation" in the prologue, one could see that "the characters and plot of the book can be described as: no one is without grievance, every love is a sin; to write it to the utmost, one must render ordinary people and ordinary feelings as the bizarre. The world of the book is a bright, clear world yet everywhere hides demons and devils, ready to be exposed and satirized at any moment with wonderful surprise... Behind such characters, plot, and world looms the boundless great transcendence of Buddhist dharma, now and then revealed."

David Der-wei Wang, the most representative literary critic of our time and a professor at Harvard, meanwhile offered an interpretation grounded in social context. He believes that in 1956, Mao Zedong put forward the slogan "let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred schools of thought contend" on the mainland. The *New Evening Post*, where Jin Yong worked, hoped to continue leftist thought within a capitalist colonial society. To cater to the tastes of petty urbanites, it revived wuxia fiction. Jin Yong himself held leftist positions. "We don't usually think of Jin Yong's *The Book and the Sword* as connected to the left wing's attempt to break the Cold War stalemate overseas."

![The Book and the Sword, formally published in book form in 1976. Image from [Douban](https://book.douban.com/subject/1329963/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181031090607beqOCKvGAILXN3R6.jpg-w600)

Toward these interpretations and viewpoints attached to his novels, Jin Yong's attitude was always one of detachment. In interviews he would always say, "I think my novels are just fairly lively and entertaining; it's fine if people find them rather tasty when they read them in their spare time." As for how Dream of the Red Chamber studies are dubbed "Redology," and how Jin Yong studies have been dubbed "Jinology," Jin Yong also politely declined: "The term 'Jinology' — I dare not accept it. 'Jinology' is not worth studying. Better that everyone just chat about it if interested."

### Politics and Literature

Besides novelist, Jin Yong's other important identity was newspaperman.

His later achievements in fiction — achievements that began as a happy accident — overshadowed his achievements in the newspaper business. Jin Yong also seemed not to take the identity of wuxia novelist too seriously. When mentioning his ideals in life, apart from the vague goals of studying hard and accumulating knowledge; working diligently to benefit society; and spending old age in carefree comfort, the only work-related thing he mentioned was: "I founded *Ming Pao*, and I am convinced this enterprise benefits society. I hope it can exist for a long time to come, continue to develop, and contribute to the public."

In fact, Ma Ka-fai believes that, because of his outstanding editorials, Jin Yong was a famous opinion leader in Hong Kong at the time. What he was most often remembered for were his criticisms of the Cultural Revolution on the mainland and of the leftists during the 1967 riots in Hong Kong. The latter even put his life under threat.

Fu Guoyong pointed out a more complicated side: mainland readers, for a considerable time, didn't know *Ming Pao* existed and had never read it, yet *Ming Pao* cared about the mainland of that era, cared about the two shores and three places of that era, and indeed the whole world. The editorials Jin Yong wrote with his own pen influenced many readers.

"He came to Hong Kong in 1948 and worked in the press. Most of his life — or rather, his most precious golden years — were spent in the newspaper business. From Hangzhou's *Southeast Daily* to Shanghai's *Ta Kung Pao*, to Hong Kong's *Ta Kung Pao* and *New Evening Post*, until he founded *Ming Pao* himself and turned it into a newspaper group. Over these more than half a century, from the 1940s to the 1990s, Jin Yong became a proudly independent newspaperman in the Chinese world. So his influence on the history of the press is just as important as his influence on the history of wuxia fiction," Fu Guoyong said.

Fu Guoyong believes that Jin Yong the newspaperman inherited the Chinese tradition of "scholars discussing politics." So-called "scholars discussing politics," in the words of Lee Chin-chuan, chair professor of communication at City University of Hong Kong, has, for more than a century, had China's press sustain the nation's fortune and people's livelihood in a three-part movement: enlightenment, revolution, and the pursuit of national modernization. A great cohort of thinkers, politicians, scholars, and newspapermen were active in it, spreading new knowledge, stirring the people's spirit, flogging corruption and evil, exploring the road ahead — fusing China's scholar-gentry tradition with the modern intellectual's spirit into a distinct "scholars discussing politics" style. Wang Tao, Liang Qichao, and Hu Shih were among its representatives.

Similarly, Wang Xiaolei believes that *Ming Pao*'s growth from a small paper into a big one basically rests on the two sentiments Jin Yong insisted on: one of nation and home, one of humanity. Jin Yong had Balzac's body and Hugo's soul — "above the absolutely correct revolution, there is an absolutely correct humanism" (from *Les Misérables*).

*Ming Pao*, for instance, distinguished itself during Hong Kong's 1967 unrest. Choy Tsz-keung, senior lecturer in the Department of Government and Public Administration at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, once wrote: "In the 67 riots, those who criticized the leftists included not only Commercial Radio but also *Ming Pao*. At the time Louis Cha wrote several editorials condemning the rioters' violence, which brought all-out siege from the leftists; vicious attacks were inevitable, and the greater threat came less than a week after Lam Bun was burned to death, when a parcel bomb arrived at the home of Louis Cha — mockingly dubbed 'Cha the Jackal' by the leftists... But in those treacherous moments when everyone feared for themselves, *Ming Pao* continued to publish editorials condemning the rioters' violence, and never retreated."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201810310908170alg8ywio6Sj7BYJ.jpg-w600)

![Image from [Ming Pao video](https://www.mingpao.com/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181031090832g7Mw31Wq5TrcXOhp.jpg-w600)

"During Hong Kong's 1967 unrest, the *Ming Pao* office came under attack from the leftist struggle committees, and the personal safety of *Ming Pao*'s key figures was threatened. This instead reinforced *Ming Pao*'s image of standing on the side of the majority of Hong Kong citizens and resolutely opposing the leftists. *Ming Pao*'s editorials emphasized making Hong Kong home and supporting the colonial government, which further established *Ming Pao*'s image as a Hong Kong newspaper," Zhang Guiyang wrote in the book *Jin Yong and the Press*.

Or take April 22, 1973, when Jin Yong published an editorial in *Ming Pao* titled "The Greatest Three-in-One," proposing that "the three-in-one of egalitarian socialism, liberal democracy, and humane humanism" is the greatest achievement in human history. This was also Jin Yong's ideal.

But by the 1980s, on many issues concerning Hong Kong and the mainland, Jin Yong "caused quite a few storms." "Regarding the development of Hong Kong's democratic system, and the angle from which Hong Kong should plan its future with Beijing, many people were quite dissatisfied with Jin Yong's conservatism. Even *Ming Pao*'s own senior staff burned copies of *Ming Pao*, attacking him heavily. Of course, Mr. Jin Yong was not necessarily acting for personal gain, and so on. He felt that Hong Kong basically could not fight Beijing head-on, and could only move forward within the frame of reality in the future," Ma Ka-fai recalled.

In an English-language interview, speaking of dictators, Jin Yong said a dictator may be reasonable today but could go mad tomorrow — using this to express his political position. But is Jin Yong a liberal? His attitude in handling relations between Hong Kong and the mainland complicates the question. When the Hong Kong Basic Law Drafting Committee was established in 1985, Jin Yong was one of its members, serving as the Hong Kong convenor of the political-system drafting subcommittee and a member of the economic-system drafting subcommittee. Jin Yong and Cha Chi-min proposed the "political-system coordination plan," whose arrangements for the election of the Chief Executive and the Legislative Council of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region after 1997 were considered too conservative and conciliatory, sparking protests.

Some therefore compared Jin Yong to Wei Xiaobao, the protagonist of his last work *The Deer and the Cauldron* — slippery, lacking principles. This is also what Hong Kong people get criticized for. Hong Kong people understand Wei Xiaobao best. Jin Yong, however, said he was not Wei Xiaobao.

In managing *Ming Pao*, many regarded Jin Yong as an "emperor" who was "rather stingy with employees' salaries." But, Ma Ka-fai says, Jin Yong's most important talent was using people — knowing what kind of person was good and knowing how to train them. "Today, many of the middle-aged-and-older generation came out of *Ming Pao*."

By the time mainland readers came to know *Ming Pao*, the paper had already changed hands. In 1991, the multi-business Ming Pao Group went public; Jin Yong exited management and began selling the paper to Yu Pun-hoi. The paper changed considerably afterward. But because of its past legacy, "*Ming Pao* today is still a resounding brand. Everyone who takes public affairs seriously reads *Ming Pao*, exchanges views in *Ming Pao*, and publishes opinions in *Ming Pao*," Ma Ka-fai said.

And because of his dual identity as newspaperman and novelist, outsiders often ask whether political allegory exists in Jin Yong's works.

Nick Frisch, the Yale East Asian studies PhD student, had interviewed Jin Yong four years earlier. He said Jin Yong acknowledged that his later novels were indeed allegories of certain Cultural Revolution events.

Wang Xiaolei holds a similar view, but he believes that in Jin Yong's works, the value of human nature ultimately outweighs the so-called political allegory. "It's true — the allegory in his novels is very obvious. That's not unusual either: the Sun-Moon Holy Cult is already called the Sunrise Holy Cult; why is it called the Sunflower Manual? Sunflowers turn toward the sun. But the more valuable things are those about human nature — say, how human nature is twisted by power," he said. Xingyuan Ping also feels that for Jin Yong, art has a longer-lasting vitality than narrow, immediate politics.

Beyond Chinese politics, Ma Ka-fai believes Jin Yong's works are important for their universal side, often probing the fate of human beings. In February 2018, *Legend of the Condor Heroes* came out in English, reaching foreign readers. British translator Anna Holmwood once told QDaily: "Jin Yong's greatest gift is that reading his books makes you feel like you're back in your adolescence."

Fu Guoyong reflected: "As Jin Yong, a figure born into the particular time and space of the twentieth to twenty-first centuries, his story still provides a very important example for an intellectual, for a person of letters. He let the world know the limits a person can reach: he could write with both hands, and with both hands he became a blessing to his era and to all people."

On the evening of October 30, Wang Xiaolei published a new piece on his official account, "I No Longer Have a Backer," recalling his stories with Jin Yong. The article quickly passed 100,000 reads and drew nearly 50,000 likes. As with every celebrity death, all kinds of articles surged forth at once, and amid the memorial frenzy Jin Yong suddenly became venerable; everyone seemed to know him, seemed close to him, and held him up as the standard.

Wang Xiaolei told QDaily that Linghu Chong is the typical embodiment of Jin Yong's values. "What is Linghu Chong the archetype of? Life is precious, love is dearer; but for the sake of freedom, both can be given up. The leader of the Demon Cult said, Linghu Chong, you may marry my daughter, but you must join my cult. Linghu Chong once considered agreeing, but when he saw the scene inside the cult — kowtowing to the cult leader, shouting 'ten thousand years,' 'ten thousand ages,' 'unification of the rivers and lakes' — Linghu Chong felt that doing such things was sickening. How could a real man do such things! He would rather not marry Yingying than join the Holy Cult in the end."

"I think these are Jin Yong's contributions. He created fairy tales for adults, told moving love stories, mocked the ridiculousness of totalitarianism, mocked its ugly face, and sang the praises of great freedom."
