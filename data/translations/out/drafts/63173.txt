## [Where Does Gentrification Come From, Why Is It Inevitable, and How Does It Bring Injustice?](https://www.qdaily.com/articles/63172.html)

In our understanding of cities, what is missing, and what concepts remain fuzzy? For example, what is society?

60

Academic discussions about the absence of society in China have been numerous since the 1980s. *Transcending Boundaries: The Zhejiangcun Life Story*, in which the scholar Xiang Biao took Zhejiangcun as his research subject, examines the microcosm of society through fieldwork and is absolutely worth reading.

A revised edition of the book was published in 2018, and the prefaces written for different editions at different times are considered especially brilliant. In these essays, Xiang Biao offers further observations: the problem of the missing society in China has not improved after several decades; as government power has expanded, social space has shrunk further and been reduced to a "physical space"; the administrative system is highly "territorialized"; and the government harbors deep-rooted suspicion toward society...

61

In a preface written in 2016, Xiang Biao notes that although most of the "Zhejiangcun people" who remained in Beijing purchased middle- to high-end commodity housing, left behind the simple labor of garment production, and no longer differed much from other Beijing urban residents in lifestyle—"urbanization and marketization do seem to have promoted integration." But the reality is otherwise—the functions that make up a city's vitality and creativity have disappeared.

> But Zhejiangcun's social autonomy—that is, its bottom-up self-organization and innovative capacity—has also disappeared. The kind of spontaneous residential construction and mutual-aid groups that existed before 1995 are unimaginable today.
> Although most Zhejiangcun people now live in apartment buildings they have bought, they still do not have Beijing household registration; their children must attend expensive private schools or pay hefty sponsorship fees to enter public schools. The integration we see is not the integration of "Zhejiangcun people" into urban society but their transaction with the urban market as commodity-housing owners and consumers of private education. At the same time, the local district and township governments have gained greater power to intervene and extract resources than before, while "Zhejiangcun" has completely lost its ability to pressure from below for policy change.
> In the 1990s, media coverage of "Zhejiangcun" often contained criticism of the municipal government; after the mid-2000s, coverage was almost uniformly supportive of the government's industrial upgrading and old-city renovation.
> 
> 
> Xiang Biao, *Transcending Boundaries: The Zhejiangcun Life Story*

62

By contrast, nearly 20 years earlier, in the late 1990s, he had judged that "within a certain sphere, a distinction between state and society has already emerged in China," but that this distinction was "in name only"—"it existed in fact, yet had not received clear recognition in policy, law, or general social meaning."

> Twenty years later, the situation looks more like "in name only": "Zhejiangcun people" are nominally independent economic and legal subjects as registered investors and homebuyers, but they no longer constitute an actual social force.
> The formalization that Zhejiangcun underwent is a general trend in social change across mainland China.
> Since the 1990s, all kinds of certifications and approvals have become increasingly transparent in procedure, yet administrative power has also grown larger and harder to bypass. Government and state-owned enterprises have once again become the preferred career choices for university graduates. The imbalance in social structure occurs simultaneously with the formal orderliness of daily life, the cleanliness of streets, and the convenience of transportation—and is even realized and concealed through the latter.
> 
> 
> Xiang Biao, *Transcending Boundaries: The Zhejiangcun Life Story*

63

The result is that social space has become purely "physical space."

> The formalization of Zhejiangcun reduced an emerging "new social space" to a physical space.
> In social space, the subject is people; what constitutes social space is the intricate and therefore inevitably messy web of relationships. In physical space, the subject is land; what dominates is a singular market plan under the administrative framework.
> Faced with social space, the government must negotiate, compromise, and learn to coexist; the means of dealing with physical space are simple and clear: demolish, relocate, sell.
> 
> 
> Xiang Biao, *Transcending Boundaries: The Zhejiangcun Life Story*

64

> Another result is a preference for segregation. This segregation stems from the government's need for administrative convenience and stability, and also satisfies the middle-class NIMBY demand. Although segregation appears in different forms around the world, its naked and unabashed expression is still startling.
> The social tensions triggered by spontaneous population mobility are ultimately caused by the overall characteristics of the administrative system; the household registration system is only a small part of it. The Chinese administrative system strictly divides rights and responsibilities according to territorial boundaries (especially provincial ones). For example, when the Beijing municipal government cleared out "Zhejiangcun" in 1995, it issued a special ban: any district or county in Beijing was strictly forbidden to accept "Zhejiangcun people," in order to push these floating populations completely beyond Beijing's administrative boundaries. Puzzlingly, this policy even received approval from the State Council.
> It seems that even the central government failed to realize that these floating populations are Chinese citizens who will continue to live in China; simply expelling them from one administrative district to another cannot solve any problem!
> 
> 
> Xiang Biao, *Transcending Boundaries: The Zhejiangcun Life Story*

65

Xiang Biao further points to the crux of the problem.

> Under a territorialized administrative system, territorial and spatial boundaries are not important in themselves; territory matters because the system believes that confining people within clearly demarcated administrative boundaries is the most effective means of control. Therefore, what matters is not the relationship between one place and another, but the relationship between state and society. As long as this stance remains unchanged, household-registration reform will not reduce the social tension caused by the increase in spontaneous migration.
> Take the example of Zhejiangcun: the dàyuàn (self-built residential-production compounds erected by the floating population) were a special kind of space that could have mediated between the government and the floating population and improved management. The local Dahongmen police station also recognized this potential and hoped to use the dàyuàn to curb criminal gangs in "Zhejiangcun." But the attempt to treat the dàyuàn as an independent unit of social management and cooperate with their bosses was not approved by higher-level government. This was not because the attempt was unsuited to territorialized administrative management, but because the government feared that these dàyuàn bosses would thereby gain more power.
> Any measure that increases social autonomy might lower crime rates, but in the long term it could also bring structural changes in the relationship between state and society. When the government faces a choice—whether to rely on "suppression" to solve sudden problems or to make structural adjustments—it always prefers the former. In other words, the real problem lies in the relationship between state and society.
> 
> 
> Xiang Biao, *Transcending Boundaries: The Zhejiangcun Life Story*

66

In a public speech at the end of 2018, Xiang Biao re-examined the new phase of eviction and dispersal since 2017 and put forward a new thesis: this phase of eviction is, in essence, a rebellion against the previous logic of capital accumulation through eviction that had been in place since 1995.

Through surveys he initiated, he found that ordinary people, and even most of the evicted floating population, could rationally accept the government's actions. They shared certain keywords with the government, such as "the bigger picture," "upgrading," and "elimination."

"Without eliminating the low-end, there can be no high-end development. Who gets eliminated? Us, of course." Those who left certainly did not welcome this, but at the same time they did not think there was anything incorrect in it.

This is still far from gentrification; viewed from the most basic levels of social function and community formation, this conclusion should be beyond doubt.

### What Is Missing From Our Understanding of Cities, and What Drives Prosperity

67

China has experienced decades of prosperity, during which the face of cities has changed completely. This includes both changes in the city's appearance and changes in the urban economic landscape brought about by economic prosperity.

> In Shanghai, urban redevelopment largely reflects the strong and direct power of the municipal and district governments. But surprisingly, some commercial streets have been shaped by the government's selective neglect. Many owners of ground-floor apartments on commercial streets converted their street-facing rooms to retail use or opened shops on the street. Although this is technically illegal, local governments chose to overlook it for social reasons. These local shops gave many people—especially laid-off workers, retirees, and the floating population—a means of employment. The rent paid by shop owners provided working-class landlords with extra income, supplementing their pensions and unemployment insurance.
> 
> 
> 
> Sharon Zukin, Philip Kasinitz, and Xiangming Chen, *Global Cities, Local Streets: Everyday Diversity from New York to Shanghai*

68

Shanghai's prosperity was the result of decentralization. During this period, because it involved large numbers of laid-off workers in Shanghai and social contradictions were acute, the government adopted a relatively tolerant policy toward the commercial attempts of laid-off workers. Of course, the commercial form of natural neighborhoods evolved like that of most cities: first locals opened shops, then they sublet them to migrants who came to Shanghai to do small business, while local unemployed and laid-off workers transformed from operators into landlords.

This is part of the market's logic.

But when the lives of migrants "disturb" the lives of locals, conflicts arise between the interests of landlords and other residents; further, as the proportion and influence of migrants grow, conflicts also arise between native residents and migrants.

A typical example is Tianzifang.

In *Global Cities, Local Streets*, Tianzifang is one of two neighborhood case studies in Shanghai, and the authors analyze it in considerable depth, although most of the material stops a few years back.

However, for urban development, this kind of contradiction is actually of little consequence and has no substantive impact; as new residents and original inhabitants integrate, prosperity itself will not be harmed.

69

As with Xiang Biao's many years of observation of Beijing's "Zhejiangcun," the attitude toward migrants—whether out of administrative needs or out of "protection" of native residents (whose positions are often close to those of government officials)—means that Shanghai or Beijing, at the government level, prefer to represent the interests of locals. This causes them to overlook the real reasons for urban prosperity and violates the essence of the government's provision of fair services.

Native residents and governments of big cities have not realized the role of migrants. Among the world-class cities that people talk about with relish, New York has the highest immigration rate, as we cited earlier.

> By setting up stalls and opening shops on local commercial streets, migrants can always find a place in a city's economic system. Their activities may not be legal, so their relationship with local governments is risky. But in the best cases, outsiders on local commercial streets provide useful services to long-term local residents and to fellow migrants like themselves, thereby promoting social integration. Moreover, local commercial streets are not only spaces for economic transactions but also spaces for cultural exchange, where people speaking different languages or with different accents can find acceptance. In short, local commercial streets are composed of diverse social and cultural spaces in everyday life. The juxtaposition of these spaces along a street—cultural diversity within a space or between spaces—forms the cosmopolitan character of global cities.
> 
> 
> 
> Sharon Zukin, Philip Kasinitz, and Xiangming Chen, *Global Cities, Local Streets: Everyday Diversity from New York to Shanghai*

70

The government draws the wrong lesson from experience, believing that prosperity is created by the government, when in fact it is largely brought about by the government "doing less and not disturbing the people."

And this wrongly summarized experience, applied in the process of gentrification, has neither protected the interests of tenants—who are largely not "citizens" in the government's eyes—such as migrants in Yangpu and restaurant operators on Yongkang Road, nor guaranteed the interests of landlords. Although, as mentioned earlier, many landlords are government agencies and public institutions, which may not care about rental income, ordinary private landlords have lost their rental income in this pseudo-gentrification process.

In Shanghai's local forums, locals often express the desire to attract high-end talent to Shanghai.

Although regional discrimination is not allowed in official management, in reality, from officials to the local masses, the negative attitude toward migrants—or more precisely, the prohibition of regional discrimination rhetoric in the name of stability—has left even less room for voices supporting "the contribution of migrants" and "the right of the poor to live and work in big cities."

71

Sharon Zukin and her co-authors reach one of the book's most important conclusions: "Although the six cities from New York to Shanghai, and their governments, represent very different urban governance systems, they share one striking similarity: the role played by planners is very limited."

> Even in China, where centralized leadership is relatively pronounced, its two streets have benefited from the government's selective decentralization at critical moments. Private apartment owners could create commercially used spaces, and migrant workers could open small shops in these spaces, all without official government approval.
> 
> 
> 
> Sharon Zukin, Philip Kasinitz, and Xiangming Chen, *Global Cities, Local Streets: Everyday Diversity from New York to Shanghai*

72

Several authors from different regions unobtrusively explore the various problems that have arisen in the gentrification of six cities, but on the whole they remain relatively optimistic. Regarding prosperity, the government's role in regulating the commercial environment of an area such as rents, and how to "protect" the elements of traditional neighborhoods—"is it the shops, the people in the shops, or the spatial scale? What policies are most effective: planning, zoning, rent control, targeted investment attraction, or excluding certain shops?"—they display a relatively optimistic attitude. After all, commercial forms, the people in the neighborhoods, and various market actors have shown ample diversity and vitality in these cities.

Of course, if their fieldwork had continued longer, they might have seen more less-than-optimistic changes.

This also shows that China still has a long way to go in understanding cities.

### What Is Missing From Our Understanding of Cities: What Is Human Scale, and What Is Dignity

73

"The markets were full of the breath of life, while the main avenues, deliberately maintained, appeared serene. The center of the avenue was for carriages and horses; alongside it were sidewalks, drainage ditches, and fruit trees. Some avenues were more than 130 meters wide, equivalent to four times New York's Fifth Avenue. But there were no shops along the avenues, and compared with the city's population, little traffic passed along them. The avenues we saw were subtly different from what we had imagined. Rather than being bonds connecting residents from different parts of the city, they were chasms separating them. If by day the avenues saw little commuting traffic, by night they lost all meaning. Once the city gates closed at night, life withdrew behind curtains." The absurd scene Yi-Fu Tuan describes for us in *Topophilia* is that of Chang'an, the capital of China's Tang dynasty.

> As a political city, Chang'an reflected the cultural ideal of supreme imperial power. In the later Tang period, as the middle class grew stronger and its demands increased, the ideal of supreme imperial power gradually faded. Control over society as a whole was somewhat relaxed. The markets on the western side of the city broke through their boundaries and merged with surrounding residential areas. At night, the gates were no longer closed...
> In the Song dynasty, the tide toward a commodity economy did not stop but gradually accelerated. Hangzhou, the capital of the Southern Song, had already been a major center of commerce long before it bore imperial foundations.
> Although both Chang'an and Hangzhou were giant cities with large populations, their urban lifestyles were diametrically opposed: the former was restrained and maintained an imperial bearing, the latter was relaxed and pursued fashion and novelty. This difference is also evident in population density: Chang'an had 12,500 people per square kilometer, while Hangzhou had 45,500.
> 
> 
> Yi-Fu Tuan, *Topophilia*

Although such a Chang'an no longer exists, this heavily political urban gene does not seem to have completely died out. Some of the policy preferences and management methods that emerged in urban transformation, mentioned by Xiang Biao in the previous section, are related to the legacy of the political city.

74

In modern times, this political urban landscape is also related to planning. Jan Gehl, author of *Cities for People* and a longtime advocate of human-scale cities, pointed out the basic features of the "inhuman" city when referring to the famous urban plan of Brasília, the capital of Brazil, in the twentieth century.

> This refers to urban planning that attends only to the scales of the first two levels and ignores the smallest human scale; unfortunately, this approach has been widely propagated as a planning principle. The three levels are: buildings, spaces, and life.
> Seen from above, Brasília's silhouette is distinct, like a giant eagle: government buildings at the head, wings sheltering residential and institutional buildings. The government district is designed with precise lines and towering buildings, surrounded by vast green areas, culminating in the National Congress—a striking sight, of course, when viewed from the appropriate distance.
> In terms of human-scale landscape, Brasília is a lamentable failure. The urban space is excessively large and utterly unfriendly to people; the roads are long, straight, and boring, and parked cars block pedestrians everywhere, making it impossible to enjoy walking.
> 
> 
> Jan Gehl, *Cities for People*

75

In contrast to Brasília is Copenhagen, where Jan Gehl lives. He says: "From the air, Copenhagen's row-house neighborhoods look uniform and uninteresting. But at eye level, these row houses have many advantages and function well, which is why most of the city's architects and their families live in them."

76

When it comes to "inhuman" cities, we find it easier to identify problems from a broad macro perspective; when it comes to "human" cities, it is often the small designs and concrete measures at the fine scale that matter. This is an interesting phenomenon.

77

> A human-scale landscape refers to scenes in the city that facilitate walking, sitting, listening, talking, and watching.
> These basic activities are closely linked to human senses and organs of movement; if they can take place under good conditions, then within a human-scale landscape they and other related activities can intertwine and combine, realizing all possibilities of development.
> Its core lies in "small scale."
> ...
> Encouraging citizens to walk and cycle in urban space is only a beginning, far from completion. People must also be encouraged to sit and stay in urban space for long periods.
> Staying activities are not only a key factor in building vibrant cities but also equally important for building truly happy cities. People stay in a place because it is beautiful, interesting, and pleasant.
> Good cities are much like good parties: guests stay because they enjoy themselves.
> ...
> A city suitable for meeting refers to a city that provides good opportunities for three basic human activities: seeing, hearing, and talking.
> ...
> One of the most important and attractive aspects of the city is watching urban life. Whether we are walking, standing, or sitting, we continuously engage in a very common activity: people-watching.
> If benches and other seating offer good views for "people-watching," their use is guaranteed.
> 
> 
> Jan Gehl, *Cities for People*

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190505170938d9aNsgvxmBYu2tDw.jpg-w600)

78

> During the Christmas season, the 40-foot space in front of Rockefeller Plaza was crowded with 15 stalls (most of them selling popcorn).
> The city administration was greatly troubled by this. Many ordinances stipulated that outdoor commercial activity was illegal regardless of whether such stalls had business licenses, although business was good in those spots. Shopkeepers generally cooperated with the police in enforcing these ordinances.
> ...
> Street vendors should be treated well. They are tacitly recognized as the people who provide food for outdoor urban life. They thrive because conventional commercial facilities cannot meet social needs.
> Plazas are especially suitable for such stalls. When these plazas were built, almost no small restaurants were included. Thus, street vendors filled the gap. Once they are driven away, the gap becomes especially obvious. Much of the vitality of the plaza space leaves with the stalls.
> ...
> New York City is not an overly rigid place in its regulations. Many cities not only legislate against selling food outdoors but also prohibit outdoor dining. If we ask officials about this, they will tell us about the terrible things that could happen if such restrictions were abandoned—the dangers of unsanitary food, the frightening garbage problem, and so on.
> Over the past ten years, partly because of these restrictions, most plazas and complex buildings have not included any outdoor dining facilities.
> ...
> Food attracts the kind of people who attract more people.
> 
> 
> William H. Whyte, *The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces*

79

William H. Whyte was an editor at *Fortune* magazine and a sociologist, best known for his work *The Organization Man*. His book *The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces* should be understood as "small-urban spaces," not "small-city" spaces.

In this exquisite little book, William H. Whyte discusses what is human-scale in all kinds of small spaces. For example, seat width—when ledges and spaces are wide enough for two hips, more people can sit comfortably; less than that width is not so good. 0.76 meters works, 0.91 meters is better. Another example is why sunken plazas are always annoying—there, "we don't find many people." "If there are shops in a sunken plaza, window displays easily hide the fact that the place is essentially empty. Unless it leads to the subway, why build a sunken plaza?" "Once we are inside it, we feel as if we are at the bottom of a well. People can see us, but we cannot see them."

He also discusses sightlines in pocket parks, iron railings (it can safely be said that no one likes them)...

He also mentions the issue of crowding.

> In summary, capacity is self-regulating. This point needs to be reiterated. Many planning departments worry about carrying capacity, fearing that more amenities and more seating will stimulate more people to use them, causing greater pedestrian congestion.
> However, what they should worry about is precisely the opposite: underuse rather than overuse. Underuse is the main problem.
> The capacity of most urban spaces far exceeds their use, and the exceptions provide encouraging experience. The places that carry the most people are those that use space most effectively and are the most pleasant.
> It is people themselves who determine the level of crowding, and they do it very well.
> 
> 
> William H. Whyte, *The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces*

80

Then there are unwelcome people. This may be the issue on which understandings of the city diverge the most, whether in Lee Stringer's questioning or Giuliani's strident assertions. So what should be the attitude of an urban planning observer and advocate of human-scale cities?

The answer is simple.

> The homeless person is a symbol, a reminder that any of us could become homeless one day; the symbol is not made for grace. For retailers, the list of unwelcome people is long: begging women, people behaving bizarrely in public, hippies, teenagers, old people, street musicians, and vendors of all kinds.
> ...
> Places designed with distrust get what they are looking for. Ridiculously, we are quite likely to see a drunk there. We will find drunks elsewhere too, of course—in the empty places they like, and it is in such empty places that drunks stand out. Public spaces of this kind seem tailor-made for drunks, unconsciously giving them the uninhabited realms they favor.
> ...
> The best way to deal with the problem of unwelcome people is to make a place attractive to everyone.
> 
> 
> William H. Whyte, *The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces*

81

The so-called human-scale city is also simple.

> I am not saying that plazas should be the most beautiful places or the arbiters of society. It's fine to have no rules. Simply put, what I propose is to make these urban public places a bit friendlier. We know how to do this. In the design and management of space, there are many ways to make it easier for people to meet and interact in these urban public places. Ideas that move in this direction are always good.
> Some of the most agreeable spaces may be leftover spaces, recessed spaces, scattered spaces, and the ends of spaces; their existence is purely accidental. At the intersection of 57th Street and Madison Avenue, there is a bank with two window ledges. Both ledges are low enough to sit on, and because they are recessed, they are sheltered from the wind. It is sunny there all day, people hurry past, and around the corner of the building is a stall selling fresh juice. This is a very beautiful urban place. There are other such places, most of them formed by accident. One can imagine what would happen if someone tried to plan such spaces deliberately.
> 
> 
> William H. Whyte, *The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces*

82

> Throughout the history of human habitation, residential areas have developed from paths and places that people often used. Then came vendors' stalls, then buildings, and later more complex urban structures. Cities begin with life, and Cape Town's important urban spaces are no exception. The planning method here is to continuously improve poor areas according to planning principles.
> Implementing a "Places with Dignity" program in areas where people most need dignity and meeting places is certainly a good starting point. This is also a strategy worth emulating.
> ...
> What matters is attention to people, dignity, passion for life, and the city as a meeting place. In these respects, people's dreams and desires around the world differ little. And the methods for dealing with these problems are remarkably similar, because in the end the common basic starting point is people.
> All people have the ability to walk and perceive, and have similar motor skills and basic patterns of behavior. Future urban planning must take people as its starting point to a far greater degree than we realize today.
> 
> 
> Jan Gehl, *Cities for People*

### Conclusion

83

Whatever happened to New York's Tompkins Square Park, mentioned earlier?

> Just as the rhetoric of "degentrification" quickly erased the violent hue of gentrification, the revanchist gentrification discourse of the 1990s reinforced this erasure. Therefore, the reopening of Tompkins Square Park in 1992 was predictably accompanied by the media's naturalization of the park's history, geography, and culture. The media noted its 1930s resemblance to Central Park (interpretation: forget what happened between 1988 and 1991), and *The New York Times* promptly declared the rebuilt Tompkins Square Park a "shining emerald." In fact, throughout that year the media's beautification of the neighborhood was in full swing, and photographs of young white middle-class families once again enjoying the park filled the media.
> 
> 
> 
> Neil Smith, *The New Urban Frontier: Gentrification and the Revanchist City*

84

In the end, cities must rely on development and expansion to solve their problems.

"My God, something must be done to change this scene."

Without sustained economic expansion, none of this would be possible.

85

But beyond that, in our long narrative, I think something different will remain. It belongs to Lee Stringer's question—how are we, as humans, related to one another?

It is worth emphasizing again: this is the root of all these questions.
