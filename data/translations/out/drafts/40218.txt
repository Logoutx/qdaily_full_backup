What these 700 meters have been through over the decades, and what they mean.

Shanghai no longer prizes the title of "ten-mile foreign quarter" the way it once did — one sign being that few people still mention the most traditional "ten-mile foreign quarter" day-trip route: from People's Square, the Nanjing Road Pedestrian Street, and the Bund's historic architecture, on to the Oriental Pearl Tower and Jin Mao Tower in Little Lujiazui.

After decades of demolition and rebuilding, spontaneous growth, tug-of-war, and compromise, the city can now put forward some new calling cards. There is Xintiandi and Tianzifang, which attract both locals and foreigners; there is "Ju-Fu-Chang," lined with specialty boutiques and made for leisurely strolling; and there are top-tier shopping malls that cover everything from luxury and lifestyle to specialty dining, yet usually feel big and empty.

The character and development model of these commercial districts are not only shaping what kind of national commercial center Shanghai becomes today; they will also serve as templates that directly influence how more lower-tier cities are built in the future.

And a planner — or simply a curious visitor — who wants to understand how these four kinds of opportunity have grown no longer needs to spend days in Shanghai visiting them one by one.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705020715402mqvbrahIH3FXiKk.jpg-w600)

All they need to do is take a stroll along that famous commercial street, "Nanjing West Road": starting from the "Jing'an Villas" opposite Westgate Mall and ending at the "HKRI Taikoo Hui" near Shanghai TV Station, which is about to open.

This district — more than 700 meters wide from east to west, covering less than 400,000 square meters — is a static exhibition, a testing ground, and a gathering place for foot traffic, both existing and planned.

### 1025 Lane, Nanjing West Road — Jing'an Villas: the "next Tianzifang" that never was

Because it reveres foreign-corporate culture and hosts few big internet companies, the notion that "Shanghai is no place to start a business" was once widely accepted. Yet the white-collar workers who had spent nine-to-five in CBD cubicles may have been the first to conceive a bit of business inspiration amid boredom and rebelliousness.

Cafés, galleries, celadon shops, craft studios, clothing stores, even youth hostels... the quiet, old shikumen lanes near downtown office buildings once became the vehicle for these small-shop dreams, directly giving rise to spontaneously formed commercial communities like Tianzifang.

Residents were conflicted at first: on the one hand they coveted rapidly rising rental income, on the other they protested — by splashing water, tripping circuit breakers, and the like — against the noise brought by the swelling crowds. But things settled over time, and the lanes became a tourist attraction that satisfies visitors' appetite for the exotic.

![Inside Tianzifang, Shanghai.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428010807mzCErHfQvUhO0Mop.jpg-w600)

In 2011, when the "Chabrol Café" opened in Jing'an Villas, a residential lane on Nanjing West Road, Qian Xiaokun and his partners felt what many young people wandering in felt: this place could become the next "Tianzifang" — and a classier one at that.

But now Chabrol is one of the only shops left inside Jing'an Villas. They have had to keep a low profile.

The café has no visible sign; all events require reservations by phone; first-timers must grope their way along, searching for a door number among the nearly 200 households of Jing'an Villas. The film screenings held almost every evening continue to this day, but entering the compound means passing through an iron gate nearly shut, leaving only a narrow gap, a parking barrier that leaves just a slim passage, and the security guard's faintly suspicious gaze. Shared bikes, of course, cannot enter — they have to be parked on nearby Weihai Road or Maoming North Road.

![Inside Jing'an Villas. Image from Wikipedia](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428005240YlhgX2I6WnUV7SwT.JPG-w600)

Jing'an Villas was once a "living lane." This is a typical Shanghai new-style lilong, with a neat and orderly row-house layout. The north end of the lane leads to the "Mei-Tai-Heng" trio at the heart of the Nanjing West Road shopping district; the south end opens onto the relatively quiet Weihai Road. Shaanxi North Road and Maoming North Road on either side are one-way streets, yet the lane itself can accommodate two-way car traffic.

Large swaths of intact new-style lilong are increasingly rare in Shanghai today. Jing'an Villas was therefore designated a historic conservation area, and after illegal structures were cleared away, it became as bright and spacious as it had been in the 1930s. It was then that the artsy young people decided to spend their savings on their small-shop dreams here.

Most shop owners came psychologically prepared to subsidize their operations for the sake of style, trying to resist the "secularization" that commerce brings with it. After Chabrol's operations gradually stabilized, Qian Xiaokun took over another single room of 30-plus square meters and turned his private-salon dream into the "2666 Library." The 2012 visit by Göran Malmqvist, a permanent member of the Nobel Prize committee, even caused a small stir in cultural circles.

But for most of the rest of the time, this private library — crammed with two to three thousand books, letting visitors rest and read for free — did sparse business. Spending outrunning income became the most common state of affairs in Jing'an Villas; some began earnestly thinking about controlling costs and profit, or less artsily chasing foot traffic, and the pace of "secularization" suddenly accelerated.

![Inside the 2666 Library. Image from the library's official Weibo account](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704280053024UkxXfhPQ8R1WvDj.jpg-w600)

The trigger may have been that "silk-stocking milk tea" shop said to use evaporated milk and Taikoo sugar, hand-pulling its tea. Like every "internet-famous" shop today, the long queue for milk tea wound through the lane, provoking residents' resentment. A municipal inspection later found that of the 87 shops opened in Jing'an Villas at its peak, almost all were operating without licenses.

This ultimately led to a round of joint enforcement in 2013 involving hundreds of people. Along with the cafés and Western restaurants, "Qiaojiao Wontons," which had served residents there even earlier, was also driven out of Jing'an Villas. The "silk-stocking milk tea" shop didn't go far — it moved a few hundred meters east, landing in a small storefront on Maoming North Road, gradually built a brand, and opened many branches.

But milk-tea trends have shifted several more times in the years since, and the storefront was finally taken over by a chain specialty coffee brand.

And not far to its south, a new commercial district called "Fengshengli" was also in its final stage before opening.

### 255 Maoming North Road — Fengshengli: Who is copying Xintiandi?

Starting in 2002, more and more Shanghai locals began spontaneously pitching a place called "Xintiandi" to out-of-town relatives who came to visit. Their selling points went roughly like this: typical shikumen lanes, the site of the First National Congress of the Communist Party, Western restaurants and brand stores you can't see anywhere else, and clusters of foreigners.

After producing this textbook case of commercial real estate development, Hong Kong developer Shui On — once little known on the mainland — is now selling this "restore the old as it was" approach in most first- and second-tier cities: in renovation, only the old buildings' facades are retained, and once built, large numbers of dining and boutique-retail tenants that can drive foot traffic while guaranteeing decent returns are brought in — and an "activation and renewal" is declared complete.

Not many people have calculated whether the "Xintiandi" model actually makes money, or which part earns the traffic and which part earns the money. But quite a few planners and government officials are still willing to take a slice of old city inside a core commercial district and give it a try.

The latest to join this ranks is the Jing'an district government. Immediately adjacent to "Jing'an Villas," right above the platform of Metro Line 12, Jing'an Metro Investment Group won the most expensive parcel of land ever auctioned in old Jing'an District at a base price of nearly 54,000 yuan per square meter, converted it into 10 delicate small buildings, and formed today's "Fengshengli."

![Architectural rendering of the "Fengshengli" development](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428005525RIsqgBGJe7t9jxan.jpg-w600)

On April 21, the district officially began its trial opening. A batch of street-facing shops — including bars, restaurants, soup shops, and bakeries — had started trial operations even earlier. The retro exterior is eye-catching, but every shop may hang only one small sign on the outer wall; if you don't walk right along the street, you might not even notice the place has opened.

"Goose Island Brew House" is one of them. Unlike the Hard Rock bar next door, it did not make a big show of advertising at the busier Line 2 Nanjing West Road Station nearby. Hard Rock is a casual-bar-and-casino brand with outposts in tourist cities around the world, and plenty of fun-loving foreigners know the name. Word of Goose Island's opening first spread only among craft-beer aficionados and industry insiders with a taste for that sort of thing.

![Goose Island's restaurant at Fengshengli, with American industrial-style interiors similar to those of its birthplace, Chicago](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428010427BuCwjW58VXHYZq7I.jpeg-w600)

Wei Ran doesn't usually like beer; had it not been to check on the tenants in person, she might not have attended the party on the first day of trial operations at all. But she found the beer tasted good, and the room full of foreign guests had a great vibe — exactly matching her image of the restaurants on the "Fengshengli" side.

At JLL, Wei Ran is director of the retail real estate department for Shanghai; beyond routine leasing for retail projects, she currently leads a team of more than 20 people dedicated to finding the right dining brands for various commercial real estate projects. According to figures she previously provided to QDaily, 90% of Fengshengli's shop spaces had been leased; for each of the few remaining best-located shops, two to three brands were waiting for the final result.

![Shops on the Maoming North Road side of "Fengshengli"](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428005605fOcoHWQiaj68Dmx1.jpeg-w600)

When planning Fengshengli's leasing strategy, the landlord proposed creating "an effect similar to Xintiandi," and the dining ratio was raised above 90%. But the tenants had to be "foreign brands of a higher tier that also intend to enter the Chinese market."

In the end, the sight of foreign-brand restaurants like Goose Island or Hard Rock filled with foreign consumers surprised and delighted Wei Ran. But for the local young people who come here for a genuinely craft beer or a quality live rock show, this "enclave"-like consumption experience may be another small embarrassment brought by the "Xintiandi model."

Actually, Goose Island's menu shows a bit of effort to cater to the local community: during dinner hours, the bar serves two special house-brewed beers, one called "Line 12 Wit" and the other "Wujiang Road Porter."

### Wujiang Road: Why hasn't the charm of the "back street" worked here?

Fifth Avenue in New York, Oxford Street in London, Ginza in Tokyo... the prosperity of nearly every world-class commercial street extends beyond that one main street of several hundred to a thousand meters. It is the distinctive "back streets" that truly define the reach of a shopping district.

In the Nanjing West Road shopping district between Jing'an Temple and the North–South Elevated Road, there are as many as 16 side streets connected to or adjoining the main street. The earliest to develop was Wujiang Road, which had already gathered private restaurants and stalls since the 1980s. And over the past dozen-plus years, Wujiang Road's fame and foot traffic have made it an ideal testing ground, visited in turn by several waves of real estate packagers and developers eager to create a template for the "back street" concept.

According to official records, Wujiang Road's heyday began in the autumn of 1999, with the "Jing'an Urban Style Week" held here in tandem with the trial run of Metro Line 2. The freshly renovated Wujiang Road was then positioned as a "dining and leisure street," and snack-and-fast-food names like "Yang's Fry-Dumpling," "Ruby," and "Tianmimi" got their chance to "enter proper premises."

![In the old Wujiang Road days, "Yang's Fry-Dumpling" was one of the most popular eateries on the street](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428005647LxaY52jmDifv9oy3.jpg-w600)

But once the "Nanjing West Road Station" of Metro Line 2 opened, everything changed. Today, the station's four exits are evenly distributed on the north and south sides of Wujiang Road. Metro passenger statistics from 2010 showed the station drew an average of 76,000 passenger trips a day at the time — equivalent to one-third of Nanjing East Road, the country's largest commercial street.

The effect on land values followed. In 2005, two property developers from Singapore and Hong Kong each took a fancy to two small parcels of land on the south side of Wujiang Road, hoping to develop them into high-end residences — then still rare on the market — priced above 30,000 yuan per square meter, to serve executives working on Nanjing West Road. As for Wujiang Road itself, it was thus split at Taixing Road into two podium blocks: "Sijifang" and "Huangpuhui."

![The "Sijifang" area, currently on the east side of the Wujiang Road pedestrian street](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428005737XIdwNDQtWsOhqpLV.jpg-w600)

The team from Yingshi China that had done the commercial positioning for "Sijifang" showed naked ambition at the time: they hoped to turn it into a quality back street that would complement top-tier commercial street Nanjing West Road with slight differentiation, focusing on specialty clothing and designer brands. To meet a target spend of 500–1,000 yuan per customer, the trendy boutiques then just emerging on Changle Road and Julu Road even became leasing targets.

The "high-spend back street" approach is now being applied by Yingshi China to the planning of another small street crossing Nanjing West Road, "Tongren Road." But judging from the actual results of "Sijifang" on Wujiang Road, the idea has been quite a failure: a year after opening, 24 clothing stores decided to pull out because rents were too high and income could not cover costs, suddenly leaving 20% of what had been a nearly fully leased Wujiang Road empty; by contrast, the "Huangpuhui" section, which brought old Wujiang Road shops into a mall, thrived on veteran names like "Yang's Fry-Dumpling" and "Ruby Cake."

With the back street's charm gone, "Sijifang" was soon resold by its original developer, Singapore's Frasers Property, for 480 million yuan, landing in the hands of a Hong Kong commercial property company, CSI Properties. After increasing the proportion of dining and leisure-retail shops, the street-facing business finally improved considerably. But the first-floor inner-street and second-floor shops once thought capable of expanding Wujiang Road's commercial volume never picked up.

Wujiang Road's chance at transformation is now being bet, on CSI's side, on the mixed-use complex "HKRI Taikoo Hui" one street away. Since 2013, the company has stated this idea relatively bluntly in its major financial reports almost every year. In the latest 2016 interim report, for instance, it put it this way:

"With the imminent unveiling of HKRI Taikoo Hui, the Swire Properties development, we expect the project (note: i.e., the Wujiang Road Sijifang project) to generate significant value enhancement through upgrading the tenant mix and raising rents."

### Nanjing West Road–Shimen Road No. 1, HKRI Taikoo Hui: the district's game-changer — can it now tell a community story?

The completion of Metro Line 2, the "Mei-Tai-Heng" forming the Puxi CBD, the makeover of the Wujiang Road commercial street, the bustle of "Jing'an Villas," the trial opening of "Fengshengli"... over the past nearly 15 years, the huge construction site at the corner of Nanjing West Road and Shimen Road No. 1 missed almost all the change happening around it.

Meanwhile, Shanghai gained 7.9 million more permanent residents, among whom urban residents' annual per-capita disposable income grew by a cumulative 276%. Old houses like "Jing'an Villas" and mid-range leisure streets like Wujiang Road could no longer meet their living and consumption needs — they could barely keep up with the hotspots and concepts that shift daily.

Shopping malls with exquisite design, a full range of categories, and perfume wafting from the ventilation system have become the new crowd magnets. Especially where commerce is less developed, once a big-box mall of substantial scale gets up and running smoothly, it can mean an entirely new city center taking shape.

A typical example is Chengdu after Wharf's IFS and "Sino-Ocean Taikoo Li" arrived. Over the past two years, foot traffic at the traditional commercial street "Chunxi Road" to the west has gradually been siphoned off by these two commercial complexes, and local residents' spending power has begun to be unleashed — according to Swire Properties' 2016 annual report, the project's annual retail sales grew as much as 78.3%, while the mall's occupancy rate grew only 4% in the same period; averaged across all shops, that amounts to a performance gain of more than 50%.

![Rendering of the Chengdu Sino-Ocean Taikoo Li project](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428005842PtO72jLlRvAaMmUg.jpg-w600)

For Swire Properties, which excels at commercial property planning and operations, Chengdu is hardly the easiest place in the mainland to do business, but it is well suited for further experimenting with a bit of the "community" philosophy it had worked out in its Sanlitun renovation project in Beijing: low-density, open shop layouts that both satisfy the sense of ceremony high-end flagship stores require and preserve the city's original street and courtyard fabric; anyone can easily walk between the stores — and even between the city's old and new commercial centers — and the no-longer-enclosed mall becomes a more valuable hub.

This product line, later named "Taikoo Li," was a fairly advanced commercial complex development concept, yet it could not be further embodied in the company's Shanghai project — the one it joined earliest but unveiled latest.

Between rewriting the high-end residential plan drawn up when HKR International acquired the land and nearly five years of actual construction, total project investment including bank loans had reached 16 billion yuan, and the economics became a more urgent issue than innovation.

In the end, "HKRI Taikoo Hui" chose a relatively enclosed model similar to Guangzhou's. Shoppers travel from the northernmost point to the southernmost along an internal S-shaped loop, a walking distance of more than 500 meters. That means mega-complexes like Jing An Kerry Centre and Shanghai IFC, which have operated for years, or "Xujiahui Center ITC," set to enter the market in coming years, will all be direct competitors of HKRI Taikoo Hui.

![Rendering of the Shanghai HKRI Taikoo Hui project](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428005917KIEGH7QwgluRNAdo.jpeg-w600)

And the ideas that knit the development more tightly into the surrounding community ended up expressed in some easily overlooked details: the passageway on the mall's north side running east–west across the site is in fact the eastern stretch of old Wujiang Road; the shikumen old building still being restored on the south side is the auditorium of Minli Middle School, moved in its entirety from the center of the site; in the final brand slogan, the Taikoo Hui side chose the Shanghainese expression "dia"; and on the white safety helmets often seen at the construction site, some were marked in marker pen with the pinyin "Dazhongli" — before it became the HKRI Taikoo Hui construction site, "Dazhongli" was the largest swath of new-style lilong in all of Shanghai, its history traceable to 1925, the period of Shanghai's fastest modernization.

![HKRI Taikoo Hui preserved the Minli Middle School auditorium originally on the "Dazhongli" site, but plans to convert it into a members' club and boutique retail space](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428010206CO9rs2ZVHc1hfbpg.jpg-w600)

In Hong Kong's high-growth 1970s, Swire Properties built Taikoo Shing on Hong Kong Island, the city's first true commercial complex; the tight integration of a high-quality residential community with commercial facilities is still regarded as a classic today. But in Shanghai, not many people will ask about the fate of "Dazhongli," erased for "HKRI Taikoo Hui," or of the residential neighborhoods that have become shopping malls.

This place has, after all, become a commercial real estate project where making money and efficiency are the point.

As the May 4 trial opening draws near, what new brands and consumption concepts this big mall will bring to the district has become a question of greater interest to curious consumers and to the retail industry as a whole.

According to a senior member of HKRI Taikoo Hui's operations team speaking earlier to QDaily, they will mix in as many different formats and brands as possible in the retail portion to meet the daily needs of the nearly 200,000 white-collar workers in the district.

Now, the street-facing shops adjacent to Nanjing West Road and Shimen Road No. 1 can hardly wait to show their brands on the hoarding: Dyson, Longines, Nike, Adidas, Puma, Kenzo, McQ, EXI.T, Tesla...

The more mid-sized shops, along with specially reserved "pop-up store" spaces, are kept inside the mall. Cosmetic brand stores from Wujiang Road, foreign restaurants and boutique retail drawn by Fengshengli, even the bookstores and startup brands that once sprouted inside Jing'an Villas — it seems all of them stand a chance of being brought into this shopping mall with over 100,000 square meters of floor space.

Will the many business opportunities that once appeared along this 700-plus-meter stretch of Nanjing West Road ultimately converge in a form like this?
