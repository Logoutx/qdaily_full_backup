The audience has changed, the star-making model has changed, and even the definition of an "idol" is different now. How will *Super Girl* make its comeback?

For a company looking to replicate its hit product from ten years ago, the ambition goes without saying — but what are its chances of success?

The product is "Super Girl." At the launch press conference in Changsha, Hunan, on March 7, Hunan TV once again emphasized how much it values the star show from ten years ago. "Mengniu Suansuanru Super Girl 2016 will become another phenomenal show of the internet era," said Luo Yi, deputy director of Hunan Broadcasting System.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702142644QRZThvA9FVCcwOEW.jpg-w600)

Reuniting with "Super Girl," the show's title sponsor Mengniu also raised a slogan: "We wish this year's Super Girl another entertainment-marketing miracle," said Xu Ying, brand director for Mengniu Dairy's milk beverage business.

Five new Super Girl 2016 contestants also appeared at the press conference, singing the signature hits of past "Super Girls." Their varied looks underscored the show's younger orientation and hinted at the stylistic possibilities for the new idols: Euro-American style, androgyny, cute style, ACG style, goddess style...

Ten years ago, Super Girl did more than create a generation of idols represented by Li Yuchun — it ultimately cemented Hunan TV's status as a first-tier satellite channel, a position that holds to this day. ([In 2004, Hunan TV ranked first among China's provincial satellite channels with a 4.505% market share; in 2015, it still ranked first with 3.2773%. Between 2004 and 2006, "Hunan TV" and "Super Girl" topped Chinese-language web searches and media coverage in the entertainment category.](http://ent.qq.com/a/20160105/024020.htm))

But the decade that followed was also one in which the internet closed in on traditional TV, step by step.

The pull of the TV screen kept weakening. With the spread of 3G and 4G network services, people's entertainment no longer centered on the household; it migrated to other screens in the mobile-internet domain — smartphones, tablets, and later wearable devices and VR headsets. According to the *2015 China Mobile Internet Research Report* released by mobile data services firm QuestMobile: ([by December 2015, the number of active mobile smart devices online in China had reached 899 million, and average daily time spent on phones was 2.36 hours.](http://tech.sina.com.cn/2016-01-08/doc-ifxnkkuy7746197.shtml))

So when "Super Girl," a product emblematic of the TV era, was relaunched, attention focused not only on whether a new Li Yuchun would emerge, but also on what this veteran entertainment empire could bring to a market reshaped by the internet-ization of entertainment.

## What Has Changed in the "Super Girl" Relaunch?

On March 7, the press conference looked like a continuation of the glory from ten years ago. Hunan Broadcasting was still the protagonist — only the main battlefield had shifted from Hunan TV to Mango TV; the sponsor was still Mengniu Dairy; and Tianyu Media, born out of the first Super Girl, was also in the mix. Whatever is new lies behind these old faces — at least, that is what they claim.

On the other side, new partners kept pouring in — Sina Weibo, WeChat, the Changba app, the Mingxing Yichu app — and each added an internet-flavored footnote to this year's Super Girl, each representing a new internet-era trick.

"'Super Girl' is no longer a simple variety show; it's more like an entertainment product," said Zhou Shan, executive producer of *Super Girl 2016*.

Zhou Shan was appointed by Mango TV. He is the executive producer of this year's Super Girl, and was once a member of the team behind the first Super Girl led by Long Danni. As a company tasked with taking on the internet's challenge, Mango TV is transforming from a distribution channel into a platform for self-produced content. Its content plan has two parts: one is an internet-era reworking of classic IPs (difficult, but with plenty of room); the other is pure original production (web series, shows, web movies, and so on). "Super Girl" belongs to the former.

"In today's media-consumption environment, TV stations serve more of an 'informing' function; they are no longer the pure entertainment-consumption channel they once were. TV can no longer gather everything together in a single instant the way it used to," said Liu Chenliang, executive vice president of Mango TV. "A project reworked on a pure TV platform still can't escape linear broadcasting, because without time you have nothing to work with."

This is one of the main reasons the main body of this year's Super Girl moved from Hunan TV to Mango TV. The other reason involves the licensing of talent shows, which means this Super Girl may ultimately never air on Hunan TV at all.

Although they claim there is still room for negotiation, this is clearly potential bad news. Changba is another partner of this year's Super Girl, and its vice president of marketing, Xu Wei, said that some contestants' parents were uneasy about their children signing up through Changba, because they weren't sure whether this event really was the same "Super Girl" that Hunan TV ran back in the day. "Some people even asked me to show the contestants a Super Girl invitation letter, and demanded that I confirm the directors and production team were genuine."

Changba is one of the direct-entry zones for registration and auditions in this Super Girl. For an internet project this is undoubtedly a good fit. Data as of March 6 show that cumulative sign-ups across all channels had already exceeded 130,000 — a big number. Liu Chenliang believes that, from a participation standpoint, the contestant pool is already sufficient in scale. "Think about it: if 100,000 people each upload one video, that's 100,000 videos. If each video is watched by just 20 people, we have 2 million. And how many people can one person actually influence? Basically around 60. So this basic scale has already fully unlocked users' capacity for engagement."

It isn't just the broadcast platform that changed — the competition format of "Super Girl 2016" has changed too. "For a talent show, the three biggest draws are the competition format, the star guests, and the contestants' stories. Super Girl's signature is its 'layer-by-layer elimination' format, in use from the auditions onward," said Zhao Hui, vice president of Tianyu Media.

According to the official competition details, *Super Girl 2016* will run for eight months, selecting a preliminary squad of 300 "Super Girls" through auditions in four main arenas (the Mango TV main battlefield, 10 domestic on-the-ground audition zones, multiple online and offline partner direct-entry zones, and eight overseas audition zones). Up to this point, the competition largely preserves the traditional Super Girl process.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021426445CYJahwlRszforIx.jpg-w600)

After the auditions, the second half of the competition will be produced entirely by Mango TV. Contestants enter the second stage, a national PK contest also known as the "idol incubation" stage; the 100 most popular contestants then battle it out in PKs for a place in Mango TV's "Super Girl Academy." At the academy, the production team will provide contestants with professional vocal coaches, star mentors, and artist-management teams for professional training, along with individually tailored courses and personal live-streaming rooms. The production team will present the birth of an "idol" in the round-the-clock, 360-degree format of a live reality show.

## Is It Still Possible to Create a National Idol?

The reason "Super Girl" is bringing in a reality-show element is that audience habits around idol consumption have changed.

"Idols today are segmented — they are no longer produced through layer-by-layer selection like before. We call today's idols 'private idols': it's no longer a matter of 'this is who I like, so you have to like them too.' It's that I have the person I like and you have the person you like; we may have nothing in common, but that doesn't stop us from being good friends. So we hope that through this kind of setup, users can find themselves more," said Liu Chenliang.

Variety shows in this mold have been dubbed "idol-incubation shows" by the industry. They really began to ferment last year, with examples already on the market such as iQiyi's *King of Pop*, and 《少年时代》(Young Times) and *X-Fire*, co-produced by Zhejiang TV with Tianyu Media and Tencent Video. Their hallmark is a combination of the selection-and-elimination mechanics of classic talent shows with reality-show elements, modeled on the Japanese and Korean star-making systems: documenting the journey of young idols from ordinary people to stars and giving them all-around performing-arts training and guidance.

Idols produced under this kind of system fit young people's current definition of an idol better. From solo idols like Kris Wu and Lu Han to the newly emerging group idols — domestic TFBOYS, Japan's AKB48 — none of them are national idols in the traditional sense.

![TFBOYS](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702142645QmiKTUy7cHBGMFju.jpg-w600)

Overall, China's star-making industry is still in its exploratory, getting-started phase, but the trend is clear. This year you will see a stream of idol-incubation shows roll out — some developed by TV stations themselves, some co-produced across TV and the internet, such as Anhui TV's 《星动亚洲》(Star Power Asia), Hunan TV's 《夏日甜心》(Sweet Summer Girls) and 《元气美少女》(Youthful Girls), Zhejiang TV's *Lady Bees*, and 《国民美少女》(National Sweethearts), built jointly by SMG's Internet Program Center and Dragon TV Center together with Youku Tudou and SNH48.

Compared with traditional TV talent shows like *The Voice of China*, *Sing My Song*, 《梦想星搭档》(Dream Partners), and 《最美和声》(The Most Beautiful Harmony), which focus more on competition and PK outcomes, "idol-incubation shows" have caught on because the forms of interaction they inspire better suit video users' consumption habits.

The "Super Girl" of old was hardly short of interactive innovation; in fact, it may have been among the first entertainment shows to make interaction work. Riding China Mobile's dominance at its peak, SMS voting, canvassing for votes by phone... triggered a nationwide entertainment frenzy and brought handsome economic returns. According to public figures from the time, SMS voting alone brought Super Girl revenue of over 10 million yuan: each text cost 1 yuan for China Mobile users; 0.5 yuan for China Unicom and Little Smart users; and 3 yuan per message sent via fixed-line phone (through a single unified IP number).

In an age when everyone uses WeChat, "SMS voting" clearly no longer fits today's audience habits. What should the new voting and interaction look like? Tianyu was the first to start exploring: in the online talent show *X-Fire*, it experimented with website voting, WeChat voting, and paid virtual items on video platforms as voting mechanisms, including partnering with a danmu site to open up live bullet-comment snark.

Huang Fang, a producer at Tianyu Media, said that Tianyu is mainly handling the preliminary nationwide scouting this year, as well as the production of the final ten shows. Interaction mechanics and the competition format are what they discuss most. "We keep thinking about how to use the internet's communication and marketing advantages to interact better with young audiences."

([Penguin Intelligence once ran an online survey on interaction behavior on video sites](http://free.chinabaogao.com/it/201506/06320c0H015.html)), and its results showed that, among the interaction features offered by video sites, usage rates from highest to lowest were: like/dislike, comments and favorites, and paid gift-giving (42.3% of users were somewhat unwilling to take part in "watch-and-buy"). Notably, danmu interaction's adoption and usage rate climbed rapidly to 13.5%, overtaking the subscription feature.

In addition, among users who like to interact while watching live streams, 67.1% preferred to do so on mobile devices, exceeding the PC share of 32.9% — consistent with the current trend of nearly 70% of video-site traffic coming from mobile.

This "Super Girl" will also adopt some of the interaction mechanics described above. And given the viewing experience of internet video content, the production team still needs to weigh multiple dimensions: episode length, the complexity of the competition format, and the consistency of interaction. "A traditional TV talent-show episode used to run about 90 minutes. This year a single match might last only ten-odd or twenty minutes — nothing is finalized yet. We're thinking about which presentation gives viewers a better experience," said Huang Fang.

## The Commercial Pull of Old May Never Return

In a sense, they certainly hope to produce more idol stars like "Li Yuchun," "Zhou Bichang," and "Zhang Liangying." But everything "Super Girl" relied on back then is different now. The way idols are produced has changed, to the point that "what should an idol be?" is full of unknowns. Not even the "Super Girl" production team is sure whether the final pick will be a single individual or a group.

And their competitors are likely more numerous: satellite-channel talent shows from the same background, reality-show-plus-talent-show hybrids from video sites, and of course all kinds of idol-incubation groups. They may not understand the essence of a talent show any better than Super Girl does, but one thing is certain — that golden age will not come back.

The 2005 edition of Super Girl was unprecedented in its popularity: according to ratings data for the 2005 *Super Girl* released by CVSC-Sofres Media, in people-meter surveys across 12 cities including Beijing, Shanghai, and Changsha, the show averaged an 8.54% rating, reaching an average of 11% during the finals. One claim holds that one in five Chinese watched "Super Girl," and its ratings record has yet to be broken. It rode the golden age of television, opening up new concepts such as talent shows and mass voting, setting the template for a period of TV production, and even changing the star-making model of the domestic music industry. It produced idol stars whose careers have lasted far longer than those from comparable shows — Li Yuchun and Zhang Liangying, for instance, remain A-listers to this day.

Along with the show's popularity came enormous commercial value. ([According to public information](http://ent.163.com/15/0610/21/ARPG37N200034UOI.html), advertising revenue from the seven finals episodes of "Super Girl 2005" approached 20 million yuan.) Title sponsor Mengniu's total investment of nearly 100 million yuan was undoubtedly the biggest contributor. And that investment delivered multiplied, steadily growing sales for Mengniu Suansuanru: in 2004 alone, its revenue soared from 700 million yuan to 3 billion yuan.

Measured against current benchmarks — title-sponsorship fees for hit satellite-TV variety shows start at 200 million yuan and can reach around 500 million (e.g., *Where Are We Going, Dad?*), while fees for web variety shows start at 50 million yuan and can reach 300 million (e.g., 《奇葩说》(Qipa Shuo)) — Mengniu's 150-million-yuan title-sponsorship fee for this year's Super Girl looks relatively rational.

"This year's 'Super Girl' has already attracted over 300 million yuan in total internet sponsorship (not counting hard ads)," Mango TV vice president Yuan Ganghong told QDaily. That scale is on par with first-tier web-variety-show title sponsorships.

Given the current climate of public opinion, the new "Super Girl" has done quite well in attracting sponsors.
