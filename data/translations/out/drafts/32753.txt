Chances are every creative envies Woody Allen's place in life.

**“Only things that are imaginative, original, and fresh make you interesting.”****Bill Bernbach, hailed as a giant in the history of advertising, created no small number of classics — “A diamond is forever,” for one. His view: an idea that can't communicate effectively is no different from having no idea at all.**

**QDaily's "Agency Daily" column will be rolling out a series of interviews with advertising creatives, to see how each of them understands the industry, understands people, and understands the world. Recommendations and self-nominations are welcome.**

Liu Yang, born in 1977, is currently Vice President of Leo Digital, CEO of Hupo Communication, and Associate Professor at Beijing Institute of Fashion Technology.

He founded Hupo Communication in 2005; the company was acquired by Leo Digital in 2014. In 2016, Liu Yang was invited to serve as a jury member in the Mobile category at the Cannes Lions International Festival of Creativity.

**Q: When people say you look like Ekin Cheng, how do you respond? Has your looks ever brought you anything unexpected?**

**Liu Yang:** I've kept this hair for more than a decade. Sometimes I put on an act and say, "No, no, no, not at all"; or I say modestly, "Grow your hair out like this and you'd look like him too," feeling pretty pleased with my own wit; when the mood is lively I'll say, "In the trade they call me Ekin the Second." Last year at an advertising industry forum we actually invited Ekin Cheng himself to speak on the same stage.

So far my looks haven't brought anything unexpected — though I'm rather hoping they will. A good first impression may earn you a few points; it can be the start of a conversation. But after that, you're still competing on ideas.

**Q:****When you were 22, how did you imagine your future self?**

**Liu Yang:** Our generation was raised to be fairly simple-minded, without many preconceptions. Every step followed our parents' plan: go to college, do a master's, move to Beijing, have a stable job — ideally at a university — become an associate professor, and then, so the thinking went, earn a bit of money so the family could live a little better.

I didn't actually do anything different; I basically lived inside my parents' imagination. I was just lucky enough to end up in an industry where you survive on creativity. But the truth is, many people of our generation have lived lives without much creativity or independence.

**Q:****Why did you want to leave academia and go into advertising in 2005? How has your view of the ad industry changed compared with back then?**

**Liu Yang:** At the time I felt this line of work couldn't be taught straight out of textbooks every day — you had to be on the front lines, working with brands. So I wanted to give it a try. It's hard to apply the word "entrepreneurship" to myself; I just wanted to do something, take it slowly. There was never a complete plan, never a slamming-the-table moment of declaring who I was going to become.

Back then I obviously knew nothing about advertising — I was fumbling in the dark, just doing it. In 2005 the industry was still fairly traditional: content expanded by riding on the influence of media, reshaping consumer perceptions and consumer behavior. Now it's completely the other way around.

Once the internet arrived, the fact of being connected stopped mattering. What matters now is using creativity to make the connections between people, and between brands and people, more meaningful — in the end, it's about depositing those things and turning them into brand value.

**Q:****Has anything stayed the same?**

**Liu Yang:** You keep testing which things are useful for the industry's development and for clients' needs — but you don't decide in advance what's proper to do and what's not. Every day there are still new possibilities; it's still growing, still taking in new things. In a sense, advertising is also a traditional industry, and it should learn more from internet operations.

![An early work Hupo Communication designed in 2010 for the Coca-Cola Expo brand experience pavilion](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160928233750ISwVvDQLjkUn0f8W.png-w600)

**Q:****How is doing creative work different from doing management? Which do you prefer?**

**Liu Yang:** When the company was small, it revolved around my personal ability and pull — that's how all creatives do it. Once it gets bigger, you need methods; you need to balance competing things.

The biggest change management brings to a creative is a better sense of the whole and a stronger sense of responsibility — it's a process of maturing. Having grown to where I am today, sitting at the executive level of a group, I can attempt things the industry hasn't tried before, and land them through industry resources and capital.

It's hard to say which one I like; both are part of your life. I'd like to balance them, to have both. Even now, a few pieces of work each year are ones I make myself. Stretch the timeline out further: when you look back at the end, what do you hope remains? I hope it's good work, and a good influence on the industry.

**Q:****You opened a bar in Shanghai and a hotpot restaurant in Beijing — out of interest, or something else? Between selling food and selling ideas, which do you enjoy more?**

**Liu Yang:** It's the mindset of a kid with a toy. These are two things I happen to like; going there relaxes me — that's all. They're just part of life, like someone who loves cars and buys a lot of cars, or someone who collects lighters and goes collecting. If we're talking professions, there are only two: teacher and creative.

**Q:****If you weren't in this industry, what might you be doing now?**

**Liu Yang:** There's nothing I regret never having done in this life. Doing advertising, I'm actually doing many other things at the same time — I don't see it as a proposition where advertising has to be subtracted.

Everyone stands on their existing experience and resources to do what comes next; nobody tears everything down and starts over — some people just make it sound dramatic. We actually have plenty of opportunities to do other things, because this industry is wide; it isn't that vertical.

Going forward, I'd like to do things that are directly 2C, rather than 2B-then-2C. The industry will probably also give agencies more opportunities like that. There may be other extensions too — film creation, for instance, which is also 2C.

**Q:****Whom in this industry would you most want to be?**

**Liu Yang:** It's hard to say I'd want to replicate any one person; there are many remarkable people in this industry. In different respects, you catch yourself measuring yourself against influential people.

On the question of what the company should and shouldn't do, I ask myself: if I were the two founders of Wieden+Kennedy, or David Droga, what would they do?

**Q:****Have you made work that people talked about at the time but that later embarrassed even you?**

**Liu Yang:** Last year we made two films for the CCTV Spring Festival Gala, 《门》(*Door*) and 《红》(*Red*). Around 30 agencies competed in the pitch, and we were the only one that won two.

Working on 《红》(*Red*) was thrilling, and the investment was huge — we shot 14 scenes, and I followed the whole thing from beginning to end. But when it finally aired, I realized only half of what we'd shot remained; it had become a mash-up of lots of old footage. I understand the client's adjustments, but afterward it was awkward to talk about the piece with people, and I was a bit unhappy about it.

**Q:****What's the most enviable thing about this industry?**

**Liu Yang:** It's a creative industry. What it creates is a kind of emotional connection — the link between creativity and a brand or product becomes an indelible part of that brand's value. Seen as an expression of creativity, it's endlessly interesting.

It's also wide. Many people want to be actors because you get to try out different ways of life and different roles; the ad industry is the same.

**Q:****Who was the last person you saw that made you envious?**

**Liu Yang:** A lot of answers flash through your mind at first — yourself, maybe, or my kid. But I recently saw a film that changed all my thinking: Woody Allen's new film *Café Society*.

Every creative must envy his position: outside the system, yet able to lay his laurels inside it. His rule is never to please the majority — to make films the way he likes. To this day he goes to a hotel to play the clarinet at a fixed time every week; he's done it his whole life. It's a state of utter detachment — especially remarkable to maintain at his age.

**Q:****What do you think most enriches creativity? And what most kills it?**

**Liu Yang:** What most kills creativity, our generation calls public values. Back in that era, for example, it was moving to a big city and having a good, stable job; now it's going public and raising money — the so-called benchmark of ultimate success. That makes creativity disappear.

What enriches creativity is the Woody Allen way: return to your own individual expression, return to the ordinary mindset of knowing where you belong, and face the things you need to do.

**Q:****What widespread misunderstanding do you think people have about this industry?**

**Liu Yang:** This is definitely a tiring industry — but there are many tiring industries. People look at actors and see the glamorous side, but actors are also exhausted, with no regular rhythm. Advertising, though, is mostly seen at its tiring side. In fact we also have our satisfactions and our moments of glory; it's absolutely not all overtime, not a life ground down to nothing.

Advertising used to be a genuinely enviable industry. Anyone who has to maximize the effect of their work through effort faces this.

**Q:****What most recently frightened you?**

**Liu Yang:** Realizing my drinking capacity and my memory are both declining — maybe I should adjust some of my lifestyle habits.

**Q:****In what moment or situation do you feel you've fallen behind the times?**

**Liu Yang:** Every day I feel I've fallen behind. Especially now, with the whole communication environment evolving at breakneck speed. Reading Moments, I find there's so much I don't know — that's already the normal state.

I'll allow myself to fall behind on the hot stuff — whatever's being hyped every day, who's feuding with whom, mutual exposés, what's happening in someone's family. It's fine not to know; there's no need to be the best-informed person in all of that. Professionally, you have to read more and keep up.

**Q:****Where do you get new knowledge and new perspectives?**

**Liu Yang:** One: look at the best work by the best people in the industry — that's something you must always do.

Two: seek out cross-industry material — art, poetry, literature, film. Like running a company, don't confine yourself to one spot; stay open and curious.

Three: talk with the people around you. "Why did you choose this particular thing?" is a question that stays on my lips, and the answers are always surprising. Take why someone buys a certain brand of phone — there's no top or bottom of a contempt chain; everyone has their reasons.

**Q:****How do you imagine your consumer?**

**Liu Yang:** Never imagine the consumer as someone just like yourself. The consumer is a living being with an independent mind; the environment they live in, the air they breathe, the food they eat — all of it is deeply connected to the judgments they lean toward. You have to be interested in the reasons behind their choices before you find the key to the problem. The attitude of "if it moved even me, it can't fail to move them" — I consider that unprofessional.

**Q:****In the ad industry today, what do you find hardest to understand?**

**Liu Yang:** Back to brand value: the industry's most fundamental thing is to shape, form, and deposit — to create a kind of brand value. That's something you have to know from day one. But often people set the "root" aside and do things for the "branch" — things that look novel, that lots of people went to see, but that never settle and accumulate into brand value.

**Q:****If you had to give yourself a slogan, what would it be?**

**Liu Yang:** A piece of work I've especially loved lately is the Under Armour ad featuring Michael Phelps, made by David Droga's Droga5. There's a line from it: "What you do in the dark, that puts you in the light."

A Droga5 work: the Under Armour film 《Rule Yourself》 | From [Tencent Video](http://v.qq.com/x/page/c0188eafy7x.html)

**Q:****What talent do you most wish you had?**

**Liu Yang:** Managing myself. Knowing when to let my passion spill out instead of controlling it; being less driven by other desires, and keeping the most creative mindset.

Honestly, it's too hard. One: after so many years of education, you constantly suppress the ways you're different, suppress your imagination. Two: in the position I'm in now, there are plenty of temptations.

**Q: If you could give some advice to young people who want to enter the ad industry, what would you say?**

**Liu Yang:** In an industry that needs inspiration and creativity to solve problems, work and life are hard to separate. Archimedes thought of something in the bath; Gu Cheng wrote poems before he was fully awake — was that work or life? You'll find that many good creatives hold several roles at once and do all of them well; how to balance it all is a kind of wisdom.

I hope everyone can have a life. It's hard for an ad person without a life to create. You take in everything happening around you, solidly — nourishment from art, from culture, from everything new around you worth absorbing — but then you solve problems in the most efficient way.

If you love this industry, then enjoy it for all it's worth.
