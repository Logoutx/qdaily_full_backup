During the National Day holiday, we invited 8 young writers to recommend books they consider good. Each has made real achievements in their own field and is happy to share new knowledge and fresh thinking. This is the second writer, Chen Chao.

**It's time to recommend books again.**

**At this time last year, *QDaily* invited a number of interesting people from big accounts on Weibo, WeChat, and Zhihu to recommend a batch of books for us. They included [Zhihu founder Zhou Yuan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15357.html), [Guokr founder Ji Shisan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15272.html), [Zhang Wei, founder of the WeChat official account "Shixiang"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15320.html), [Wu Miao, founder of the WeChat official account "Leviathan"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15303.html), and [screenwriter Shi Hang](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15617.html), among others. The invitation at the time read: please recommend a book you think is good from the past year — it may help your career, or it may simply be good. Chinese or English, no restrictions.**

**This year, we've decided to change the way we make recommendations, just a little.**

**In any era, professional readers are a scarce resource. Their judgments of the value of book recommendations and reviews can become an important reference for how we choose books — and the reviews themselves can even become reading material, as with Tony Judt's *Reappraisals*.**

**Of course, not all of our invited writers reflect on twentieth-century history as heavily as Tony Judt does in his book. They are younger, more interesting, and sharper-eyed; they are not professional writers, but they wield real influence in their own fields and are happy to share new knowledge and fresh thinking.**

**We hope that through this "book recommendation + long review" format we can introduce them to you and offer a little reference for the small matter of reading.**

**Since everyone's experience differs, each person's recommended books and reviews naturally vary in style — and puzzling over those differences is itself rather fun. From October 1 to October 8, we will publish one writer's book list and review each day; we hope you find them useful. If you come across other good professional readers around you, leave us a comment and tell us — you might see their article here before long.**

**This is the second one, Chen Chao.**

Chen Chao is a product manager, a science writer, and a member of the science writers' collective Songshuhui (the "Squirrel Club"). He trained in cognitive neuroscience and now focuses mainly on handset R&D and the mobile internet in countries such as India. So you're definitely about to see a lot of popular science and internet book picks. At the same time, you'll see this young writer's humanistic sensibility — which makes it easy to understand how technology acts on human society.

### **Book Recommendations**

**Text/Chen Chao**

***Growth Hacking* (《增长黑客》)**

Author: Fan Bing

Publisher: Publishing House of Electronics Industry

Publication date: July 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B011BHQSV02328624/?asin=B011BHQSV0&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B011BHQSV0&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161001012433wIojJie6M80fkZsa.jpg-w600)

**The clever little tricks of our industry**

The internet industry doesn't just manufacture "business models" — it also manufactures "job categories." "Growth hacker" is one of the new job categories just manufactured. With the deep anxiety of a practitioner afraid of falling behind, I hurriedly studied the cases inside the book. The word "hack," in computer engineering, sometimes describes a quick and dirty solution — in Chinese roughly "crude, fast, and fierce" — often a choice made under pressure. From that kind of pressure, elegance or grace is hard to come by. And unlike the "flighty" label so often pinned on the internet industry, in the long run the "plays" in these cases all end up "played out." The fast company is really fast fashion — out of style before you've even put it on. How could you not be anxious?

***Life Ascending***

Author: [UK] Nick Lane

Translator: Zhang Boran

Publisher: Science Press

Publication date: November 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B018R4Z0J62329357/?asin=B018R4Z0J6&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B018R4Z0J6&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161001012545MOiU2mDbhWGEIB4u.jpg-w600)

**The genuine article in popular science**

There is more than one kind of good science book, offering more than one kind of value: new knowledge, leisure, a knowing smile. *Life Ascending* belongs to a very rare kind — the deeply satisfying kind. Author Nick Lane starts from biochemistry, and the knowledge he covers runs from the ancient Earth's environment to genes, from the origin of life, DNA, and photosynthesis to vision, consciousness, and death. It can be read as a continuation of the blind watchmaker question: how does such complex life emerge from the simplest physicochemical changes? A science book that can supply the logical chain to answer these questions — we might as well call it the honkaku school of science writing.

***City Gates Open* (《城门开》)**

Author: Bei Dao

Publisher: SDX Joint Publishing Company

Publication date: July 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B012M9A8I02322445/?asin=B012M9A8I0&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B012M9A8I0&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930225207iAO8qYyPvzgIuf9d.jpg-w600)

**Beijing, too, is a palimpsest scraped over and over**

In *Shanghai Homes: Palimpsests of Private Life*, Harvard professor Jie Li uses an exceedingly rare word, palimpsest — originally referring to parchment whose text was scraped off so the surface could be reused for new writing — to describe Shanghai's urban transformations, with quiet significance. Reading Bei Dao's *City Gates Open*, I can't help but drop this bit of pedantry. For a Beijinger like me, born in the mid-1980s, the Beijing of my childhood has been erased and rewritten. Yet only after reading did I realize that beneath the two or the multiple Beijings I have experienced, the old marks of repeated scrapings after the founding of the People's Republic still faintly show through. Symbols like "compound families" (the gated work-unit compounds) and "Beijing No. 4 High School" still appear in my life today, but their meanings have changed. Bei Dao's book is, for my generation, a kind of archaeology of memory.

***A Cultural Tour of New England* (《新英格兰人文之旅》)**

Author: Du Xianju

Publisher: Guangxi Normal University Press

Publication date: November 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B017TWHWFC2328212/?asin=B017TWHWFC&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B017TWHWFC&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930225453vMKwCZSEJnQI9gOL.jpg-w600)

**Worth following the map**

Thoreau's essays were one of my literary awakenings — or rather, Mr. Xu Chi's translations were one of my awakenings. Walden Pond and Cape Cod were both "imaginary place names" from my middle school years. In this book, Du — affectionately known as "Juzi" — carefully sorts out the life stories and geographic details of New England's cultural luminaries, and it reads very easily and entertainingly, making you want to open Google Maps and walk the route as you read. Finally, these place names become concrete, interweaving with ever more "cultural knowledge" into a network.

***How to Read Literature***

Author: [UK] Terry Eagleton

Translator: Fan Hao

Publisher: Henan University Press

Publication date: May 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B00ZOGYRFE2329116/?asin=B00ZOGYRFE&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B00ZOGYRFE&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930225702Hd4DIXYvSjF2z6LT.jpg-w600)

**Acquired taste**

*How to Read Literature* is like a handful of chili flakes: Eagleton moves with ease through *A Passage to India* and *The Turn of the Screw*, having enormous fun with so-called serious literature and literary criticism. "Acquired taste" — a taste acquired through experience. On its face it means liking something because experience has taught you to; underneath, it's saying you know perfectly well it tastes bad but you put on a show anyway. And yet in this book, even the "literary classics" that have largely faded from ordinary readers' lives are given new pleasures by this acquired critical faculty. The author also cheerfully picks apart Harry Potter at some length, then declares the book beneath criticism — he just wanted to show everyone how criticism ought to be done.

### **Book Reviews**

***Polio: An American Story***

Author: [US] David M. Oshinsky

Translator: Yang Xi

Publisher: Tsinghua University Press

Publication date: September 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B013HW150G2328624/?asin=B013HW150G&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B013HW150G&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930225924xuanzstERlcp709y.jpg-w600)

**Walking in their light — the reversals of history in the polio vaccine**

**Text/Chen Chao**

In the Shandong vaccine scandal that shocked the nation, two details caught my attention: one was that the exposed vaccines were so-called Category II vaccines, none of which are on the national mandatory immunization schedule; and yet, at the same time, the list of problem vaccines prominently included the inactivated polio vaccine. This was a little confusing: isn't the polio sugar-cube vaccine required for all children (taken orally)? Why is this vaccine a Category II vaccine that isn't mandatory?

The explanation is actually simple: the sugar cube we all ate as kids is an attenuated vaccine, containing live virus that is almost incapable of causing disease. After taking it, the body develops resistance to the poliovirus. The inactivated vaccine seized in this case, by contrast, is made by inactivating the virus, so it contains no live virus; its protective effect is virtually identical to the attenuated vaccine, but the risk is smaller — the few-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of causing disease that comes with the attenuated version disappears entirely with a well-made inactivated vaccine. But because this vaccine costs somewhat more, many countries still choose to promote the attenuated version. In China the inactivated vaccine is also available, but for now it is classified as a self-paid vaccine.

Don't underestimate the difference between these two vaccines — behind it lies a famous case in the history of science. The story behind it is a lifelong debate between two vaccinologists, and a great human victory over disease. That story is recorded in *Polio: An American Story* (published in Chinese as 《他们应当行走》). The book was originally published in 2005 and won that year's Pulitzer Prize. Its author, David M. Oshinsky, is a historian in the Department of Humanities in Medicine at New York University, skilled at writing about different facets of American history. In this book he reviews the history of twentieth-century humanity's invention of the polio vaccine, and the men and women who played different roles in that history.

Humanity has long been tormented by severe infectious diseases: smallpox, plague, typhoid, cholera, and influenza... Contrary to what we might imagine, polio was never a particularly "important" infectious disease. The reason is a little cruel: compared with the horror and wailing of other vicious infectious diseases, polio was merely an unceasing background hum, usually drowned out. It often infected children, and sometimes attacked adults too, but many patients suffered no lasting after-effects; and once you had been mildly infected, it was hard to be infected again. While vicious diseases raged, polio — flaring up every few years — seemed inconspicuous. But as sanitary conditions improved and the wailing grew fainter, the background noise grew louder. Many infectious diseases strike people living in poor sanitary conditions, but polio prefers children who escaped viral infection in early childhood. From the late nineteenth century on, the disease became an American nightmare. Every few years came a "polio summer": public facilities closed, schools suspended classes, and in some small towns hit by outbreaks, local hospitals nearly collapsed. The result: a few children were paralyzed for life; individual children died when the infection reached the medulla oblongata and they could no longer breathe; and the virus "lay dormant," waiting for another summer of outbreak.

Some diseases produce their own celebrity disease-fighters, and polio had the most famous one of all: Franklin D. Roosevelt. In the summer of 1921, after attending a round of events, Roosevelt felt utterly exhausted, then came down with a high fever, and soon lost all feeling in his legs. At first the Roosevelt family tried to conceal the illness — after all, for a political rising star, "disability" was hardly a plus. But before long Roosevelt repositioned himself, returned to public life, and became a star in the fight against disease. Roosevelt's later political career needs no retelling, but fewer people know that he was also one of the founders of the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis. Because of the disease, he came to know a young lawyer, Basil O'Connor, and the two partnered to found a firm. Unlike Roosevelt, who came from a family of political and business elites, O'Connor was the son of an Irish workingman, bursting with energy and only waiting for his chance to make his mark. As it turned out, Roosevelt had picked the perfect collaborator: over the following decades, O'Connor ran the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis. This foundation virtually "invented" modern philanthropy, attracting both the donations of the wealthy and a steady stream of small contributions. Its fundraising and PR were so effective that it was even accused of hogging the public's attention. But however that may be, O'Connor's money and PR skills backed the career of one man: Jonas Salk, the first inventor of the polio vaccine.

Salk was famous for his diligence; during World War I he had been employed by the army to develop an influenza vaccine. By the time he joined the effort, several laboratories had already thrown themselves into polio vaccine research — and had taken plenty of wrong turns. Salk's approach was the one he knew well: use formalin to inactivate virus samples and produce an inactivated vaccine. Even in the 1940s, this method could hardly be called advanced. The microbiological theory and vaccine-making techniques of the day all aimed to cultivate low-virulence viral strains that would let the human body build resistance without causing disease. Salk's method was dismissed by many scientists, among them the famous microbiologist Albert Sabin. But with huge charitable donations and a relatively simple production method, in 1954 the Salk vaccine underwent its first nationwide test in the United States. The trial was conducted across the country using the double-blind method — the children did not know whether they had received the vaccine or a placebo — to prove the vaccine safe and effective. The foundation hired statisticians to organize the trial, deployed IBM's most advanced machines, and for the first time used the FORTRAN programming language to process the statistical results. In the end, the statistician in charge announced at a grand press conference that the vaccine was 60% to 80% effective. The press conference carried on the foundation's signature PR style, and from then on, despite endless criticism from academia, Salk's life was fixed as that of "humanity's benefactor."

Looking back now, that press conference was probably the peak of Salk's life. For the inventor of the polio vaccine, having no second feat to rival inventing the vaccine would be no great tragedy. But for Salk, what came after was especially ironic. After the press conference, Salk became an angel sent by God to earth, an American hero. Yet the danger of poorly made vaccines remained, and the question of effectiveness was unresolved. The scientific establishment still refused to buy his method — at the time this looked extremely petty, though in hindsight it seems not without foresight. And the loudest voice of mockery belonged to the virologist Albert Sabin.

Sabin was another Jewish doctor who, like Salk, had carved out a place in American medicine on talent and hard work. The difference was that Sabin had a stronger academic foundation, and his approach to developing a vaccine better fit what the scientific establishment "expected." In the race to develop the vaccine, the various laboratories did everything they could, and quite a few of them conducted experiments on children and prisoners — smudged with the ethical shadow that medical experiments of that era so often carried. But Sabin was different: this irascible scientist was the most scrupulous about both science and ethics among all these scholars, and the one with the most remarkable achievements. In 1956, his live-virus vaccine was successfully developed.

Several historical events came together to make the Sabin vaccine's great success. First came a tragedy: the public wanted the government to push Salk vaccine inoculation, but the Eisenhower administration, committed to the small-government ideal, hoped that companies and non-governmental organizations would shoulder this public health undertaking. The result was that the government was first slammed for inaction. Then a massive vaccine contamination scandal erupted — many vaccinated children fell ill, forcing health officials to step down. At the same time, across the ocean, a fresh breeze was blowing from the postwar Soviet Union. The newly installed health officials and biologists hoped to sweep away the gloom of the Lysenko era and vigorously push science forward. As a result, Sabin was invited to the Soviet Union to deploy his vaccine. This time, backed by the Soviet system that could mobilize the whole nation, the new vaccine was administered on a massive scale without double-blind testing — the vaccine we know better: the polio sugar cube. It proved to be a safer, more effective vaccine. American public opinion erupted as well: "Not only space — now we're losing public health to the Soviet Union too." So before long, Sabin's vaccine gradually replaced Salk's, and it became the polio vaccine adopted by nearly every country.

What Sabin never replaced was Salk's public fame. Perhaps because the dispute was too fierce, none of the scholars involved in the vaccine research won the Nobel Prize. Yet almost all the leading scholars other than Salk were elected to the U.S. National Academy of Sciences. Salk continued making inactivated vaccines with his old method, but never succeeded again. In the 1980s he announced he would take on AIDS, only to be mercilessly mocked by Sabin. Even so, he remained humanity's benefactor. The city of San Diego, California, gave him a tract of land, and he built the Salk Institute there. The institute's building is a masterpiece by the great architect Louis Kahn. If anyone really had to design a "temple of science," I suspect it would look exactly like the Salk Institute. And a story has to come to an end: Sabin was the lesser-known "winner."

But the story reversed once more. After both great scientists had passed away, fear of polio had also nearly dissipated; today the disease's name is more often linked with war, ignorance, and poverty. In recent years, wild poliovirus has survived only in Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Nigeria, and interference with and obstruction of vaccination campaigns still occur from time to time. In China, sugar-cube vaccination has been remarkably successful — there has been no large-scale outbreak since the 1990s. Very soon, the World Health Organization may well announce that humanity has completely defeated this virus. At this point, the attenuated vaccine in long use has itself become a rare cause of disease. In its next phase, China will carry out the 2013–2018 Endgame Strategic Plan for polio eradication, and the oral attenuated live polio vaccine may gradually exit the immunization battle. The inactivated vaccine — mocked by the previous generation of virologists — will, by virtue of its properties, begin to replace the attenuated one.

One of humanity's many twentieth-century achievements is the development of the polio vaccine (twice over!). The Salk–Sabin dispute has quieted with the two men's passing; history has turned and moved forward, letting us enjoy the shelter of two great men and of generations of public health workers. The Chinese title of *Polio: An American Story*, 《他们应当行走》 ("They Shall Walk"), comes from the Book of Isaiah in the Bible; besides the history of the vaccine's discovery, the book records what patients, medical staff, charitable organizations, and governments did in this battle. Today that battle may be nearing its end: we can walk without carrying fear in our hearts, because we walk in the light of the many.
