That figure may look decent, but measured against today's market, it can't carry Huayi back to its old number-one spot.

Don't put all your eggs in one basket. Huayi has been burned by its "A-list director" strategy before: done well, it can be glorious; done badly, a single film can inflict heavy losses — as with *Devils on the Doorstep* (《鬼子来了》), *The Emperor and the Assassin* (《荆轲刺秦王》), and *Back to 1942* (《1942》) back in the day. The era of ruling the roost on the strength of one or two famous directors has clearly passed.

Huayi smarted for years from that lesson. But now it has learned to wield the advantage of capital to spread that risk. So it scooped up virtually every established director in Chinese-language cinema, foreign ones included.

When it comes to making films, the success of *Mr. Six* (《老炮儿》) undoubtedly gave Huayi Brothers the confidence to regroup. It then poached Ye Ning, an executive with 15 years of experience in production, distribution, and cinema management, from Wanda, appointing him vice president of Huayi Brothers Media Corporation and CEO of Huayi Brothers Pictures. Right after that, Huayi couldn't wait to roll out its new strategy, ready to make a big push.

At a press conference on March 20, Huayi unveiled the latest wave of its "H Plan" — a slate of 22 new films for 2016–2017. The 2016 slate had actually been announced as early as January this year, while the newly announced 2017 slate is anchored by sequels to classic properties: the third installment of Tian Yusheng's "Ex-Files" series and the third installment of Tsui Hark's "Detective Dee" series.

The slate itself is small news. What deserves more attention is the roll call of directors and producers who showed up to lend support that day: Feng Xiaogang, Tsui Hark, Nansun Shi, Manfred Wong, Guan Hu, Cheng Er, Tian Yusheng, and others — 10 directors and producers in all. Their presence effectively signaled that Huayi is rebooting its "A-list director" strategy.

And it's not just the 10 who attended. Huayi also announced upcoming two-year collaborations with Stanley Tong, Zhang Yibai, Zhang Meng, Teng Huatao, Gao Qunshu, William Brent Bell, Shane Black, Duncan Jones, Ash Brannon, Peter Segal, and more — 27 well-known directors and producers from China and abroad combined.

Leveraging star and name-director assets to boost box-office pull is what Huayi has always done best, and probably what works best. In the past, stars were the one thing Huayi never lacked. But in recent years, under the "de-filmification" slogan and a shift in its core business focus, quite a few stars and directors have "flown the nest" to set up their own shops — Fan Bingbing, Huang Xiaoming, Chen Kuo-fu, among them — including Feng Xiaogang, who once left and then returned. Now Huayi is trying to win them all back one by one, vowing to continue the "director + star" tag-team playbook.

Unlike past practice, though, this time Huayi is letting more capital get involved — the goal being to provide a more solid guarantee against the risks of individual films at the capital level. Take last year: Huayi Brothers acquired Dongyang Meila, the company in which Feng Xiaogang held a 99% stake, for 1.05 billion yuan. (The company was founded on September 2, 2015, with registered capital of just 5 million yuan; Feng Xiaogang held 99% of the equity and Lu Guoqiang 1%.) Its main businesses are investment and production of film and TV projects, screenwriting, planning, and rights trading. Projects currently in the pipeline include the films *Cell Phone 2*, *Never Forget* (《念念不忘》), *If You Are the One 3*, and *Beauties on the Road* (《丽人行》), the TV series *12 Love Letters* (《12 封告白信》), and variety shows. After the acquisition, Feng Xiaogang will provide resources — even serve as producer — for new directors under the banner, helping them develop film projects.

The acquisition, however, also involved a "guaranteed profit" betting agreement. According to the *Equity Transfer Agreement*, Feng Xiaogang and the other two shareholders must generate annual profit of at least 100 million yuan, increasing by 15% year over year. If they fail to meet the target, Feng Xiaogang and the other former shareholders must make up the shortfall against Huayi's annual performance target in cash (or in another form acceptable to Huayi).

Clearly, Huayi is now demanding more of its directors in terms of discipline.

At the March 20 press conference, Feng Xiaogang appeared for the first time representing Meila Pictures. He not only introduced the company's core team but also worked hard to plug several new projects, and pledged his loyalty to his old employer: "You can rest assured about Meila — it will be a film company that delivers high-quality movies and high returns at the same time." Soon, *I Am Not Madame Bovary*, Feng Xiaogang's new film as director, will hit theaters — the first film Meila Pictures has co-invested in and distributed.

Although the Chinese film market has changed considerably and is expanding rapidly, hitting an annual profit target of 100 million yuan in today's environment may not be easy.

Feng Xiaogang's most commercially potent period can be traced back to 2004's *A World Without Thieves*, which took 120 million yuan at the box office that year. For several years after, he rolled out one blockbuster after another: *Assembly* (2007) ultimately grossed 260 million yuan, and *If You Are the One* (2008) took 250 million yuan.

Those years of box-office results from Feng Xiaogang's films kept Huayi perched atop the box-office charts. According to the prospectus from Huayi Brothers' 2009 IPO, Feng's *Assembly* and *If You Are the One* accounted for 40% of the company's film-business revenue and 18% of its total operating revenue at the time.

Not all of Feng Xiaogang's films performed: 2006's *The Banquet* took only 130 million yuan, and 2012's *Back to 1942* was a severe blow to Huayi. Over the past 20-plus years, the box office accumulated by Feng Xiaogang's personal brand has exceeded 3 billion yuan, but his drawing power today plainly can't be mentioned in the same breath as back then. ([We've touched on this in a previous article.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/22724.html)) So what looks like Huayi handing Feng Xiaogang a "big red envelope" is, in fact, a risk agreement written for one of its own — one that coldly states: we've given you all our support and trust, but the box-office risk is yours to carry.

Huayi Brothers' peak should have come around 2010, and Feng Xiaogang's contribution then was undoubtedly the largest. *Aftershock*, released that year, brought Huayi Brothers 650 million yuan in box office and 172 million yuan in revenue (box office plus rights), and helped push Huayi's share price up for days on end. *If You Are the One 2* hadn't even opened before it drove Huayi's stock to its daily limit, putting it at the top of the ChiNext board. That year, Huayi's film business revenue also doubled, rising 209.39% year over year. Yet even in that prolific, high-return period, Huayi Brothers' net profit had only just surpassed 100 million yuan. [According to Huayi's 2010 annual report, it recorded operating revenue of 1.072 billion yuan for the year, up 77.4% year over year, and net profit of 149 million yuan, up 76.46%.](http://media.china.com.cn/cmcy/2015-08-06/480184.html)

Starting in 2011, Huayi gradually went downhill. Especially after its blockbuster year of 2010, its medium-budget 2011 films — *Snow Flower and the Secret Fan*, *Magic to Win*, and others — all ended with dismal box-office runs.

In 2012, Huayi came roaring back. *Painted Skin: The Resurrection* and *Chinese Zodiac* performed well, but the box-office failure of *Back to 1942* reversed the tide: the film's first-week (four-day) cumulative gross was only 140 million yuan, far below Huayi's expectations — a result that directly drove down Huayi Brothers' share price. [News reports at the time said the episode erased 1.3 billion yuan from Huayi's market value.](http://v.qq.com/cover/m/moo4weidxn74i33.html?vid=f0011u2vus1) The film ultimately grossed 360 million yuan; if its cost really was the officially stated 210 million yuan, then the damage to Huayi was a loss of no less than 100 million yuan. It also directly hit Huayi's 2012 annual report: net profit growth that year was just 20%, the lowest since its 2009 listing. The biggest winner among private companies that year was Enlight Media, which released *Lost in Thailand*.

Starting in 2014, Huayi ceded the top spot among private film companies at the box office (Enlight led with a 19% market share).

Film is no longer Huayi's most profitable business — that role has been taken by gaming, thanks mainly to the companies Huayi acquired, Ourpalm and Yinhan Technology. Compared with film, the steady growth of the gaming business is far more worry-free. Thus "de-filmification" became the consensus of everyone at Huayi.

Now, facing the film market once again, Huayi is setting requirements for the box office — and for the A-list directors behind that box office: if you can't guarantee a hit, then keep costs under control first. That job has been handed to Director Feng, and to the other directors and producers collaborating on this H Plan.

In returning to film, Huayi has gone from a company with a burning passion for cinema to a cold-eyed capitalist. Although the specific figures and terms of the new partnerships aren't visible, the box office the big-name directors will contribute can be estimated with some rationality.

Judging by box-office pull in recent years: Feng Xiaogang's 2014 New Year film *Personal Tailor* got poor reviews but still grossed 700 million yuan, while last year's *Mr. Six*, in which he starred, took 900 million. Overall, in today's film market, Feng Xiaogang's single-film drawing power should hold at around 800 million yuan.

Next, Feng Xiaogang will release *I Am Not Madame Bovary* in 2016, followed by two sequels to classic properties — *Cell Phone 2* and *If You Are the One 3* — plus the film *The Songs Fading Away from Me* (《歌声离我远去》), drawn from his own youth, and two more Meila Pictures productions, *Never Forget* and *Beauties on the Road*. A rough estimate puts Feng Xiaogang and his company's box-office contribution to Huayi over the next two years at no less than 3.5 billion yuan.

Guan Hu, who won extra favor from Huayi by directing *Mr. Six*, will make his producing debut in 2017 on the spin-off film *The Young Master* (《小爷》). His 2013 directorial effort *The Chef, The Actor, The Scoundrel* grossed 270 million yuan; conservatively estimating his single-film pull at 400 million yuan, Guan Hu should bring Huayi around 500 million yuan in box-office returns.

Jackie Chan is no longer just a kung-fu action star — he began trying his hand at directing and producing long ago, and the *Chinese Zodiac* he directed in 2013 took 880 million yuan. This year he will team up with Stanley Tong to release *Kung Fu Yoga* in the 2017 Lunar New Year slot. *The Dragon Shakes the World* (《龙震天下》), made with *GoldenEye* director Martin Campbell and actor Pierce Brosnan, is also due out in 2017. Crunching Jackie Chan's past box-office clout: his 2015 vehicle *Dragon Blade* grossed 740 million yuan, and 2013's *Police Story 2013* took 530 million yuan. A conservative estimate puts his single-film drawing power at around 600 million yuan — meaning his contribution to Huayi's box office over the next two years could exceed 1.2 billion yuan.

In the new slate announced under the "H Plan," Tsui Hark's "Detective Dee" series drew considerable attention. The first film, *Detective Dee and the Mystery of the Phantom Flame* (2010), created a National Day holiday box-office miracle, taking 286 million yuan. The second, *Young Detective Dee: Rise of the Sea Dragon* (2013), grossed 590 million yuan; two years on, Tsui Hark's pull hadn't diminished one bit. And his 2014 New Year film, *The Taking of Tiger Mountain*, raked in over 800 million yuan. On that trajectory, the third "Detective Dee" should gross at least 500 million yuan.

Another series director worth watching is Tian Yusheng, who specializes in low-budget romantic comedies. In 2014 he scored 128 million yuan over the Lunar New Year slot with *Ex-Files*, and 2015's *Ex-Files 2* also brought in nearly 250 million yuan. By that rough math, the third installment should open at around 200 million yuan.

Conservatively — estimating and projecting solely from the box-office pull of directors like Feng Xiaogang, Jackie Chan, Guan Hu, and Tsui Hark — the box-office returns they'll deliver to Huayi over the next two years (not the same as final revenue shares) will come to roughly 6 billion yuan. And that doesn't even count the contribution from the foreign directors.

6 billion yuan. The number looks great. But it must be said: compared with Huayi in its golden age, when total box office was only 1 to 2 billion yuan yet the company claimed around 30% of Chinese film ticket sales and ranked first in market share among all private film distribution companies. Starting in 2014, under the "de-filmification" goal, the box office of films distributed by Huayi Brothers fell to 7% of the full-year domestic box office.

And now — perhaps in the future, when Chinese cinema comes up — it won't be the single standout, the first production company that comes to mind.
