For now, it looks more like a triumph of marketing than of the product itself

Last March, Uni-President launched a bottled tea drink called Xiaoming Tongxue. Its taste was a faint, mild tea flavor — not particularly memorable, but not off-putting either. Then, in Uni-President's recently released 2015 annual report, it became a "hero product."

Uni-President's revenue and net profit both surged last year, with net profit up 192.26% year on year to 830 million yuan. The company credited Xiaoming Tongxue and "Hai Zhi Yan," launched in the summer of 2014, with a combined contribution of 2.5 billion yuan in revenue — nearly 20% of total beverage sales.

Food companies are always extremely cautious about launching new products, because the odds of success are low and the risk is often seen as far outweighing the opportunity. [According to Zhu Danpeng, an analyst at China Brand Research](http://www.chinanews.com/business/2015/12-22/7681499.shtml), "From R&D through production to promotion, launching a new beverage costs around 60–80 million yuan, and you need it to sell well for two years just to break even." Uni-President actually launched more than 10 drinks last year, but Xiaoming Tongxue is probably the only one that can be called a success so far.

On Zhihu, discussions sprang up around ["What factors are behind Xiaoming Tongxue's hot sales"](https://www.zhihu.com/question/29994107) and ["How would you evaluate Xiaoming Tongxue's packaging design"](https://www.zhihu.com/question/29702828), among other threads. Xiaoming Tongxue won a gold award at the 2015 Pentawards, the international packaging design awards. Pentawards is the world's first and only design competition devoted exclusively to product packaging, regarded as the "Oscars" of the packaging design world. The other award-winning Chinese brand was Nongfu Spring, whose much-talked-about high-end water in glass bottles took the 2015 platinum award.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702125034x3fmUJPRuVXOqDCl.jpg-w600)

Large swaths of orange, brick red, fruit green and blue wrap the bottle so completely nothing shows through, and a wide-eyed, exaggerated-expression Xiaoming Tongxue takes up nearly two-thirds of the label — a far cry from most beverage makers' obsession with showing off the translucent tea inside. The name, too, gives little hint that it's a tea drink. But whether it's the more eye-catching packaging or the more attention-grabbing name, these are time-honored tactics for food and beverage companies trying to draw in consumers.

"We think Xiaoming Tongxue's biggest difference is that it dropped a very different kind of bomb into the tea drink category," Uni-President told QDaily. That "bomb" is a tea drink that claims to target the post-95s. Before this, no beverage had ever explicitly been positioned for a specific age group — or at least no company had said so publicly.

Why the post-95s? This group, the youngest of them 16 and the oldest barely in their early twenties, is arguably the youngest consumer cohort today. And young consumers are always the hardest to figure out. Most brand marketers can recite insights like "loves trying new things," "pursues individuality," "has a mind of its own" — observations that sound perfectly reasonable — but few have any real certainty about any of it. Yet with an eye on future markets and revenue, big companies are full of enthusiasm for young consumers.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702125035pnf4rl9Bs6J7MiGx.jpg-w600)

More importantly, the trend of consumption "upgrading and renewal" has become increasingly visible in the Chinese market. Consider Uni-President's best-known tea drinks, "iced black tea" and "green tea" — even though many people never figured out how they differ from Master Kong's versions, those were things from 1995 and 1998. With so much choice available today, it's very hard to win the mass market with a "product" alone; you have to be different.

A more immediate predicament for Uni-President: 2014 was the first year since 2009 that its beverage business revenue declined. The company's annual report shows tea drink sales of 5.526 billion yuan and juice business revenue of 3.939 billion yuan, versus 6.143 billion yuan and 4.259 billion yuan respectively in 2013.

According to the latest data from market research firm AC Nielsen, overall tea drink sales shrank 3.6% in 2014. Beyond the impact of a cooler-than-usual summer, one major reason was consumers shifting between categories. Moreover, the overall juice market declined 1.6% in sales value and 4.7% in volume in 2014, and the milk tea category was also slowing, with total sales value up just 1.1% over 2013.

The post-95s, then, are less a product positioning for Uni-President than a talking point. What the company wanted was to put out a product young people would like.

But the industry consensus is that tea drinks are not young people's favorite beverages. The problem Uni-President had to solve was getting its so-called post-95s to notice the product — and be willing to buy it and give it a try.

"Healthier than soda, more flavorful than water — tea drinks hit the sweet spot for young people," says Wang Kaiyi, general manager of Leo Burnett Shanghai. Young consumers have always had a demand for tea drinks, she argues; there just hadn't been a product that "speaks to young people" specifically. Leo Burnett was the creative agency for Xiaoming Tongxue, involved in the entire communications strategy from the very start — responsible for designing the brand's tone and producing the TVCs.

Beyond Leo Burnett, Uni-President also brought in BRAVIS, a well-known Japanese packaging design firm, along with Nielsen, a professional brand marketing consultancy and market research company, and SOWHAT, among others, to participate in the early market and consumer research. "We work with many research firms, both international and domestic, and we rotate and mix them depending on the research purpose and each firm's strengths," Uni-President said. From the moment the idea emerged internally to Xiaoming Tongxue's final launch took nearly a year, and almost half of that was spent on marketing research.

The reason we're interested in this process is that it's hardly news for big companies to develop products aimed at young consumers. Judging by results, Xiaoming Tongxue achieved what Uni-President set out to achieve, and that process might offer some reference for every company that studies young people without being young itself.

Indeed, not one of the people making decisions in that conference room was a post-95. In the earliest stage, Uni-President had to settle some things on its own. This stage relied mainly on an internal review panel — a consumer group made up of company employees and their family members — to give first impressions of the product. Beyond that, Uni-President also set up youth communities called "Xin Yi Ke" at universities nationwide, effectively an external panel of post-90s reviewers, hoping to get consumer input involved as early as possible.

As of last year, Uni-President had established partnerships with 150 universities and Communist Youth League committees, testing new concepts and new brands through product trials, questionnaires, and on-site interactions. Compared with the internal review panel, the focus of this testing was setting standards — for instance, a reliable concept should win at least 75% approval, and so on.

Beyond the fixed partner universities, Uni-President also selected individual colleges to work with. Since college students come from all over the country, the company saw this as an ideal method for a nationally distributed beverage. As the project moved into its next phase, Uni-President brought in professional third-party research firms.

This was when SOWHAT entered the picture. "Cold-brew tea" and the post-95s target audience were the two elements already locked in when SOWHAT took over the project, but whether to explain the concept of "cold-brew tea" to post-95s consumers was the point of the fiercest disagreement.

"We tried to describe what cold-brew tea actually is, using different approaches to explain the process technically, and found that young consumers simply don't care," says SOWHAT founder Ma Qingtian. Consumers' indifference to "cold-brew tea" was one of the biggest surprises of the entire research phase.

Yet Uni-President initially had a strong urge to tell consumers what cold-brew tea was. Ma Qingtian says this happens often in food and beverage research — clients always assume the things they consider important are important.

"Young people don't actually ask many follow-up whys. For a young consumer, knowing cold-brew tea is something new — 'I tried something new' — is enough. Anything beyond that, they won't remember and don't care."

This raises a question: can you tolerate selling people something without telling them what it is? For young consumers, sparking curiosity may be the more attractive approach.

Another dispute during the research was the discussion about "cheekiness." According to SOWHAT's research feedback, the Crayon Shin-chan-style "cheeky" cuteness was very popular among college-age consumers but got negative feedback from younger ones. In Xiaoming Tongxue's case, the core audience skewed toward younger middle and high schoolers, so "cheekiness" was an idea that should be dropped.

But in terms of communication cost, "cheekiness" is a sharp concept that's easy for consumers to remember and very cheap to spread. "Cheeky-cute" was also a popular humor culture among the young. The final solution was to blur it: in the finished presentation, small details — like the design of the WeChat stickers — could be "a little cheeky," but "cheekiness" was absolutely an element to be used with caution in the overall direction.

![Post-95s don't even need words to chat — they can carry on entire conversations with stickers](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702125037KlFJMgT9kchIyxjr.gif-w600Gif)

There were many more disputes and compromises like this. The team also spent a long time hashing out what visual style Xiaoming Tongxue should have. Leo Burnett devised dozens of communication tones for Xiaoming Tongxue at the time, and after studying the feedback SOWHAT's research had collected, it presented more than ten to the client — including "humorous," "funny," "goofy," and "cute-selling," among others.

There were subtle differences among them. "Cute-selling," for instance, skews more adult than "goofy," and "humorous" carries more wit than "funny." Ma Qingtian says, "If you use Ge You's jokes on the post-95s, they won't laugh, because they won't chew over the substance. How much wit and humor should Xiaoming Tongxue pack into a 30-second ad? Our decision was: none needed. They can't digest it."

The final direction settled on was "seriously funny." Once that was set, Leo Burnett would provide multiple versions of the TVC, because funny comes in many types — "Stephen Chow-style," "Guo Degang-style," "Crayon Shin-chan-style," and so on. SOWHAT's job was to show these versions to target consumers and provide suggestions and data to help Leo Burnett keep revising the plans. The cooperation between the two worked like an iterative process of defining and verifying, back and forth.

As for that much-praised packaging, it too was the result of compromise. Uni-President spent nearly five or six months grinding away, trying everything already on the market — glass bottles, frosted materials, cork-style caps, you name it. Some options had to be abandoned because of mismatches with internal supply chain technology. As an industrially produced product, Xiaoming Tongxue's packaging had to strike a balance between individuality and production efficiency.

And the most distinctive feature, the dual-cap design — the cap can be removed to play with or collect — actually began with the idea of gluing two caps together, but due to manufacturing problems the big cap kept falling off. When Uni-President's outreach staff visited universities and high schools, they found kids taking the caps off to do arts and crafts, to draw, or to express their moods on them, or using them to play "cap kung fu" (a game where you flick bottle caps against each other). Some would stack caps of different colors together, or cap someone else's bottle — an unexpected windfall from Uni-President's field visits.

You see, that's more a matter of luck.

![Xiaoming Tongxue's "dual-cap" design](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702125038RjsKmh5lpVoe6qDY.jpg-w600)

Despite all the communications research, Uni-President still had no certainty when Xiaoming Tongxue first hit the market. A year after four flavors launched — Feileng green tea, jasmine-infused tea, lime black tea, and iced orange green tea — Xiaoming Tongxue rolled out a new flavor, "Liuliuda" tea, replacing the mediocre-selling Feileng green tea. The best seller was the lime black tea, "Xiao Huangming."

![Weibo comment counts; data source: [Data Iceberg](http://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/hemingke)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702125038xuTNzIr9cvJARWhd.jpg-w600)

The problem Xiaoming Tongxue may face is this: having become a runaway hit in the beverage world as a tea drink in an entirely new guise, what comes next? After all, in terms of taste, Xiaoming Tongxue is not a product that changes anything fundamentally.

In that Zhihu discussion thread about the hot sales, the most-mentioned factors were the brand name, the packaging, the marketing approach, and the distribution channels — but rarely the flavor.

One anonymous comment reads: "Doesn't taste good. Fooled by the packaging." That is probably the last consumer feedback Uni-President wants to see right now.
