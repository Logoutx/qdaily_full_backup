**“No Forbidden Books.”**

On April 10 forty years ago, the magazine *Dushu* 《读书》 was officially launched. In its inaugural issue, the editorial titled *No Forbidden Books* 《读书无禁区》 caused an uproar: “After ten years of censorship, the Chinese people are hungry for all kinds of good books. But the forbidden zones around books have not yet fully opened, because a fundamental question of right and wrong remains unresolved, the lingering poison of the Gang of Four’s cultural dictatorship still haunts us, and some of our comrades remain haunted by fear. The principle at stake is this: Do the people have the freedom to read?”

In the years that followed, *Dushu* published a series of influential, boundary-breaking essays that heralded the neo-Enlightenment movement of the 1980s—for example, Yu Haocheng’s “Freedom to Publish Is an Important Issue” in the first issue of 1981. Alongside such editorials, the magazine also ran reviews of works including Alvin Toffler’s *The Third Wave*, Erich Fromm’s *The Art of Loving*, Max Weber’s *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, Samuel Huntington’s *Political Order in Changing Societies*, and Boris Pasternak’s *Doctor Zhivago*.

Re-reading these forty-year-old essays today, it is striking how relevant they remain. [Indie musician Li Zhi was banned across the internet on April 12](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62891.html); his social-media accounts became inaccessible and his songs were pulled from streaming platforms. [Director Lou Ye’s new film *The Shadow Play* sparked debate over whether its plot was too melodramatic](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62723.html), but audiences saw a heavily censored version, rendering the discussion largely meaningless.

**Two years ago, *QDaily* spoke with Shen Changwen, who served as editor-in-chief of *Dushu* for ten years. [To mark the magazine’s fortieth anniversary, we are republishing that interview.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/44636.html)** On the meaning of the magazine, business writer Qin Shuo offered a succinct summary. He said:

“At that time, society as a whole—college students in particular—cared about reform and about the world: What kind of world is this today? How can China catch up? There was a sense of responsibility to the age, a prevailing atmosphere. *Dushu* gave us, while we were still relatively young, a softer kind of temperament, a spiritual disposition. At the core of that disposition were elements of enlightenment, of lighting up oneself, of using a universal yardstick to measure the present, of pursuing a transcendent intellectual and spiritual state.”

This week, the tech giants faced an uncomfortable round of congressional hearings. Over two days, spokespeople for Google and Facebook were questioned from opposite ideological directions. On April 10, the House Judiciary Committee demanded they explain why a video of the New Zealand white-supremacist shooting spread so widely through their platforms. A day later, the Senate held a hearing with the blunt theme “Stifling Free Speech: Tech Censorship and the Public Discourse.”

**This week’s “What Exactly Is Antitrust Opposing?” series focused on these two hearings. [The first article traced Google’s evolution and controversies over the past two decades.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62803.html)** As early as 2005, a New York State district court blocked its settlement with book publishers because of the control it wielded over online book information. As Google grew, the more serious and frequent accusations from the U.S. Congress concerned speech control rather than market competition.

**[The second article went further](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62874.html), pointing out that behind this shift lies a simple fact: compared with fifty years ago, tech giants are far larger, making it easier for them to monopolize markets and to possess the power to do harm.**

Yet there is no simple solution to the problems created by monopoly; indeed, people do not even know how to look for one. For the tech giants, the paradox is how to balance free speech with the regulation of harmful content. And regulators still do not know what tools can curb corporate power while protecting all consumers.

[**“QDaily’s Annual Book Recommendations” reaches its nineteenth installment.**](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62048.html)

This week’s theme is the development of cities and their interaction with the people who live in them. In the still-short history of urban development, a general pattern has held. Industrial growth created a working class; the working class flooded into cities and became the dominant population of these spaces. To house them, the first residential neighborhoods emerged. Before long, manufacturing moved from developed countries to developing ones, the working class lost its jobs, and working-class neighborhoods fell into ruin. The only rescue plan that seems viable is for big capital to buy them up and convert them into expensive middle-class apartments.

**Scientific progress remains one of the few bright spots in human society.**

[For the first time, humans have “photographed” a black hole: a fuzzy, warm-orange ring.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62868.html) The M87 black-hole image released on April 10 by the Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration (EHT, the international astronomy project known as the “Event Horizon Telescope”) was undoubtedly the day’s biggest astronomical news. Google also swapped its Doodle for an animation of the M87 black hole to celebrate the breakthrough.

Karen Uhlenbeck, the first woman to win the Abel Prize, saw a mathematical universe in soap bubbles. [This long read from *The New York Times* is not easy going, but it at least conveys that advanced mathematics and everyday life are not unconnected.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62793.html)

This week *Why Read* also recommended two books about mathematics. [Morris Kline wrote *A Brief History of Mathematics* 《数学简史》.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62817.html) Across twenty-five centuries, mathematics has weathered repeated crises: the challenge of non-Euclidean geometry to Euclidean geometry, the discovery of irrational numbers and the expansion of number systems, and the analytical dilemmas brought by calculus. Through the major events in mathematical history, he step by step unpacks the evolution of mathematical thought and thinking.

[The essay collection *Spring Night Dialogues* 《春夜十话》 by the Japanese mathematician Kiyoshi Oka is more romantic still.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62799.html) He argued, “The common view is that scholarship depends on the intellect, but I believe emotion is what really matters… For me, studying mathematics is simply to experience the pleasure of studying mathematics.”

**Other business news worth watching:**

[The United States is seeing severe labor shortages.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62687.html) For a long time, immigrants—especially undocumented immigrants—have supplied low-skilled workers. Yet the number of undocumented immigrants in the U.S. fell from a peak of more than 12 million at the height of the 2008 housing bubble to 10.7 million by the end of 2017. That pushed the average hourly wage for non-supervisory workers in residential construction to US$25.34, more than 6 percent higher than a year earlier and close to the largest annual increase recorded by the government in nearly three decades. Economists worry that the shortage of low-skilled workers could have broader economic effects. Businesses across sectors complain that without immigrants they would lose their workforce.

[Hip-hop artists are often broke before they make it, and Wall Street hedge funds have taken notice.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62710.html) Bloomberg reported that hedge-fund titan Jamie Dinan signs artists to long-term contracts and collects earnings after they become famous. Sometimes artists may have to pay interest exceeding 30 percent of the principal. A legal document shows that producer, songwriter, and hip-hop artist Sean Garrett received US$44,852 from Sound Royalties in August 2018 and, based on estimates of his advance-year income, would have to repay US$74,174 over the next three years.

[More and more American fast-food chains are adding bacon to their burgers.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62762.html) NPD food-and-beverage analyst Darren Seifer thinks adding bacon is a good way to boost flavor without much added cost. Conversely, adding bacon lets chains raise burger prices with a straight face. When customer traffic is flat, it is a direct way to increase revenue.

[Luckin Coffee, which had been expanding frantically, now seems to show signs of crisis.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62752.html) After pledging its coffee machines, milk refrigerators, and bean silos as collateral, the company secured 45 million yuan in financing. Previously, Luckin Coffee had used heavy subsidies to acquire users, and the result was strained cash flow. In the first nine months of 2018, it sold 80 million cups of coffee, generated 375 million yuan in revenue, and posted losses of 857 million yuan.

After the explosion in Xiangshui, Jiangsu, [the local government adopted the kind of one-size-fits-all solution that is all too common in China.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62776.html) Jiangsu issued a draft plan for the rectification and upgrading of the chemical industry, aiming to reduce the province’s chemical-production enterprises to 2,000 by 2020 and no more than 1,000 by 2022, and to require the 34 enterprises located within one kilometer on either side of the Yangtze River’s main and tributary channels but outside chemical parks to withdraw, in principle, by the end of 2020. The sheer number of chemical plants to be shut down in such a short time will obviously have no small impact on Jiangsu.

[The casual sneaker brand Allbirds, popular in Silicon Valley and New York, opened its first store in China](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62832.html), located at HKRI Taikoo Hui in Shanghai, while its Tmall flagship store and Chinese official website went live at the same time. Allbirds’ selling point is that it turns premium New Zealand wool into everyday footwear and sells it at an accessible price of US$95.

[JD.com has recently seen a wave of changes and adjustments.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62841.html) In addition to executive and management-structure shake-ups, JD.com may cut 8 percent of its workforce, involving 12,000 people, with logistics one of the key departments affected. Previously, JD.com eliminated base salaries for couriers, hoping to motivate them to collect more packages by increasing performance-based pay. JD.com later acknowledged the adjustment to its pay structure. In addition, starting in early March, JD.com began enforcing a 996 work schedule.

**A few other interesting things:**

[Netflix has released the nature documentary *Our Planet*.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62746.html) The name *Our Planet* easily brings to mind BBC’s *Blue Planet* or *Planet Earth* series. In fact, *Our Planet* was created by the team behind *Planet Earth*. David Attenborough, the signature voice of nature documentaries, also narrates *Our Planet*.

[Mindfulness practice is catching on in some armies, including the U.S. military.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62735.html) Major General Walter Piatt believes the best way to make decisions draws on an ancient and powerful tradition. The major general often begins his daily command work by breathing deliberately, relaxing his jaw, and staring fixedly at a palm tree.

[Korean director Bong Joon-ho’s latest film, *Parasite*, has released a trailer.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62778.html) Although the title sounds frightening, it is neither horror nor science fiction. In it, Song Kang-ho plays a father, with Choi Woo-shik and Park So-dam as his children. Lee Sun-kyun and Cho Yeo-jeong play a married couple, and the story revolves around two families.

[In 2017, 11 million people worldwide died from unhealthy eating habits, with excessive salt intake topping the list.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62795.html) A Harvard study previously noted that after the reform and opening-up, as the economy developed, Chinese dietary habits changed significantly: the popularity of red meat, sugary soft drinks, and fast food caused a sharp rise in cardiovascular disease incidence, making it the leading cause of death in China. In 2011, 44 percent of deaths among Chinese over the age of 35 were due to cardiovascular disease.

[Idol talent shows continue.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62791.html) iQiyi’s *Youth With You* (the second season of *Idol Producer*) was delayed, faced ranking controversies, and finally saw nine contestants form a new idol boy band on the twelfth episode aired on April 6, under the name UNINE. Meanwhile, at 8 p.m. the same night as *Youth With You*’s finale, Tencent’s *Produce Camp 2019* (the second season of *Produce 101*) began airing.

[The Shanghai Dramatic Arts Centre at 288 Anfu Road, Xuhui District, opened to the media for the first time after major renovations.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62833.html) The 2019–2020 season, comprising four programming cycles, will form the theatre’s opening year; the ten productions announced so far are *Beautiful Times* 《良辰美景》, *Galileo*, *Skylight*, *One Summer* 《一个夏日》, *Henry V*, *Behind the Lies* 《谎言背后》, *Art*, *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, *The Sailor’s Mystery* 《水手之谜》, and *The Piano in a Factory*.

[The widely used Helvetica typeface has been redrawn and now has a new style, “Helvetica Now.”](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62858.html) In the new family, nearly 40,000 letters, symbols, and code points in Helvetica have been redrawn; to suit different reading contexts, the designs offer more choices in form, weight, slant, and size.
