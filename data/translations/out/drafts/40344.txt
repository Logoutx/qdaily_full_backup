In an exclusive interview, QDaily speaks with Paola Antonelli, MoMA's senior curator of architecture and design, who has been called "the most modern curator."

Paola Antonelli believes that "the age of design has arrived, and it is enough to send us into rapture."

Since being hired as the Museum of Modern Art's senior curator of architecture and design in 1994, Paola Antonelli has been working at one and the same thing: constantly expanding the boundaries of "design" and challenging people's preconceptions about museums.

This December, an exhibition curated by Antonelli—the second fashion exhibition in MoMA's history—will open. It will be MoMA's first major clothing exhibition since 1944, when the last one was curated by architect and designer Bernard Rudofsky under the title "Are Clothes Modern?" That exhibition later came to be known as "the least fashion-show-like fashion show"; Rudofsky gathered together clothing from the World War II era, using it as a medium to explain the relationship between fashion and society.

!["Are Clothes Modern?" Image from: MoMA](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170502000359OC493GYqUNBWxl2n.jpg-w600)

Using clothing to explore contemporary society—this curatorial logic has also been applied to Antonelli's new exhibition. Yet whether fashion should enter the museum remains a contradictory topic (is fashion art?). Vanessa Friedman, fashion critic for The New York Times, once wrote in an article: "Fashion, though famous for its elitism, has in fact long been regarded as the 'adopted child' of art—lacking in creativity and not valuable enough."

Antonelli, however, believes that many people are coming to recognize the power of fashion, and that MoMA should care about it too. She named the exhibition "Item: Is Fashion Modern?", in which objects, rather than designers, are the primary protagonists. She wants people to learn from it where fashion comes from and how it has changed.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170502002543uvjes6OiYc73EUVp.jpg-w600)

![Item: Is Fashion Modern? Image from: MoMA](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705020026026RbveG2NMyHB4WKl.jpg-w600)

Antonelli graduated from the architecture program at the Polytechnic University of Milan, and before being hired by MoMA she worked at architecture and design magazines such as *Domus* and *Abitare*. She says she is not a fashion person, but she reads fashion from her own perspective and always wants to extend her reach further.

In April 2017, at the invitation of Ye Xiaowei—fashion director of Modern Media Group and editor-in-chief of Modern Weekly—she came to Shanghai to attend Shanghai Fashion Week, and during the week took part in a discussion titled "Eyes Wide Open" held at Design Republic. Antonelli was a keynote speaker at the event, where she once again emphasized her view: "Fashion is not necessarily fleeting; it actually has continuity. I looked further into this industry, and I think the time is ripe for us to be discussing fashion right now."

Many young designers came to the event, which delighted Antonelli. "I met a lot of young Chinese designers, and I really like Angel Chen, Museum of Friendship (Wang Tianmo), as well as Uma Wang and Feng Chen Wang." She also felt that the Shanghai she saw was no longer the Shanghai she had heard about while in New York. "Walking the streets of Shanghai, I saw many people wearing all kinds of clothes. They can express themselves very freely, and that is wonderful."

As the curator of this year's year-end fashion blockbuster, Antonelli began preparations a full year in advance. She recruited an advisory board of 14 fashion insiders—including Penny Martin, editor of *Gentlewoman* magazine, and Shayne Oliver, founder of *The Hood by Air* and an artist—to discuss which objects should be selected.

In the end, Antonelli chose 111 objects: the white T-shirt, Levi's 501 jeans, a Casio digital watch, and the little black dress, even the Jewish kippah and the Palestinian keffiyeh. You may still wonder whether these things can represent fashion, but Antonelli feels that "these are fashion designs that reflect the social, economic, and political changes of human society over the past 100 years."

Antonelli wanted to use the exhibition to show the origins and evolution of certain popular elements, and there were many interesting discoveries along the way. For example, everyone believes the style benchmark of 20th-century fashion was set by the wrap dress launched by Diane von Furstenberg in 1974, but as early as 1932, in the Taxi Dress designed by British designer Charles James, you could already see the spiral close-fitting wrap cut. Trace it back further and you would even find that the source of this close-fitting cut lies in the traditional Japanese kimono.

In an interview with QDaily, Antonelli said, "Because I like numbers divisible by 3, I started from 99 and added a few more, and it became 111." When she previewed the Item: Is Fashion Modern exhibition last year, the number of objects made public was 99; this year it grew to 111.

"My selection criteria are influenced by Western culture of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. I'm not saying this list of 111 objects is important to everyone. I can only say that this is my personal point of view—I think it is important, and I want to use it to inspire people to think about fashion from another angle."

Placing the white T-shirt and Levi's jeans solemnly on display at the Museum of Modern Art, welcoming visitors alongside Picasso's *Les Demoiselles d'Avignon* and Van Gogh's *The Starry Night*, may seem less than serious—but it hardly counts as Paola Antonelli's most radical curatorial act.

"If I were given the chance to curate freely, I think I would do an exhibition about nature. We now use nature, take from nature, and we must also find ways to pay nature back. So I want to do an exhibition that explores this question, touching on furniture, digitization, architecture, and other topics," Paola Antonelli told QDaily.

"But the theme of that exhibition would very likely not be approved. Just like my exhibition five years ago—it was not accepted at first."

In the exhibition from five years ago that Antonelli refers to, she unprecedentedly brought 14 video games to MoMA, including Pac-Man, Tetris, The Sims, and Myst.

When MoMA first announced that it would classify video games as part of its permanent collection, The Guardian's art critic Jonathan Jones promptly published an article titled "[Sorry MoMA, video games are not art](https://www.theguardian.com/artanddesign/jonathanjonesblog/2012/nov/30/moma-video-games-art)," a claim that spread widely on social media and sparked heated discussion.

Faced with overwhelming skepticism, Antonelli did not feel wounded. "Whenever you decide to collect something, it always sparks debate," [Antonelli responded](https://hyperallergic.com/74749/a-conversation-with-paola-antonelli-about-momas-video-game-collection/). "MoMA's collection is a whole, and the reason we divide it into departments is that human knowledge is like this—no single person can reach the entirety of human consciousness."

People criticize video games as not being art, but in fact the fundamental curatorial mission running through MoMA's entire history is to expand the very meaning of art, blurring the boundary between "art" and "other" creations—such as design, architecture, film, and fashion.

![MoMA Image from: [rctom](https://rctom.hbs.org/submission/moma-a-museum-that-grows-with-its-audience/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170502002843o0yg5INlX13zxU7L.jpg-w600)

MoMA's founding began with a very simple idea from a group of art enthusiasts and philanthropists: they wanted to build a small museum to give the public an opportunity to engage with contemporary art.

On November 7, 1929, MoMA's first exhibition offered New Yorkers a distinctive perspective on art. Alfred H. Barr, the director at the time, brought the works of Cézanne, Gauguin, Seurat, and Van Gogh into MoMA, and despite the hard times of America's Great Depression, the month-long Post-Impressionist exhibition still drew 47,293 visitors.

Perhaps Alfred H. Barr did not realize at the time that the artists he first exhibited at MoMA would become the brightest names in the history of modernist art, but his vision has guided MoMA ever since. Barr likened the art museum to a laboratory; he encouraged innovation and hoped to break people's conventional notions of what an art museum is.

"Design is the highest expression of creativity—then games are clearly a form of modern design. Good design, like the Roman numerals we have always used, becomes part of our identity. I want people to understand that design is far more than cute chairs; design is everything around us in our lives." In her 2013 TED talk titled "[Why I Brought Pac-Man to MoMA](https://www.google.com/url?sa=i&rct=j&q=&esrc=s&source=images&cd=&ved=0ahUKEwi4irHXjM_TAhUCOCYKHXKUB3oQjhwIBQ&url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.ted.com%2Ftalks%2Fpaola_antonelli_why_i_brought_pacman_to_moma&psig=AFQjCNGNw-izRHOJ0b9P2pH4dhmB-QVxgA&ust=1493741979148712)" (Why I Brought Pac-Man to MoMA), she explained why she had classified video games as part of MoMA's collection. The talk is the most popular of [Antonelli's three TED talks](https://www.ted.com/speakers/paola_antonelli), having racked up 910,000 views.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170502002003nZJR5WvCopGAkh2w.jpg-w600)

In its August 2014 issue, The Atlantic called Paola Antonelli "[the most modern curator](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2014/07/the-most-modern-curator/372297/)." Antonelli always has some bizarre ideas; the strange things she has brought to MoMA include not only video games but also a mining detonator and, well, a bottle of sweat...

In 2013, Antonelli held an exhibition called Design and Violence. She selected a number of iconic objects born after 2001—a year regarded as a watershed in the public's understanding of "violence"—to show their relationship with violence: for example, a 3D-printed handgun, and a mining detonator (a tool used to set off explosions) designed by Afghan students.

Antonelli also innovated on the exhibition format for Design and Violence. She tried to stretch the duration of the show to keep people's curiosity flowing: during the first month she released two objects at MoMA, then added one new object each week, continuing for a full year.

"Design means many things; it is by no means just Eames chairs and Braun toasters. It can also be a history of violence." Antonelli tried to provoke broader thinking about design: it can bring destruction even as it creates beauty, and scientific progress further expands design's capabilities while raising new questions. For instance, as 3D-printing technology becomes ever more widespread, is a 3D-printed handgun ethical?

"Every exhibition I've curated has shown me a different world, as if I changed jobs each time," Antonelli said. The 2008 exhibition "Design and Elastic Minds" is the one she remembers most fondly.

MoMA had in fact held a technology exhibition called "Machine Art" as early as 1934, but like many exhibitions of that era, it was shaped by an unshakable faith in the power of technological change. It displayed the best modern design in America at the time, brimming with infatuation and reverence for technology.

Antonelli, by contrast, always tries to explore further. A year before the exhibition opened, she sought out Adam Bly, founder of Seed magazine, to collaborate on a monthly salon inviting designers and scientists to participate together. In the exhibition she prominently showed conceptual works from both designers and scientists, hoping to use the occasion to show how they present us with the future.

"A good designer should be like a sponge, actively absorbing the information they encounter and transforming it for us to use." This was Antonelli's view of designers in her preview lecture for the exhibition, and it applies to herself as well. Antonelli always places the topic of "design" in a broader context, observing it through its relationship with other things, and then interpreting it in her own way.

![Design and Elastic Minds Image from: [universaleverything](http://universaleverything.com/projects/moma/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170502001322YCvQ5Z7AHikacD9T.jpg-w600)

People always think of museums as libraries, exhibition halls—some solemn, sacred place. Paola Antonelli, however, wants to update people's view of museums through the "down-to-earth" exhibitions she stages. When someone questioned whether the video games she placed in MoMA were in fact nothing more than piles of code, she retorted, "Are Picasso's works then just paint?"
