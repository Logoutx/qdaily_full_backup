The Bar Street Called "Yongkang Road" and Its 13 "Masters" Are All Falling Apart

We've been following Shanghai's recent wave of urban-renewal efforts to restore its historic neighborhoods. The story is still unfolding, and so are we.

Everyone on Yongkang Road more or less feels they are the master of this street. They all have their own ideas about how to plan its future.

### 10:30 P.M.

At 10:30 p.m., David closes up the Pain Chaud bakery at No. 39 Yongkang Road. When he steps out of the shop, the famous bar street is "packed with people." In Shanghai, this narrow 550-meter stretch of Yongkang Road is lined with more than 90 businesses, mostly food and beverage. On the travel review site TripAdvisor, it is described as "a core area dotted with good bars and restaurants."

These days, only at this hour does David, the owner of Pain Chaud, appear on Yongkang Road. But starting in October 2010, he worked at a bar called "Cafe des Stagiaires" ("Interns' Cafe"). Given that 83% of the street's residents are renters, David may have spent more time here than most of Yongkang Road's actual residents.

During his two years at "Stagiaires," David would often wait until 2 a.m. for the street's slightly weary crowds to disperse before hauling the tables, chairs, and awnings lined up along both sides of the road back indoors.

It was four years ago that David and two colleagues from "Stagiaires" took over the La Boulangerie bakery at No. 39 Yongkang Road as partners. They kept the baker and changed the brand name. Now Pain Chaud has opened a new branch in Jing'an Temple, 2.5 kilometers away, bigger than the Yongkang Road one.

![No. 39 Yongkang Road, the Pain Chaud bakery. Image from: [shanghaidaily](http://www.shanghaidaily.com/newsimage/2016/07/20/020160720213850.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824055413MLYofqHagUFVZIw5.jpg-w600)

David knows full well why this business "has never spent a cent on promotion — it all comes down to this street." Last Halloween, as David describes it, "the whole street was a surging mass of people." And so Pain Chaud may have become the latest-opening bakery in Shanghai — nowhere else has enough foot traffic and buzz to guarantee customers late at night.

"But I doubt we'll see that anymore," David said, standing inside Pain Chaud at 10:30 p.m. on August 8.

Yongkang Road has also joined the "Hengfu Historic District Environment Rectification" project that began this May. Not long ago, the same project was carried out on ten nearby streets — Yanqing Road, Fuxing Middle Road, and others — in the form of "removing the five illegals" (illegal land use, illegal construction, illegal business operations, illegal pollution discharge, and illegal residence).

When the news first reached Yongkang Road, most of the street's merchants were half-doubting, half-helpless, unsure what to do. It wasn't until the end of July, when a group of uniformed market-regulation bureau staff came door to door to check each shop's business license, the nature of its premises, and its operating status, that the news of Yongkang Road's impending rectification was confirmed.

Starting August 1, the tables and chairs that once stood outside Yongkang Road's shops — often considered the street's signature feature — were strictly banned.

![On August 1, some outdoor tables and chairs were forcibly confiscated.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824060247NpWx3gXUQasmnPyJ.jpg-w600)

Wang Xiaoyang figures "it doesn't matter if there are no tables; they don't need tables." A few days earlier, she had filmed a six-minute short film, *Yongkang Road: Goodbye, See You Again or Not*, and when she came to shoot her last footage on Yongkang Road on July 31, she was still fairly optimistic.

But David later noticed that without the outdoor seating, "the crowd instantly shrank by half." More importantly, the street's unusual vibe made many people assume its shops must all be closing anyway.

Even as David said "everything is up in the air," he also seemed to have made peace with shutting down. Pain Chaud's business license has been framed and hung high on the wall directly facing the front door — though he didn't mention that the address on the license doesn't match Pain Chaud's actual location. These kinds of issues swirling around business licenses are common among Yongkang Road's merchants.

But the street's unspoken rules and a special "arbiter" let such problems be glossed over for a time. These people came here, invested a hefty sum to renovate their storefronts, hired dozens of employees, and became one of the street's masters — they hoped the street would get better and better, and their business along with it.

But now Pain Chaud may be ordered to close. David says he'll take the staff to the new Jing'an Temple branch — it's big, just barely big enough to hold them all.

### 2016

An employee of Paifeng Company looked somewhat displeased at not being notified of a "Yongkang Road comprehensive rectification media briefing" held on August 5, 2016. "It's definitely unreasonable as far as our boss is concerned," this employee, who wished to remain anonymous, said of the rectification. She had been on the job for only a short time, but said she had "heard about" the history of the street's investment promotion and had "taken part" in the street's daily management work—

Under a self-imposed street "10 p.m. rule," every night at 10 p.m., Paifeng Company employees would join several street workers in urging businesses to switch off their neon signs and herd the merry-making customers indoors. Her shifts happened to fall on Friday and Saturday nights. But the measure was never very effective to begin with and soon became a mere formality. A few months ago, a family on the fourth floor of a Yongkang Road building — they had just had a newborn — moved out because the baby kept being woken in the early morning. They had lived there for more than a decade.

Most of the time, though, Paifeng Company kept the street running in a simple, effective way, with considerable authority. Since its founding in 2009, it has been like a "clan elder" standing between merchants and the government, between the local gentry and the throne.

The company's full name is Paifeng Yongkang Commercial Management Company. According to corporate registration records, it was formed as a joint venture between the US-invested Paifeng Group and Xinhui New Group, the Xuhui District Non-Staple Food Company, and the Xuhui District Grain and Oil Company (Xinluda Group). Paifeng Group holds a 70% stake, while the other three government-backed companies hold 30%.

Largely to ensure the implementation of a project then called "Phase One of the Yongkangli Specialty Block," Paifeng Company was granted 13 years of operating rights over Yongkang Road by Xuhui District. The project required "unified renovation of Yongkang Road, packaged as a fashionable, modern specialty block."

But the early operations didn't go smoothly. It wasn't until Paifeng Company brought in the street's first food-and-beverage business, "Stagiaires," in 2010 that it finally escaped a year of dismal tenant performance. In director Wang Cheng's documentary short *Yongkang* (《永康》), Paifeng Company once claimed it had completed the investment promotion in just one week. But it later turned out that the small shops selling clothing and handicrafts simply couldn't survive on Yongkang Road — only restaurants and bars were viable.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824063751N45BLzTMVdH7heJ8.jpg-w600)

And as merchants poured in, the street's landlords quickly realized that if they bypassed Paifeng Company — which amounted to a sublessor — they could charge higher rents. So starting in 2013, few landlords were still willing to lease their properties to Paifeng Company. Paifeng Company adjusted the way it inserted itself into the street accordingly: it charged a management fee and administered the merchants uniformly — for instance, regularly convening merchant meetings, coordinating street affairs, and sharing outside information.

Now, "roughly 80% of Yongkang Road's merchants are connected to our company," this anonymous employee said, crediting Paifeng Company's position to the "government." "At the very beginning, we were building this street jointly with the government. We took part in the design, filed records with the housing authority — without the government, how would we have had the power to transform it?"

According to information QDaily obtained from multiple sources, "jointly building" meant this: among the more than 50 shops on the eastern section of Yongkang Road, some were housing that could not legally be used for commercial purposes. But in order to clear out the roadside wet market that then plagued Yongkang Road as quickly as possible — and to "placate the residents upstairs" — the Tianping Road Subdistrict, which administers Yongkang Road, once provided Paifeng Company with dummy addresses to make it easier for the latter to obtain business licenses.

"If only we'd pushed all the way during that 'by-any-means-necessary' era!" this Paifeng employee said. "We should have done it all at once back then — first floor to third floor, on a bigger scale." That way, the Yongkang Road of her ideal wouldn't have been thrown into endless disputes over who is the rightful master, disputes hard to judge as right or wrong — and perhaps it wouldn't have become so conspicuous that it now carries "a reputation for over-commercialization" and needs thorough rectification, threatening the "master" status of "street planner" Paifeng Company.

!["Remove the Five Illegals" notices posted on Yongkang Road after August 1.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824064335ViHaRrgmyqu4h1W3.jpg-w600)

In the merchant survey conducted in July this year, the market-regulation bureau didn't bring Paifeng Company along. And when merchants turned to Paifeng at that point, it no longer represented any authority — it said the information it held was almost the same as what the merchants had.

This new employee said she saw the sudden appearance of "uniforms of every kind," and that a considerable number of the street's foreign tenants "couldn't understand" what was happening and "lost confidence." That is Yongkang Road's special feature: even as Chinese customers have increased year by year, a shop like Pain Chaud still gets 80% of its customers from foreigners.

![With so many foreigners around, ads for Chinese-language schools can be found here too.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824071025Cqzb0dlKX7nvLkDV.jpg-w600)

### 8 A.M.

Yongkang Road is lined on both sides with uniform aluminum-alloy railings and awnings. These facilities, built around 2009 by the subdistrict's unified construction, later became the infrastructure for businesses to expand their operating area. From then on, the street was almost fated to become famous for its outdoor tables and chairs and its wild roadside drinkers.

At 8 a.m., through a few scattered clues, one can faintly glimpse what kind of night the apartments in these four-story buildings have just been through.

The resident of the small fourth-floor room built on the rooftop terrace may not have felt as disturbed as his neighbors. The furnishings on the square balcony suggest he is very likely a foreigner who moved here specifically to soak up the lane culture. Because there are Western restaurants nearby that he frequents, the sublessor renovated the roughly 50-square-meter room, installing things like an oven that would easily make him feel at home. Apart from the rent of around 15,000 yuan, he has no complaints about the living atmosphere here.

And some residents probably fall asleep to the smell of grease. Because the buildings have residents starting from the second floor, the street's shops theoretically cannot pass the environmental assessment for food service. The residents above The Sailors Fish & Chips are among the few no longer troubled by it. As the landlord of the ground-floor shop, they once lodged a protest, which led the restaurant, in its second year, to rent the adjacent unit as a kitchen and build a ventilation duct to pipe the grease fumes straight onto the roof of the three-story building.

8 a.m. When the restaurants and bars close, the coffee shops and bakeries on Yongkang Road open their doors. Ben, the barista at Pain Chaud, pulls coffee one cup at a time from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Behind the booming business, the surroundings have become "maddening" again.

When he gets off work, Ben has to "squeeze his way out of this street." At 4 p.m., some bars' happy hours also begin. "There are already foreigners drunk on the street. The street is too small and there are too many people; once there are too many, you can't tell who is making the noise," Ben says. "Up to a certain point, a crowd can be quite pleasant — like a dedicated spot for drinking is pretty nice — but too much is no good."

His ideal Yongkang Road would be "in a controllable environment, not too noisy" — perhaps closer to what it looked like when he first came to Pain Chaud in 2012, when "the street was still fairly clean."

But Ben feels "Yongkang Road probably can't be controlled anymore."

Jiji Dry Cleaner's also opens at 8 a.m. These past years, thanks to the lively bars, the owner, Master Yu, has pushed his bedtime past midnight. But he doesn't feel his life has been disturbed by the newcomers. "Noise isn't a problem for me — I sleep late," Master Yu says.

Master Yu's dry cleaner's and the street's Liming sundries shop are the only two businesses on Yongkang Road that haven't changed in over a decade. He opened his shop on Yongkang Road in 1997, and probably few people understand better than he how the street went from the removal of the roadside wet market to its return, then to the decline of the fashion-and-trinkets market and the rise of restaurants.

![No. 71 Yongkang Road, Jiji Dry Cleaner's.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608240702026uaXvjOVfsBQgxmt.jpg-w600)

Master Yu says he doesn't like Yongkang Road at its liveliest; the current version, with outdoor seating no longer permitted, actually fits his vision. "As long as no one operates beyond their door, foot traffic drops a lot. But foreigners definitely aren't interested in sitting indoors — they'd rather sit outside and soak up the sun even in the blazing heat."

But according to another person who runs a business on Yongkang Road, the dry cleaner's is also one of the beneficiaries of the heavy foot traffic. "There are very few dry cleaner's nearby and lots of foreigners around, so his business is very good too. If this street disappeared, his business would definitely drop by at least 30%."

If business really did fall off directly as a result, Master Yu might have to rethink whether he actually likes a lively Yongkang Road.

### 2013

Xiao Ming, the owner of the pizza place "Casa," believes Yongkang Road's foot traffic truly surged on March 6, 2013. He remembers it clearly: that day, the street's "water-splashing" incident made Tencent News. Not long after, the shop's three people could sell nearly 100 pizzas a day. Rents later rose nearly threefold; rent for a space of a little over 20 square meters exceeded 12,000 yuan a month.

The "water-splashing" incident refers to that night when second-floor residents of Yongkang Road splashed several basins of water down onto dozens of foreigners in high spirits below. They were two elderly Shanghai women who had lived there for years, attempting to assert in the most drastic way that it was not Paifeng Company, nor the merchants it brought in — the residents were the masters of this street.

At the time, international media including the British *Daily Telegraph* reported the incident. Afterward, outdoor tables and chairs were explicitly banned, and Paifeng Company set a 10 p.m. "curfew" for the merchants under its unified management: extinguish the neon signs, move customers on the sidewalk indoors, and stop selling alcohol.

But just three months later, the alert was lifted and everything went back to normal. "In hindsight, these measures couldn't solve the problem of disturbing residents," a government official told 13 media outlets at the "Yongkang Road comprehensive rectification media briefing."

The merchants realized the bans were coming before the government did, and they also knew the government wouldn't really step in to handle the matter at the time. They had to find their own solution. Some merchants began sending the two residents 1,000 yuan a month through Paifeng Company, asking them not to let the water-splashing incident happen again.

![The worrying state of the lanes off Yongkang Road.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824071116pPM0Va1czUSZnm8O.jpg-w600)

But most of the street's residents didn't have the two Shanghai grandmothers' fierce approach; they couldn't exercise their rights like true masters. The family of Master Chen on the second floor became a well-known topic of local joking, because every member of the household had a pair of noise-canceling earplugs.

Master Shi, who lives in Lane 65 on Yongkang Road, says he has called the police (110) several times. Master Shi has lived on this street for more than 70 years, and his home is now the only first-floor residence on the street that hasn't been rented out. No one denies his emotional attachment to the street, and he has a strong sense of homeowner's rights. He can rattle off a list of reasons he doesn't want to move away — including the ceiling height of over 3.5 meters, almost impossible to find in newer public housing — on this matter, he hopes an outside force like the police could affirm his homeownership of Yongkang Road and stand up for him.

Master Shi made several efforts, going to negotiate with the bars. At first the merchants acted "quite embarrassed," but later they began to "find him troublesome." "The proprietress said, 'Ah, running a shop is this much trouble?'" Master Shi recalls of the negotiation. "I said, we're residents here. This place is primarily residential."

But before this round of rectification began, almost no one — the government included — would recall that Yongkang Road was primarily residential.

### 7 P.M.

Two or three years ago, when BluBlu came to Yongkang Road at 7 p.m., it was already "starting to go crazy." Because she found it too crowded — "people spill onto the road" — BluBlu decided to stop going to Yongkang Road's bars at night. The place she frequents most is a coffee shop called Cafe del Volcan near the entrance of Yongkang Road.

BluBlu is a contributor to "Penguin Food & Drink Guide," a consumer-review platform. She has explored many streets including those of Shanghai and Ho Chi Minh City, knows a fair amount about food, and is considered able to offer a relatively fair assessment of Yongkang Road.

In late June, BluBlu ate her way through every restaurant on Yongkang Road — though at the time, she didn't know the street was about to be rectified. "The conclusion is that there's really not much worth eating." Coupled with its formerly dirty and messy environment, BluBlu doesn't like the place. "It's very much like a bar street in Ho Chi Minh City, Southeast Asia... the mix of businesses is rich, but the quality isn't as high as I imagined. So how did they get famous?"

If you judged purely by the quality of the food and the environment, it would be hard to explain the nearly 10 hours of bustle Yongkang Road sees every day.

Christopher St. Cavish, an American, is a food columnist with 20 years of experience as a sous chef in high-end restaurants in Miami, New York, and Shanghai. He has lived in Shanghai for 10 years. Last April, he wrote a widely shared article for SmartShanghai, *The Shanghai Soup Dumpling Index*.

Christopher St. Cavish was already hanging around Yongkang Road when it didn't have a single bar. Now he goes to El Luchador, the Mexican restaurant on Yongkang Road, every two to three months. He believes Yongkang Road is important.

"It's like a natural incubator," Christopher St. Cavish says. If the government wants to see an upgrade of the service sector, it needs a relatively cheap place like this — like a street that serves startups. "Here, some shops fail, and the ones that succeed expand elsewhere."

![Yongkang Road before the August 1 rectification — though this is far from the wall-to-wall crowds of 2014.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824071400r87IiZyWCA9fo1Gt.jpg-w600)

This is pretty much Christopher St. Cavish's ideal Yongkang Road: a "naturally grown" "downtown" (downtown here referring to old districts like Lower Manhattan and Brooklyn in New York, as opposed to the newer areas to the north).

Unlike the ambitious, meticulously planned new districts of every city, a "downtown" has no single designer and "allows for more possibilities." If anything could be improved, Christopher St. Cavish says, it would be: "relatively lower rents, so new brands are encouraged; well-managed outdoor tables and chairs that won't disturb the neighbors... a place where Chinese and foreigners mix."

On the evening of August 13, Nicholas, who lives in Hangzhou, came to Shanghai for the weekend and was invited by friends to meet here. Before that, he knew almost nothing about Yongkang Road, but he said, "One evening here is enough to get a feel for it — the street is one way at 9 o'clock and another at 10. Drinking at The Hop Project, I felt the beer here is pretty authentic."

![The Hop Project, which had already been demolished and rectified on August 23.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824071517eINP2sj5z04R6VUx.jpg-w600)

The Frenchman whose Chinese name is Heilong was here for the third time. He doesn't often go to bars, but because a friend was leaving China, they decided to throw a "farewell party" on this street.

After BluBlu's article about Yongkang Road was published on "Penguin Food & Drink Guide," its backend received more than 200 comments, many saying "I really want to go back one more time." Still, the unpleasant experience at 7 p.m. left BluBlu feeling that "this place was always going to be torn down."

"If I went there at night and I were a resident, I'd absolutely hate it. To me, this place is a very deformed existence," she says.

### 2011

But only a few shop owners truly understand (rather than merely see) how this somewhat "deformed" existence came about.

"Stagiaires" was the first bar Paifeng Company brought to Yongkang Road. At the time, it was still squeezed in among the street's previous wave of clothing and handicraft shops. Relying on another of French owner Alexandre Daune's projects — an internship program for French students — "Stagiaires" gained its earliest loyal customers.

Sean, a man from Xinjiang, often passed by Yongkang Road. In the cold of winter, he saw students standing outside "Stagiaires," smoking and drinking, no tables set out, yet seemingly carrying their own buzz.

In 2011, several snack shops opened on Yongkang Road: Hesheng Sushi, and the craft-beer house Cheersin. A Taiwanese sausage shop wasn't doing well and planned to transfer its tiny stall, which had only three or four seats. Sean decided to take a chance and open a British fish-and-chips shop on this street "where foreigners come and go." He took over the stall, thinking, "this street might just develop into something."

The personnel on the street and the ownership structures of the shops soon became tangled and complex — and this, first of all, marked the birth of a new batch of masters. Another Frenchman opened a bar called "Revolution Road," which initially held mutual stakes with "Stagiaires." The Australian owner started with the Irish bar Blarney Stone and then opened El Luchador, the wrestling-themed Mexican restaurant. And then there was Xiao Ming, who moved from Sheshan Golf to Yongkang Road and opened Casa, a Western restaurant focused on thin-crust pizza.

Sean has opened three brands and five restaurants in Shanghai, two of them on Yongkang Road. Because of the expansion of their respective outlets, Sean's The Sailors and the bakery Pain Chaud eventually ended up with two shops each, becoming next-door neighbors.

![The Sailors fish-and-chips shop. Image from: The Sailors](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824072655XmVaIbGNFgPpfWsL.jpg-w600)

Now, almost every established Yongkang Road shop owes thanks to "Stagiaires" for bringing foot traffic to the area. At its peak, seven shops on the street were operated with Alexandre Daune's involvement, and he looked like the decisive figure in the street's life or death. By now, though, Alexandre Daune has already transferred all of them.

And according to 2014 statistics, 83% of Yongkang Road's population lived apart from their registered households — meaning finding a master in the sense of property ownership isn't easy either. Lawsuits between merchants and landlords are sometimes handled with the "fourth landlord."

Mobility, absence — none of this changes the fact that everyone on Yongkang Road more or less feels they are the master of this street, and they all have their own ideas about how to plan its future.

Barista Ben is therefore upset. Just like Jing'an Villas, where people "used to go on dates as kids," the places people regularly visited on Yongkang Road may soon be impossible to find at all. "It's hard to know the reasons; you only know the result — it's demolished."

### Now

At No. 82 Yongkang Road, viewed from the roughly one-meter-high outer wall, it is an old-style residence, with a jujube-red wooden door and wood-framed glass windows.

At 11 p.m. on Saturday, August 13, it received a long-haired female driver in a red Ferrari. She parked in front of Blarney Stone and walked straight into Big Movie, this not-yet-demolished video shop. Until 2 a.m., the "foreigners" — some who had come specially on mopeds with French bulldogs in tow, others clutching beer bottles and spending the weekend at nearby bars — all walked through its doors. For just 12 yuan, they could watch the latest pirated Hollywood movie.

![Behind the jujube-red wooden door in the middle was once a pirated-DVD shop.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824072016BGnyHrTQhAUoeN5z.jpg-w600)

At 11:15 p.m., several rickshaws were parked at the intersection of Yongkang Road and Xiangyang South Road. Decorated with colored lights and blasting upbeat English songs, their drivers were persuading a few half-drunk foreigners to get in, offering to take them off to keep partying.

But a little over an hour earlier, more than ten uniformed staff members had been walking up and down this street. Not many people were surprised by their presence. Some vividly remembered the "Hengfu Historic District" campaign — covering Yanqing Road, Fuxing Road, and others — that had ended on June 30. And they knew Yongkang Road's rectification would begin on August 26.

Four days later, on the morning of Wednesday, August 17, five or six people in construction helmets arrived at Big Movie, followed by a clamor of hammers. A "restricted zone" was cordoned off with satin ribbon outside the shop, with four staff members in special-duty uniforms standing guard. They didn't drive away the onlookers, nor did they scold anyone taking photos; most of the time they were silent or kept their heads down. Across the street sat five "Shanghai Peace Volunteers," and two chengguan — urban management officers — carrying briefcases paced back and forth.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824072244YoSHT7EgcpKe2rq6.jpg-w600)

![On August 17, the scene of the Big Movie illegal-structure demolition.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824072244hUuXygSlN8s0nAba.jpg-w600)

For this group of uniformed people, the day went very smoothly. They shut down a shop with no business license, and swung the first hammer of the Yongkang Road rectification as deployed by their superiors.
