## Like film, the evolution of this new language may take decades, and its final, mature form may be beyond anything anyone today can predict.

Regular article

How does virtual reality at GDC 2016 differ from [GDC 2015's](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/7174.html)? More demos, more talks — the sheer volume of content has forced GDC to spin off a dedicated "VRDC" track. With [Magic Leap](http://magicleap.com) still keeping a low profile¹, everyone's attention remains fixed on the big three: Oculus, Sony, and Valve (HTC Vive). The three have now all announced their prices and release dates — $599 / about ￥3,895 / late March, $399 / about ￥2,594 / October, and $799 / about ￥5,196 / May, respectively — and players who have been teased for over a year can finally start to have something concrete to look forward to.

Even the most optimistic VR believer cannot deny that as a consumer device, the VR headset in 2016–2017 will remain the exclusive domain of hardcore gamers. The prices above cover the headset alone: for the Oculus Rift and HTC Vive, players also need a high-end PC, which adds another $900-plus (￥6,000) to the bill. Even the cheapest option, Sony's PlayStation VR, requires the PlayStation Camera accessory to work at all — sold separately, and a bargain at $44 (about ￥280).

That said, Sony remains the most affordable gateway into virtual reality. According to figures Sony released earlier this year, the PS4 has sold nearly 36 million units worldwide. PS4 owners only need to buy the PlayStation VR and the PlayStation Camera on top. Someone with no game console or gaming PC who wants to jump straight into virtual reality can put together Sony's full setup for a little over 5,000 yuan; the equivalent figure for the Oculus Rift and HTC Vive exceeds 9,000 yuan.

![Image from Visual China](/uploads/image/201603/1bd54737062c.jpg)

Interestingly, the three companies also presented very different styles at the show. Sony's presenters were the most professional, with a childlike excitement that carried real infectious energy. Oculus played it straight down the middle — a startup, but one that already has a touch of the formal corporate politeness common at big companies. By contrast, Valve, the company behind the most expensive headset, the HTC Vive, enjoys legendary status among gamers but still can't match the other two in mainstream name recognition. Its booth sat in a rather dim, out-of-the-way corner — I stumbled across it while buying lunch. The Vive demo room had its lights turned way down, giving off an air of mystery, and the people filing in and out radiated professionalism. Sony and Oculus, by comparison, felt like two little playgrounds.

The VR games shown at this year's GDC represent a big leap over last year in both quantity and quality, but it's still unclear whether any era-defining game will emerge this year — the kind that makes people buy a particular VR headset just to play it. (Plenty of people bought a PS3/PS4 to play *The Last of Us*.) That said, several VR game developers confirmed to me that supporting multiple VR platforms at once isn't hard — far easier than a mobile developer supporting both iOS and Android. Even so, most of the VR games we've seen so far are still built for one specific platform. Clearly, all three companies understand full well how much a platform's fate hinges on the quality of its games, so each has prepared a slate of first-party titles that will, naturally, appear only on their own platform. As for third-party developers, in these dawn days of VR hardware, they typically choose to polish the experience on one platform first.

The three VR games I played this week gave me a feel for VR's potential. They were *Bullet Train* (Oculus Rift), a first-person shooter from Epic Games; Sony's *Playroom VR* (PlayStation VR); and *John Wick* (HTC Vive), adapted from the film of the same name by Lionsgate/Starbreeze.

![Gameplay from *Bullet Train*. Image from [venturebeat.com](http://venturebeat.com/2015/09/26/bullet-trains-virtual-reality-made-me-feel-like-neo-from-the-matrix/)](/uploads/image/201603/683fdd3cf0fa.jpg)

First-person shooters and adventure games fit people's imagination of VR perfectly, and they are the genres developers prioritize when porting to VR platforms. But as with all ports, the biggest challenge is how to give the game qualities unique to the target platform — in other words, to make it feel native rather than ported. *Bullet Train* contains one design detail that shows the designers were thinking along exactly these lines. In the game, the player is inside a subway station, trading fire with thugs in every direction. As everyone knows, one of the biggest problems in VR games is how to handle the dizziness and nausea players can feel when moving through virtual space. When you are aboard a moving virtual train, the problem only gets worse. The *Bullet Train* designers' solution was to turn movement into "teleportation." To get from point A to point B, you aim the Oculus Touch controller at a ring that appears at your destination and press a button to shift yourself over. It's a brilliant compromise. In many people's eyes, the more your behavior in a virtual space resembles behavior in the real world, the more "complete" the VR technology is. But that is like saying a painting is artistically better the more it resembles its model — a pre-modern misconception taken for granted. Moving long distances through space by instant shift may feel "not real enough," but it is a sensible choice given the current limits of VR hardware. What's more, the extra operation itself can become part of the fun.

At a talk on Tuesday, *Bullet Train* team members Nick Donaldson and Nick Whiting said the game's inspiration came in part from Asian action films — John Woo's *Hard Boiled* and Park Chan-wook's *Oldboy*, for instance. The stylized gunfight sequences created by John Woo and martial-arts director Philip Kwok have never lacked devotees in the United States, and the game's designers have now borrowed those moves for *Bullet Train*. We all know the scene: Chow Yun-fat knocks down one man, casually picks up another gun that just happens to be lying nearby, and at lightning speed guns down another thug in the opposite direction. The *Bullet Train* designers turned this routine into what they call "chaining actions." "Shoot one guy, pick up another gun from the next guy, shoot another guy — it makes you feel like a badass," Whiting said.

In the demo segment, the player seems to be immortal. Even when three or five enemies are spraying bullets at you in dense volleys, the rounds turn into *The Matrix*-style "bullet time" slow motion right before your eyes. (Slow as it is, watching bullets whistle past you can still give you a slight start.) More mischievously still, you can use the hand gripping the Oculus Touch to grab those bullets out of the air and throw them back at the enemies! I look forward to the day when Jeffrey Lau's black comedy *92 Legendary La Rose Noire* gets adapted into an interactive VR film.

We can see that the *Bullet Train* designers will always sacrifice VR realism for playability. Historically, squeezing a device's computing power dry has never guaranteed anything in game design. Going back to *Super Mario*, many of the most creative games have been made under severe constraints of computing power and design resources. The pioneering work of VR gaming will very likely share this trait.

![Gameplay from *Playroom VR*. Image from [www.roadtovr.com](http://www.roadtovr.com/sonys-new-vr-party-game-the-playroom-vr-pits-4-players-on-the-couch-against-one-morpheus/)](/uploads/image/201603/f6d6ca4ba600.jpg)

*Playroom VR* is Sony's own multiplayer, family-friendly VR game. Made up of several mini-games, it looks very much like the kind of demo piece that would ship bundled with the PlayStation VR. That sounds as boring as a home-appliance manual, but all four mini-games I played on-site with three strangers cleverly put VR's properties to use. In a cat-and-mouse game, the player wearing the PlayStation VR plays the cat, while the other three, holding regular controllers, play the mice. The cat, hidden behind a curtain, must pounce and grab at the mice with a head-thrusting motion whenever the curtain briefly opens. In another mini-game, the player wearing the PlayStation VR must use a laser beam, guided by the verbal directions of the other three players, to destroy invisible ghosts in the virtual world.

To be fair, *Playroom VR* will most likely end up as a party trifle for when friends come over. Still, more than 50 VR games will be released on Sony's platform by the end of the year, including major titles like *EVE: Valkyrie*. And one thing that must be said: the PlayStation VR is more comfortable on the head than the Oculus Rift and HTC Vive. If, like me, you're a near-sighted glasses wearer, you may have to take your glasses off to wear those two comfortably — but the PlayStation VR gave me no such problem in my testing. Since the Rift and Vive are about to ship, this is probably settled; we can only hope the second generation improves things.

*John Wick* is an adventure-plus-shooter adapted from the Keanu Reeves film of the same name. Because the HTC Vive, like the Oculus Rift, comes with two controllers built specifically for VR interaction, the adventure elements of *John Wick* — searching for clues around the virtual space, observing and manipulating objects, interacting with characters — have ample room to breathe. As with *Bullet Train* mentioned above, the game designers here face a choice: should the game feel "more VR," or should it be more fun? The two are in tension because, at this stage, being inside a virtual environment carries a heavy cost of learning — or exploring. This exploration is novel and fun, but it belongs to the rules of the game, not to its core pleasure. If a bridge player spends the entire time learning the rules, she will likely give up before ever enjoying the intellectual thrill of bridge. Oh, so this telephone receiver can be picked up. Huh? Hold it to your ear like a real receiver and the sound actually does get a little clearer. Pleasure of this kind is perfect for posting to WeChat Moments, but it cannot constitute sustainable gaming happiness. Looking back on *John Wick* now, I am still struck by the strange sensation of riding that old-fashioned elevator as it slowly rose through the hotel, and of pulling the old-fashioned elevator lever — but those things will not determine whether *John Wick* succeeds as game design. Since *John Wick* is slated to launch in sync with the film's sequel, due out early next year, the development team still has a relatively generous amount of time to polish it further.

![Max Planck speaking at GDC. Image from [www.polygon.com](http://www.polygon.com/2016/3/15/11241858/oculus-story-studio-max-planck-gdc-2016)](/uploads/image/201603/011b6181ca7f.jpg)

That virtual reality needs a visual, auditory, and interactive language of its own is, after the repeated reminders of industry forebears, common knowledge. At this year's GDC, Max Planck — a ten-year Pixar veteran and now technical director at Oculus Story Studio — expressed cautious optimism about VR's narrative capacity. Like film, the evolution of this new language may take decades, and its final, mature form may be beyond anything anyone today can predict. But if *Bullet Train* and *Playroom VR* teach us anything, it is that eternal principle: the technology itself doesn't matter. Just as Mario wears a solid-color cap in *Super Mario* because the technology of the day couldn't render the texture of hair, the real skill game developers compete on is how to turn the technology — and its limitations — into style. The "VR" in the phrase "VR games" stands for nothing more than what you see through the coin-operated viewer on the observation deck of the Empire State Building. Successful VR games will certainly not succeed because of the VR.
