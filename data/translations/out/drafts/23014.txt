## At the Oscars, We Saw a Completely Different Hollywood.

Regular article

The 88th Academy Awards have wrapped. Has your excitement worn off yet? Good. Now let's calm down and discuss a serious question: what makes a good movie?

Look back at the 2015 US box office. The five most successful — or most popular — films were *Star Wars: The Force Awakens*, *Jurassic Park*, *Avengers: Age of Ultron*, *Inside Out*, and *Furious 7*. That's right: of the top five films at the 2015 US box office, four were sequels.

But at today's Oscar ceremony, we saw a completely different Hollywood. The Best Picture winner, *Spotlight*, is a drama that cost only US$20 million to make — right at the budget threshold of the Independent Spirit Awards, the prize for indie film. It has none of the lavish special effects of those other films, no outsized production budget, no flashy crowd-pleasing plot — it tells the story of a group of journalists. And its box office take was just US$59 million.

![The cast of Spotlight, image from Kevin Winter/Getty Images](/uploads/image/201602/04f3598c689f.jpg)

The film the Oscars deemed the best of 2015 was, at the box office, thoroughly unremarkable. The difference comes down to this: the people who drive the US box office are the young.

This shows up in the market statistics of the Motion Picture Association of America. From 2010 to 2014, the most frequent moviegoers in the US were aged 12 to 24, averaging more than six trips to the cinema a year. Next came the 25-to-39 group, at between 3.7 and 5 visits. These young people under 40 are the main force of the entire movie market: over those years they bought roughly 65% of all movie tickets and propelled several blockbusters to the top of the box-office charts.

But the Academy's establishment voters have little interest in what young people like. This is an old story. In 2012, the *Los Angeles Times* published an article whose data became Exhibit A in the case against the Oscars: that year the Academy had 5,765 voters, their median age was 62, and only 14% were under 50. These "old folks" have different tastes from young people, and they don't feel any particular need to accommodate the new, unripe standards.

Edward Jay Epstein, a veteran investigative reporter, also addressed this in his book *The Hollywood Economist*, published a few years earlier. Assessing the 2009 Best Picture nominees, he wrote: "What these films have in common is that they have little to do with the studios' real business. The Hollywood studios show films aimed at teenagers on 3,000 or more screens around the world."

But most Oscar-nominated and winning films harbor no ambition to win over the world's teenagers. Epstein described how these films look to Hollywood's upper crust — like Mozart in music, like avant-garde artists in oil painting.

If you dig into the question, you'll find that Hollywood today does something entirely different from what the Oscars honor. Our earlier Hollywood Report series put it this way: Hollywood's core may really have become that odious word: IP (intellectual property).

Take the most "admirable" of them all, Disney. Disney, that diversified, multi-brand "entertainment company," always calls its popular films "valuable assets." Wrapped around those assets is an industrial chain so long it leaves an indelible impression of their products: movies, TV, theme parks, merchandise, games, and every kind of cross-over collaboration.

That's right — a film is just one link in this chain. This is of course hugely beneficial to the studios, and the most important benefit is that it extends the product life cycle and adds revenue channels. With competition at the box office growing ever fiercer, everyone wants to do both of those things. In 2014, Disney's revenue was higher than every other entertainment company's, second only to the retailer Walmart and Apple — yet its filmed entertainment division (movies) accounted for just 14% of total revenue.

Following Disney's example, Hollywood's studios are all striving to become companies that manufacture and distribute IP. This is a rule of the game distinct from the traditional film industry, but it has long been deeply internalized in Hollywood; the only worry among those people is that their own pivot isn't fast enough.

The Oscars, however, keep this industrial chain at arm's length. What they uphold and honor is still the traditional mode of production. For the Oscars, the core is producing films. You can see this clearly from how the awards are divided: the Academy Awards, established in 1929, hand out honors for directors, actors, screenwriters, art directors, cinematographers, editors, composers, sound engineers, costume designers, makeup artists, and for films, animation, and documentaries — all these roles and categories of film revolve around the traditional film industry.

And starting in the 1990s, the Hollywood majors set up their independent-film divisions as separate companies, letting those divisions focus exclusively on making the "good movies" that might contend for Oscars, without any pressure to turn a profit.

To borrow a standard Chinese formulation: the Oscars are disconnected from the market and from demand.

For a considerable stretch of time, though, the Oscars — in the early film market — genuinely reflected the industry's most important shifts.

Start with the technical side of film. The earliest Oscars could be said to have witnessed cinema's evolution from silence to sound. Take the Best Picture winner at the second Academy Awards, *The Broadway Melody* — it was the first sound film to use contrapuntal sound-and-image technique. Beyond sound, refined production design and the artistic use of staging and lighting also often earned films good results at the Oscars. Films of that era were mostly fairly rough-hewn, and the Oscars set technical standards for cinema.

![A still from The Broadway Melody, image from Douban](/uploads/image/201602/c0723231888a.jpg)

In the early days, there was also often a positive correlation between the Oscars and box-office clout — films the establishment embraced rarely did poorly at the market. The 1935 Best Picture *It Happened One Night* and the 1944 Best Picture *Casablanca*, for example, earned US$2.5 million and US$3.7 million respectively in their release years — hefty figures for the time. Then there's the 1940 Best Picture *Gone with the Wind*, which has grossed more than US$390 million to date, making it one of the biggest hits in cinema history.

![A still from Gone with the Wind, image from Douban](/uploads/image/201602/187c83c45f2f.jpg)

Beyond ordinary moviegoers, those films were usually also recognized by the "elite" viewers who hold more sway over a film's reputation. *Casablanca*, just mentioned, holds a lofty 8.5 rating on IMDb, ranking No. 32; *Gone with the Wind* and *It Happened One Night* both sit at 8.1, at No. 153 and No. 164. *12 Angry Men*, ranked No. 7, and *It's a Wonderful Life*, ranked No. 25, also both received Oscar nominations in their day. Many of the Western films on IMDb's entire Top 250 list have some connection, more or less, to the Oscars. They were once the mainstream of the market; now they have become the "classics" in people's minds.

![A still from Casablanca, image from Douban](/uploads/image/201602/696d47c9292a.jpg)

But as time has moved to the present, apart from the annual awards for special effects, cinematography, and the like — where you can still see advances in technique and artistry — the once-virtuous relationship between the Oscars, the market, and popular reputation has largely faded. This has everything to do with how the Hollywood market has changed today, and the establishment seems to deliberately keep its distance from the market's evaluation system.

From the standpoint of protecting "good movies," the Oscars may be right to do so. If the films the establishment endorsed also became a string of box-office-unstoppable IPs, then... that really would sound rather sad.

But as market and reputation have drifted apart, "good movies" can no longer be so easily defined.

Judgments of "good movies" in the broad sense today perhaps still rest with the IMDb Top 250 list, or the Douban rankings, or the recommendations of certain film critics. In any case, the definition survives in the hands of the formerly mainstream TV-era audience. They preserve an understanding of the traditional film market and demands for the art of cinema, and they hold the power of discourse, telling everyone: only movies like these are good movies.

But the market seems to see it differently. What the market considers "good movies" are the ones young people endorse. Compared with the classic production approaches of the past, they are far more at home with entertainment-driven cinema. That entertainment-first attitude is now the mainstream of the market.

The "capital" behind it all — strict demands for return on investment and market performance — has even changed what the pinnacle of a "good movie" looks like compared with the past. Either kind of "good movie."

For stable box-office returns, studios today, in catering to young audiences, have chosen the direct and simple route: make sequels, adapt classic IPs, and pile on special effects. Look at the box-office champions of the past two years: *Star Wars 7*, *Furious 7*, *Jurassic World*, and more recently *Deadpool*. They all performed superbly at the market.

Powerful capital has brought even the indie film companies under the control of the majors. Big companies like Fox and Paramount have set up subsidiaries to invest in stylistically independent production, while major studios like Disney simply acquire successful indie companies to make films for them. Bear in mind that indie film companies have a pretty good record at producing Oscar winners. Take 1969's *Midnight Cowboy* — an adults-only film that won Best Picture. David Lynch's *The Elephant Man* and Damien Chazelle's *Whiplash* were both nominated for Best Picture as well.

What's more, in the business of producing films today, a globalized market must be taken into account. And the focus of that market might be China.

Speaking of China's film market, its demand at present is for technical showmanship — the crushing force of the big blockbuster, the piling up of technology. Films whose technique verges on the sublime, like *Avatar* and *Life of Pi*, have long since fallen off the IMDb Top 250 list, yet on Douban they still rank No. 93 and No. 27 respectively.

At the box office, three of China's top ten films of 2015 were Hollywood productions — *Furious 7*, *Avengers: Age of Ultron*, and *Jurassic World*, at No. 1, No. 5, and No. 7. And No. 2's *Monster Hunt*, No. 4's *Mojin: The Lost Legend*, No. 8's *Jian Bing Man*, and No. 9's *From Vegas to Macau II* were merely domestic films running the exact same Hollywood playbook. *Kung Fu Panda 3*, the huge recent hit that took 976 million yuan at the box office, is an animated film made directly for the Chinese market.

Now this market, China, claims it will surpass the US within a few years, and every Hollywood company is getting involved with it. The founding of companies like China Media Capital and Oriental DreamWorks, together with wave after wave of acquisitions, is slowly reshaping the world's film production and evaluation systems through the eyes of today's Chinese consumers.

But none of that seems able to sway the Oscars. They simply refuse to give awards to these winners of the market. Even though what now draws people to the Oscars is not so much the films as the side stories — Leonardo DiCaprio, the "20-year Oscar runner-up" who finally took home Best Actor, and "Big Cousin" Jennifer Lawrence, the closely watched actress. These conversations began colonizing our social networks months ago. Just now, Twitter's trending data showed the biggest news wasn't *Spotlight* at all — it was Leonardo finally getting his gold man. Second was Jennifer Lawrence not getting hers.

!["Big Cousin" Jennifer Lawrence at the Oscars, image from Twitter](/uploads/image/201602/de98c28c32ff.jpg)

Beyond that, even the seemingly well-founded criticisms can't change it — such as: the Oscars are too white. The Democratic Party and Black artists raise this criticism every year. Black director Spike Lee boycotted this year's ceremony to protest the situation, arguing that it wounds Black self-respect and ignores their power.

Yet after all these years the Oscars still haven't changed. They always stress that they are "apolitical." That claim isn't without reason. The Oscars don't really practice racial discrimination — though Black people do have a relatively weak presence, don't forget that Hollywood is a world built by Jews. In this world, 38% of the Best Directors have been Jewish.

Yes, the Oscars are simply a set of rules about film held in the hearts of a group of old people. They will not move with the market, nor heed criticisms unrelated to film. The rule of film here may be just one: to stay disconnected from the market, and to tell us from the perspective of "those who have been through it" something different from what the market says — that is the Oscars' greatest political correctness.

Still, things may change slightly in the future. On January 21, 2016, the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences unanimously approved a reform plan in a vote. The major reforms, to be completed by 2020, include: a more diverse voting membership, more active within the film industry, with faster turnover of personnel.
