We will present this as a series of reports: in China's "trading up" market, what has changed for brands and consumers, and what efforts they have made. This is an opening note.

Something that happened two months ago: once again, customers queued in a long line outside a store.

On December 12 last year, MUJI opened its largest flagship store in China on Huaihai Road, in Shanghai's busiest commercial district. At nine that morning, people were already lining up at the door. With customer traffic too heavy, MUJI imposed entry restrictions; around 10:30 the waiting line was nearly 100 meters long, and by 2 p.m. it had doubled. By 4 p.m. the number was still growing rather than shrinking, and each customer waited roughly an hour before getting into the store.

Total card transactions at the store on its first day came to about 7,000, with sales exceeding 2.15 million yuan — more than double the sales MUJI's China unit had projected.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124929d1an8gESFl7jyABo.jpg-w600)

![The long line outside MUJI's Huaihai Road flagship store on opening day; image from The Paper](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124930ovdzU8bSIEsijAXw.jpg-w600)

Su Jing, founder of the Japanese culture magazine 《知日》, heard the news in Beijing and was astonished. He knew full well that "because supply in the market is so abundant," MUJI has nowhere near this level of influence in Japan. But in China, consumer demand is growing faster than product suppliers imagined. MUJI has 134 stores in 39 cities in mainland China, and is still expanding at a pace of 30–35 new stores a year. By 2017, that number will rise to 50. The stores keep getting bigger, and the average location recovers its costs in just 15.9 months.

This is thanks to the emergence of the urban consumer.

The Atlantic, together with Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, released [a 2015 report, "The Rise of China's New Consumer Class"](http://www.theatlantic.com/sponsored/goldman-sachs-2015/the-rise-of-chinas-new-consumer-class/589/), whose conclusions likewise exceeded expectations: the number of middle-class consumers in China's cities has passed 100 million — about 146 million — with average annual income of US$11,733 per person. Together with another 236 million mass-market urban consumers, these 146 million middle-class consumers are "no longer spending money only on basic necessities."

Consulting firms spotted this new consumption mindset early on and named it "trading up." "Consumers place greater emphasis on their own needs and on expressing their personal style," [explained a report published by the Boston Consulting Group in 2006](https://www.bcg.com/documents/file15317.pdf). That report also highlighted differences among consumers in different regions: "Even though some brands are quite common, in a market like Russia or China they may still attract more attention than in other, more mature markets — and become an expression of trading up." In the Chinese market, MUJI is one such expression.

Even if consumers don't utter the phrase "trading up," and even if they would struggle to describe how their lives have moved up a notch, entrepreneurs and industry observers across China today have all noticed this.

"Everyone is talking about 'trading up,' in Beijing, in Shanghai. What is the core meaning of this thing?" said Wang Zhiwei. "I think it's really just being able to spend a bit of money to live a better life." Wang Zhiwei is the founder of the review media outlet "Penguin Eats & Drinks Guide"; last December its review of 300 bags of potato chips racked up more than a million reads on WeChat.

![300 bags of potato chips](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124931Ds36qMzPxmCpwI0X.jpg-w600)

These reviews are detailed, discerning, and expensive to produce. If you followed the guide's recommendations, you could easily end up spending 100 yuan on a bag of potato chips, driving a dozen-plus kilometers to a ramen shop for a bowl of noodles costing over 100 yuan, and habitually drinking craft beer, direct-from-the-vineyard wines, and coffee better than Starbucks.

Consultants Michael Silverstein and Neil Fiske wrote in [an article for Harvard Business Review, "Luxury for the Masses"](https://hbr.org/2003/04/luxury-for-the-masses), that when a new "luxury brand" takes root, it can quickly rewrite the rules of its category and achieve market leadership, as Starbucks did with coffee. Over the past 16 years, Starbucks has opened more than 1,900 stores in China; in 2015 alone it added more than 400 new stores. Most recently, it said it plans to grow its China store count to 4,400 over the next five years.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124931h9BfYFLDH6C5xElX.jpg-w600)

But this is likely a dangerous move. "A new competitor always makes yesterday's new-luxury winner look dated," Michael Silverstein and Neil Fiske added. A competitor can appear at any moment — and in particular, once better coffee comes along, Starbucks will quickly become the basic option.

Xu Ke, co-founder of the consumer decision media outlet "Qingdan," realized this Spring Festival that even a humble pack of instant noodles can show "trading up" in action. At a small shop in rural Hebei, Xu Ke found that most of what was on sale was "Baixiang" bagged instant noodles at 3.5 yuan a pack; but a few days later, at a Lawson in Osaka, he bought bagged instant noodles at 105 yen a pack — about 6 yuan.

"Those two little shops drew an upper and a lower line. The lives and consumption of the vast majority of urban Chinese residents around us fall between those two lines," Xu Ke said. "And so-called 'trading up' is the process of starting near one line and gradually moving closer to the other."

For urban consumers, reaching "the other line" may still take time, but they do have far more channels now to learn what that other line looks like — without even having to make a special trip abroad. 《好物 100》 (*100 Good Things*), a book on the aesthetics of living co-written by Japanese bestselling author Yataro Matsuura and stylist Masako Ito, was published in China last May to wide acclaim. Chen Ken, the book's publisher, believes Matsuura is talking about "quintessentially middle-class things," and that these ideas are arriving in China at exactly the right moment — because "it's all about imagination and aesthetics... and aesthetics is indeed something you spend money on."

Fortunately "the middle class has now taken shape," and its members are learning to combine consumption (spending money) with a philosophy of life (imagination and aesthetics), the way Matsuura does — setting them apart from the moneyed crowd of old that "worshipped famous brands." Purui Culture, the publishing house Chen Ken founded in 2011, also brought Matsuura's other lifestyle book, 《100 个基本》 (*100 Basics*), to China four years ago. That book — which espouses life precepts like "don't accept things for free" — is reprinted every year and has sold nearly 100,000 copies, doing especially well in airport bookstores, those key places where businesspeople pick up new ideas.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124932LVqM5pR1c3wysBbI.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124932BAHnO8hwUmjVz1s4.jpg-w600)

Linghu Lei, creative director of the monthly magazine 《生活》, likes to recommend this Japanese lifestyle writer to people. Matsuura is so appealing, he said, because he acts like a mentor in life and in how to consume. He always takes young people to better restaurants and earnestly lets them know that he himself uses these things (no matter how long he's actually used them), having "walked away from brand preaching."

People resist such preaching mostly because, as their needs have risen from before, they lack confidence in picking the right products. "All the fresh produce is delivered straight from the source, all the wines are group-imported by someone else, every restaurant is full of artisanal dedication" — but very likely, "they don't know what their money can buy." Wang Zhiwei said that apart from a small circle of hardcore enthusiasts, mass urban consumers need friendlier ways to learn all this.

When it comes to content production, Monocle is a model. Its guidance to the world of consumption comes in a friendly, vivid form — warmer than Wallpaper, the fine-things aggregator Tyler Brûlé founded earlier. "Right, I think it's something beyond the elite, because what he chooses isn't necessarily expensive, but it's always fitting. He believes this stuff is right for society as a whole, for the entire planet, for all urban people — the design is apt," Linghu Lei said. "And he's like someone who built a party, a church: you believe in these things, and they can change how you see the world. There's a whole system — a VI system and a creative system."

That consumer change is always systemic is pretty much beyond dispute. Anna Lappé is an advocate of organic food; in the organic cause she may not count as a success, but you've probably heard her line: "Every time you spend money, you're casting a vote for the kind of world you want." Most entrepreneurs have embraced this logic; they believe changes in lifestyle will show up first and most urgently in consumption. "If I tell you about a lifestyle you can adopt, the best way to spread it is through goods — through things that can be copied and standardized." Based on this understanding, Gong Yingqi and Xu Ke co-founded "Qingdan" to provide consumers with guides to daily goods.

Another facet of "trading up" lies in Qingdan's 600,000 users, 78% of whom are urban women aged 25–35. Gong Yingqi believes urban women want to buy products efficiently, do less scouting and comparison, and have greater need of professional consumer advice. Su Jing and Chen Ken corroborated this: women make up the majority of readers of 《知日》 and of Matsuura's books, and they are more interested in learning about another way of life.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124932TgAHhi1kqF39YXdL.jpg-w600)

![《知日·Zakka》](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124933x1e95DbQ30pmMiG7.jpg-w600)

Female urban consumers are almost certain to be the ones brands court in this round of trading up — just as in the past they propelled the rise of high-end department stores in postwar America with astonishing force.

In the Chinese market, "in just the last three to five years," Su Jing feels that strong demand will also drive new brands to grow big remarkably fast. Feng Su, founder of the home-sharing community "Haohaozhu," firmly believes the same. This January, introduced by an investor, the two discussed where the market is heading and excitedly reached the same conclusion: the arrival of new things "will be faster than people think." On another question their views also aligned: in the future consumers will have ever more choices, and even within the same market segment, a dozen or even dozens of options can coexist.

"Users all have aspirations for brands, but because brands are so scattered, we don't see most users chasing a single brand; it's more like every brand has its own fan base behind it," Feng Su said in an interview last November. These users have already passed the stage of getting basic knowledge about buying and styling a home from IKEA.

Even as economic growth slows, brands prefer to believe that China's urban consumers are maturing. Naoyuki Yamamoto, MUJI's general manager for China, said in a January interview that MUJI has found Chinese consumers have changed: "Overall, the number of customers who 'cherish every single item they buy' keeps growing, and the new generation of customers is more cautious and weighs more factors."

And indeed, [the Boston Consulting Group's observations found](https://www.bcg.com/documents/file15317.pdf) that "trading up" always comes with "trading down": consumers will rethink how they allocate their income, spending more on the product categories they care about most while squeezing spending on others. "Consumers in emerging markets are beginning to use their growing pool of their own money to choose better goods or services, while in some categories paying ever closer attention to getting more for less."

Yet these cautious consumers still looked less than rational when MUJI's new store opened. In the year before the Huaihai Road flagship opened, MUJI had already opened what was then its largest global flagship, at Taikoo Li in Chengdu. Customers wanting a taste of the MUJI Cafe & Meal restaurant — introduced to China for the first time — filled an entire flight of stairs with their queue.

Scenes of demand outstripping supply keep happening, which means more upgraded products need to appear. Su Jing thinks "20 to 30 more brands like MUJI will grow big in China," which may solve the problem. Meanwhile, more people like Su Jing, Feng Su, Gong Yingqi, Xu Ke, Linghu Lei and Wang Zhiwei have appeared; they are doing something similar to what Tyler Brûlé and Yataro Matsuura do: in China's "trading up" market, serving as mentors.

[The business guidance consulting firms have offered on this](http://www.bcg.com.cn/export/sites/default/en/files/publications/articles_pdf/Trading_Up_New_Luxury_Oct2003.pdf) is equally blunt: "Why should (consumers) bother with something that offers neither a price advantage nor a functional or emotional benefit? Companies that make such products are on the verge of death."
