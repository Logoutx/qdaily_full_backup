“Rebelliousness” makes us ask: in this new era, what exactly are “success,” “fun,” and “beauty”?

If you had to sum young people up in a single word, that word would probably be “rebellious.”

Which generation of young people wasn’t rebellious? Many people tend to exaggerate the distinctive traits of the generation right in front of them, forgetting that every year brings a fresh crop of 22-year-olds — and that they themselves weren’t exactly models of calm at that age either. Look back at the trend cultures that have since become memes, and you’ll find the footprints of every generation’s youth: anti-war, anti-dictatorship, anti-violence, smashing guitars on stage, aimlessly cruising the highways, sticking flowers in rifle barrels, or writing long poems to vent a whole heap of “I don’t believe it.”

This cohort’s distinguishing trait is a knack for maneuvering cleverly within consumerism. After all, they were born into it and raised in it.

Sometimes this maneuvering is also called a “life attitude.” Factor in relationships — virtual and real, between people and between people and organizations — and it might also be called one’s “values.”

You can see this in the multiple surveys published by QDaily Research Lab — [“Which merchant gimmicks designed to engage you actually leave you cold?”](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2603.html) [“Which of businesses’ attempts to ‘talk to young people’ make you laugh the moment you see them?”](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2476.html) [“Which parts of the ‘looks economy’ haven’t really won you over?”](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2473.html) — and others. From products whose only selling point is being the “same style as the celebrity,” to WeChat-business customer-harvesting tactics like “share-to-enter lotteries,” to the “[life manifestos](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2303.html)” in advertisements (which, rhyme aside, don’t sum up anyone’s life), sharp-witted young people with finely tuned radar have long since trained piercing eyes amid consumerism’s jungle of tricks — and can only say “no, thank you” to all of them.

On a broader level, the definition of “beauty” in contemporary life has quietly shifted with each wave of rebellious thinking: the assembly-line [refined influencer face is no longer the aesthetic high ground](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/14968.html). Young people in China voted for Wang Ju in idol survival shows, liked the Instagram posts of “alternative influencer” Naomi Watanabe, and began following evergreen actresses like Isabelle Huppert; this year The Guardian even [reported](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2018/oct/26/escape-the-corset-south-korean-women-rebel-against-strict-beauty-standards) on a movement of South Korean women resisting “mainstream beauty” by smashing their cosmetics… Someone joked that “when we picked Wang Ju, what we were really picking was ourselves” — and in a sense that’s true: without a rebellious heart that refuses to follow the beaten path, there is no subverting so-called traditional aesthetics.

Take the recent vogue for “earthy” style, for instance — it carries more than a hint of rebellion. Consider food videos alone: cooking tutorials are everywhere, so why did Huanong Brothers’ [“100 reasons to eat bamboo rats”](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/56257.html) and teacher Wang Gang’s plain-spoken “a generous pour of oil will scare you off” go viral this year? “Looks delicious” is surely part of the reason, but “down-to-earth and genuinely hardcore” is a factor you can’t ignore: before this, most online food tutorials went the refined route — all about “composition, a soundtrack, artful plating, and a cat” — and the point seemed less to teach people how to cook than to transmit a value system of “this is what life is supposed to look like.” Earthy food content is different: it puts on no airs and gives people a genuine sense of warmth and everyday bustle. For young people already tired of being repeatedly nudged with “you should live the way I do,” loving the “earthy” is naturally a small rebellion from refinement toward [charming roughness](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/17464.html).

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109015741RB84moGkPfyeO3sC.jpg-w600)

The rebellious spirit of contemporary people also shows in the stubbornness of [“the hotter it gets, the less I want to pay attention”](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2403.html). “Everyone likes it so I like it too” proves nothing about being a firework of a different color; “everyone raves about it but I hold my ground and refuse the pitch” — that is the new nonconformity. Woody Allen said, “Everyone fell in love with Snow White, but I took one look at the wicked queen and was smitten.” Contemporary young people’s self-discipline in refusing to follow the crowd in their tastes is arguably no weaker than Woody Allen’s: you’re chasing hot topics and gossip; I’m blocking entertainment news. You play Honor of Kings; I’m still feeding my frog son. You watch hit survival shows; I like the refreshingly un-hyped programs. You’re hooked on livestreaming; I simply won’t download Kuaishou or Douyin. This instinctive aversion to the so-called “national craze” is a major vehicle of the rebellious spirit.

There is no question that “rebellion” today is no longer a set of individually enumerable “behaviors” but a continuous, consistent life attitude. Having survived our tribulation — the traditional style of upbringing built around “you must amount to something” and “look at other people’s kids” — and made our way into society, we who are now economically self-sufficient and spiritually independent have finally earned the right to voice disagreement, and with it the chance to vote with our feet: “As long as I don’t want to, no one can occupy my mind and my attention — let alone take my money.” From this angle, what true rebellion wants to express is essentially a personal, mini Declaration of Independence; heresy for its own sake is not required.

But rebellion is never pure rebellion; sometimes it takes another form: self-deprecating labels like “wage slave” or “salted fish,” or passive attitudes like being “demotivational” or “Buddha-like.” In my institute’s poll [“Why do you love mocking yourself with words like ‘Buddha-like / good-for-nothing / old fogey’?”](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2414.html), the top answer was “These words genuinely describe me — at least some of them hit my inner anxiety or helplessness.” From this you can see that the emotions which look less “obedient” and less relentlessly positive are partly a contrarian, “playing devil’s advocate” psychology, and partly a mix of anxiety about life’s pressures and traditional values, plus a self-soothing wish to “not simply submit to that anxiety.”

### What does contemporary rebellion actually look like?

Based on QDaily Research Lab’s observations, here are 11 expressions of the contemporary rebellious spirit:

**1. Lifestyle rebellion: Smoking and drinking are cool? Not smoking and not drinking is the real individuality**

Out of novelty, in our teens we might deliberately mimic adults, feeling there was a flamboyant abandon to smoking, drinking, and perming our hair. But once you grow up, you realize none of it is a big deal. Surveys of European and American teenagers have reached [similar conclusions](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/57190.html): they no longer think smoking and drinking are particularly cool. For adults, alcohol and cigarettes often mean uncomfortable unwritten rules of socializing — the culture of boozy dinner parties, the workplace vogue of “smoking socials,” and so on. Refusing them means not just refusing the acts of pressuring someone to drink or offering a cigarette, but having the courage to “refuse to maintain social relationships through such acts.” Compared with the chuunibyou-style teenage obsession with alcohol and cigarettes, this kind of “grown-up rebellion” is plainly a more refined breed.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109015826pBR0lyHkhNT2VSoz.jpg-w600)

**2. [Information detox](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2579.html) rebellion: I ignore whatever people say**

Faced with the pervasive internet, contemporary youth cannot fully cut themselves off — but that doesn’t mean they’ve given up autonomy over what they take in: not opening the Moments entry, not clearing the little red notification dots, turning off message push the moment they join a group chat, switching off the danmaku before watching a video, blocking chat while gaming, shutting down DingTalk after work… In a world exploding with information, keeping a quiet personal space for yourself delivers the mischievous glee of sneaking a forbidden treat as a kid.

**3. Ritual rebellion: the simpler the [wedding](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/33854.html), the better — or skip it entirely**

Sometimes rebellion shows up as indifference to ritual. In my institute’s survey [“Test how rebellious you are about ‘ritual feeling’”](http://www.qdaily.com/tots/2445.html), more than half of respondents chose “I want the wedding kept simple, or not held at all.” And it’s not just weddings: young people have largely been disenchanted with shopping ads waving the banner of “treat yourself,” advertorials insisting “you owe yourself at least one luxury-brand piece in your lifetime,” and the habit of “photographing your food for a consecration before you can pick up the chopsticks.” All that hollow pretension has lost its magic. Frankly, ritual itself isn’t off the table — provided the form never outweighs the meaning.

**4. Algorithm rebellion: even when NetEase Cloud Music recommends a song I love, I refuse to favorite it — lest it think it understands me and push its luck**

In the big-data era, the algorithm is always [“guessing what you like”](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52158.html). If you don’t want to be pitched or marketed to, fighting the algorithm calls for a bit of rebellious scheming: deny it access to your contacts — no thanks, “people you may know”; disable cookies in the browser and clear browsing data regularly; shuffle your music and alternate tapping “like” and “not interested” to emphasize your unfathomable taste; if you wander into clickbait on a news app, quickly click several topics you genuinely like to drag the recommendation style back on course.

**5. Like rebellion: when can we get an “I don’t like” button?**

A Facebook [survey](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/IJyUN3-ctAdJ5RB6mpbubA) showed that more than 3 million users wanted a “dislike” button added — a wish probably shared by many: see entertainment news you don’t care about? Click. Toxic chicken soup from Ayawawa or Mi Meng shows up? Click. The same person posting the same-expression selfie for an entire week? Click. Run into a bad-faith comment from a contrarian troll? Click. Compared with the like — which long ago stopped representing “like” — an “I don’t like” would more sincerely express the resolve of “no thanks, downvote.”

**6. Rules rebellion: “knowing the rules” — knowing which rules, exactly?**

The [“being sensible” and “knowing the rules”](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2566.html) championed by traditional childrearing naturally cannot escape young people’s questioning today: when parents physically punish a child, does the child’s silence count as “being sensible”? Telling elders exactly what they want to hear and voicing no opinion of your own — is that “being sensible”? Everyone “voluntarily works overtime,” but I leave the moment my shift ends — does that make me someone who “doesn’t know the rules”? Turning thirty without marrying or having kids — is that also “not knowing the rules”? Rebellion questions not only the rules themselves, but also the inaction of accepting them unconditionally on the grounds that “we all went through it this way.”

**7. [Persona](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/55044.html) rebellion: a taste for “anti-formula” characters who don’t play by the book**

After watching one too many aggrieved, long-suffering “white lotus” heroines, this year everyone instead fell for *Story of Yanxi Palace*, whose female lead has a temper and is not to be trifled with. The stars’ model-student, harmless personas no longer win everywhere with one trick, either; public figures who are “straight-shooting” and “genuine” have instead won young people’s hearts. In young people’s eyes, a persona has to be laboriously “worked at,” but the essence of rebellion is being “truly yourself” — realness matters more than perfection. Rather than exhausting yourself with pretense, you might as well let yourself fly.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181109103403vGHeEh0SX3kWYnDT.jpg-w600)

**8. Viewing rebellion: official pairings, step aside — I’ll ship my own**

The most “internet-native” rebellion of our time is the viewing kind — “won’t follow the official plot, love second-creation instead”: in 2017, fan-edited CP videos on Bilibili repeatedly became topics across the whole web, the most representative being the [mashup CP](https://www.bilibili.com/read/cv115700/) of “Voldemort × Lin Daiyu.” Bilibili already has hundreds of quality fan edits related to “Voldemort–Daiyu,” with combined views in the millions. Beyond that, viewers bored stiff with domestic dramas have developed habitual flights of fancy aimed at hackneyed plots: villains who are ruthless and tight-lipped, protagonists who die the moment they act up, women disguised as men spotted at a glance, villains who should attack from front and back when the hero is outnumbered, and 《淑女的品格》 (*The Qualities of a Lady*), in which none of the female leads ever marry… All in all: no scenes from the official version? We’ll add our own. No material from the official version? We’ll produce our own. If that isn’t “rebellion is productivity.”

**9. Ambition rebellion: isn’t life just up, down, down, down, down?**

The rise and spread of “demotivational culture” in China is really a protest against the single, crude homegrown successology of the [“winners in life”](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2444.html). Nobody likes a life hijacked by ambition, especially once you realize the road to the peak of life looks like a carpet of flowers but is pitted with traps everywhere. Rebellious youth use toxic chicken soup to yank themselves back to reality in a single second, showing instead a touch of “demotivational fire” — the spirit of “seeing life’s true face and still loving it”: “If life deceives you, don’t grieve, don’t be impatient — tomorrow it will keep right on deceiving you.” “Sometimes you feel like an idiot. Don’t lose heart — at least your feeling is correct.” “Isn’t life just up, down, down, down, down…?” “If you never slack off, how will you ever find out you can do even worse?”

**10. Values rebellion: dragging “values” into everything — aren’t you just a values zealot?**

Aversion to the demand that “everything must conform to proper values” is also an indirect form of values rebellion. In the [“annoying values tribunals”](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2560.html) and the open-ended survey [“your least favorite things in reading comments”](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2544.html), the second-ranked answers in both happened to be complaints about the abuse of “positive energy”: the criterion for judging a film or a book is no longer the content itself, but whether the author or the work has the “right values.” To this kind of moral-exemplar trial in which “values trump everything,” all we can say is that the “right values” claimed by self-styled champions of justice carry a subtext of nothing more than “their values just happen to match mine.”

**11. Halo rebellion: can we take off the “selfless devotion” curse a little sooner?**

A mother must be “strong now that she’s a mother,” a teacher is inevitably a “gardener and red candle,” an athlete can only “win glory for the country”… Under such an [identity halo](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2585.html), the professionalism of judging things on their merits and simply doing one’s own job well has instead become a kind of so-called “rebellion.” To borrow the words of reader @Te: any occupation is, in essence, just a job; forcibly binding workers with spiritual-level praise reflects nothing but a long-standing shortfall in material compensation. Rebellious young people need a spirit of contract, not sentimentality.
