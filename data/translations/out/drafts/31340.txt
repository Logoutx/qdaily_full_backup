It has moved from the circles of the well-off middle class into smaller, even wealthier circles of the rich.

The medal ceremony for the men's stroke play at the Rio Olympics had barely ended when Joe Posnanski, a columnist for NBC Sports, published [an open letter](http://www.nbcolympics.com/news/posnanski-open-letter-golfers-who-passed-rio-olympics-you-blew-it), calling out the top players who had withdrawn.

The letter began like this—

> Subject: To the golfers who missed the Rio Olympics—you blew it
> 
> 
> To: Jordan Spieth
> Cc: Jason Day; Rory McIlroy; Dustin Johnson; Adam Scott; Hideki Matsuyama
> Bcc: Vijay Singh
> Title: Rio!
> 
> August 15, 2016

These unfamiliar names are almost all golfers ranked in the world's top 20, yet every one of them refused to play in these Olympics. Because [23 golfers](http://sports.sina.com.cn/golf/pgatour/2016-06-29/doc-ifxtmweh2744906.shtml) withdrew one after another before the Rio Games began, this year's gold medalist, Britain's Justin Rose, was only the world No. 15.

Incidentally, golf's return to the Olympics this year came a full 112 years after the last time.

"They don't want to promote golf, and they don't realize how lucky they are," [an angry Gary Player told reporters.](http://www.golf.com/tour-and-news/gary-player-disappointed-golfers-withdrawing-2016-olympics) "They don't know what the Olympics means for a sport." Gary Player, now 81, is considered one of the greatest golfers of all time.

The bad news about golf has been piling up for a while. Most of the withdrawing players cited security concerns in Rio and the Zika virus, but the media generally agreed the real reason was that the Olympic tournament offered no prize money, and its schedule clashed with the packed calendar of professional majors and tour events. Ironically, among the top female players—who supposedly faced higher infection rates—only one withdrew.

And the real decline happened to begin in 2009. That year, [golf was voted back into the Olympics](http://news.bbc.co.uk/sport2/hi/olympic_games/8292584.stm) by the IOC, but Tiger Woods, who had lent his backing to the bid, promptly slid from the peak of his career. Since then, golf seems never to have produced another player who combined every kind of storyline and trophy in a single figure.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160823151345jEt0BoTzIlkgRL6e.jpg-w600)

The big companies are also heading for the exit. On May 4, 2015, [the Adidas Group announced it would sell its golf business](http://www.adidas-group.com/en/media/news-archive/press-releases/2016/adidas-group-concludes-strategic-review-golf-business/), including TaylorMade, the world's largest golf-equipment maker; on August 4 this year, [Nike abruptly announced](http://news.nike.com/news/nike-focuses-golf-innovation-on-footwear-and-apparel-transitions-out-of-equipment) it would leave the golf-equipment market, halting production of golf balls, clubs, and bags while keeping only footwear and apparel.

Matt Owens, the owner of a golf course in the United States, took in just [$200](http://www.economist.com/news/christmas-specials/21636688-though-thriving-parts-asia-golf-struggling-america-and-much-europe) in green fees on one weekend afternoon in 2014 (the "green," or putting green, is the patch of grass where the hole sits—the Chinese word for it is a phonetic transliteration of "green"), and he had been running that course for 12 years.

At the time, [Greg Nathan, senior vice president of the NGF (National Golf Foundation), said](http://chronicle.augusta.com/sports/golf/2014-04-05/youth-competition-reflects-efforts-grow-game-golf): "Golf as a business is under tremendous pressure. The boom from the mid-1980s through 2005 created an imbalance of supply and demand. Golf properties are hard to sell, and competition has grown fiercer."

Play had fallen off after the economic crisis of 2008, and the same thing was happening across the United States in the years that followed.

Later, Owens's course became a park.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016082315081624TSbAvBymsqh3Pn.jpg-w600)

### Golf Is Boring, But Rich People Love It

Golf is a slow, loose, and endless game. Yet precisely because of these qualities, it is especially suited to doing business or hashing out a problem. In 2011, when U.S. President Barack Obama and House Speaker John Boehner tried to find common ground, they chose 18 holes.

This kind of "impromptu meeting room" suits the upper crust so perfectly that membership in golf clubs is often given as a gift, tantamount to social status and connections. In turn, these people have set a high threshold for the sport—building a standard 18-hole golf course takes anywhere from a hundred qing of land at the low end to tens of thousands at the high end; a basic set of golf clubs costs at least a thousand-plus yuan, and that doesn't even count the green fee for every round.

Golf began among the Scottish aristocracy in the late 19th century, but—as you might expect—it captured its first enthusiasts in America. The United States is the biggest market for the little white ball: golf-equipment sales account for 41% of the global total, and nearly 30,000 courses are spread across the country, roughly 75% of them public. Whether the sport rises or falls can largely be read from golf's fortunes in America.

On February 6, 1971, Alan Shepard, commander of the Apollo 14 moon mission, sent the little white ball 200 meters with a 6-iron, leaving behind the famous line: "Miles and miles and miles." Via the television signal, everyone remembered the "lunar swing."

![Shepard's famous lunar swing | Source: PGA](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160823143715Fr72KvEJBtLzVosc.jpg-w600)

In fact, in that same era, television broadcasts, marketing, and books also made another little yellow ball hot: tennis. Yet the thousands of courts built in a frenzy turned from money machines into redundant empty lots. It took many years before tennis, as a sport, returned to a normal balance of supply and demand. Had a golf superstar not burst onto the scene, golf's fate would likely have been much the same as tennis's. Luckily for golf, America had Tiger Woods.

### Tiger Woods, Tiger Woods, Tiger Woods

Because of Tiger Woods, golf's run of attention was extended by roughly 10 years.

Tiger Woods was born in Los Angeles into a mixed-race family. His father was an African American and a Vietnam veteran; his mother was of Thai, Chinese, and Dutch descent. Woods calls himself "Cablinasian" (a shorthand for a blend of Caucasian, Black, Asian, and African American). To most white Americans, his background was practically synonymous with the word "mystery."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016082314381829FcKGIAzop3aCsQ.jpg-w600)

Like the football star O.J. Simpson, who had already become famous the year Woods was born, Woods was a handsome African American with astonishing athletic gifts. In 1997, fresh on the scene, he won the Masters by a staggering 12 strokes, and over the following year he captured 8 PGA titles. By then he had already signed a $40 million contract with Nike two years earlier.

It was also around that time that Nike decided to start making golf equipment. Woods happened to switch to Nike's new ball in the millennium year and produced a dominant run sweeping the year's three majors. In 1999, right on Nike's heels, Adidas acquired TaylorMade, the top golf-equipment maker in the U.S. market, charging into this "sport with promise."

They did all this entirely because Woods's results were so dazzling—single-handedly, he won the sport unprecedented attention and commercial opportunity. In [a 1996 Nike ad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tAnlcW_ILyw) made for Woods, you can see Black, yellow, and white children—some confident, some green, some lost, some carrying a maturity beyond their years—repeating again and again before the camera: "I am Tiger Woods."

Through this marketing and media adulation, Woods became more than a golfer—he turned into an icon for America's ethnic minorities. [The Chicago Sun-Times put it this way](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2010/may/29/tiger-woods-racial-politics): "Woods represents the best part of the American dream... Woods is the embodiment of our melting-pot culture, a symbol of the pluralistic society we aspire to, and deserves to be called—an American."

Tiger Woods thrust golf into the center of culture and public debate, and social mores did change. In 2010, three former Goldman Sachs employees sued the investment bank over its "[golf-centric, male-dominated trading-department culture](http://www.nytimes.com/2010/09/16/business/16bias.html)." The three women all held senior posts at Goldman—vice chairman, managing director, and partner. One of them, Ms. Orlich, said that although she had been a golfer in college, she was never invited to the company's frequent golf outings.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160823143935pJfaL2Z8g390mWzM.jpg-w600)

After Tiger Woods turned pro, he dragged the number of Americans playing golf from 24 million up to a peak of 31 million. But in 2009 the turning point came—Woods hit an image crisis over a sex scandal, and sponsors began, subtly or openly, pulling down posters featuring him and abandoning the beverage lines named after him. The consulting firm Accenture was blunt: it announced it was terminating Woods's contract and cutting all ties. This directly caused his endorsement income to drop by more than $20 million the following year.

By one estimate, the entire golf industry lost [$590 million](http://www.chinavalue.net/Finance/Blog/2013-3-23/963768.aspx) because Woods sat out a year, and Nike lost [more than 100,000 customers](http://www.chinavalue.net/Finance/Blog/2013-3-23/963768.aspx) over the scandal.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608231439465r3olCf0J4SwnYZM.jpg-w600)

### Is Golf's Decline Also Because of Tiger Woods?

Later events proved that people only wanted to watch Tiger Woods, not to become him.

The "rookies" drawn to the course by Woods's play never actually stuck with it. Between 2000 and 2012, the number of occasional golfers in the U.S. grew by [2.5 million](http://www.golfwrx.com/219815/barney-adams-is-golf-participation-really-dropping/), while the number playing eight or more rounds a year fell by [9.8 million](http://www.golfwrx.com/219815/barney-adams-is-golf-participation-really-dropping/).

Yet the bubble golf created shrank not one bit. Steve Skinner works at Kemper Sports, a large golf-course management company, and [he believes](http://www.economist.com/news/christmas-specials/21636688-though-thriving-parts-asia-golf-struggling-america-and-much-europe) it will take at least another decade to work off the oversupply of golf courses.

[A golf-course designer now working mainly in the Chinese market, Brian Curley, put it this way](http://www.economist.com/news/christmas-specials/21636688-though-thriving-parts-asia-golf-struggling-america-and-much-europe): "If golf-course designers were a publicly traded stock, they'd be the cheapest penny stocks right now."

China, though, is a very different story—here everything seems to be retracing America's path of the 1970s.

In 2005, Dan Washburn, an American journalist at ESPN, came here for the first time. He spent nine years writing a book titled 《Golf and the Chinese Dream: The Forbidden Economy of the "Green Opium"》—for although China's social climate tied golf to capitalism and corruption, making it a "dangerous import that breeds decadence," the country's fast-growing economy did power the sport's takeoff: between 2005 and 2010, the number of golf courses in China tripled.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608231443466DhOGovgHpAS75r3.jpg-w600)

The golfer the book dwells on, Zhou Xunshu, was not rich to begin with. He had worked as a security guard at a golf course in Guangzhou, dealing every day with the wealthy class he had never before been able to reach. Among the Chinese who picked up the club in the same era were farmers, sushi chefs, stunt motorcyclists... Washburn tried to use their stories to prove that a cohort of Chinese, riding the confidence brought by the economic leap, changed their lives by becoming golfers.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160823141024MrSwl2DGkYIn4OZ9.jpg-w600)

Meanwhile, in most mature markets—Australia, Japan, England, Ireland—[golf is struggling](http://www.economist.com/news/christmas-specials/21636688-though-thriving-parts-asia-golf-struggling-america-and-much-europe). In England, the number of people playing at least once a month has fallen by more than a quarter since 2007; in Australia, club membership is down a fifth from its 1998 peak; in Japan, golf participation has dropped more than 40% from its early-1990s high. A few places, to be sure, are doing fine: the Czech golf market is growing, and so is Germany's. But these markets are too small compared with the size of the United States.

There are, of course, all kinds of intertwined reasons. The 2008 financial crisis, for one, undoubtedly delivered a heavy blow to an industry that is a near-luxury good. But just like the collapse of the U.S. housing market, the industry itself may have contained a bubble that Woods had been holding up since the 1990s. Now the bubble has burst; perhaps everything is simply returning to normal.

More importantly, compared with their predecessors who doted on the little white ball, today's entrepreneurs and founders really don't have that much free time—and the new kinds of business create no comparable need that forces you to spend four hours on a round just to get to know a partner. Even if such a need existed, the business partner you're looking for most likely wouldn't be found by the greens at Augusta.

"The anxiety for a startup is whether it can land even a single paying customer," [said Chris Heivly, co-founder of the startup MapQuest](http://www.inc.com/chris-heivly/why-golf-will-never-be-a-business-tool-for-startups.html). "Startups focus on building a product or service of value; sometimes they don't even need to put effort into marketing." For them, doing business over golf is too inefficient.

The changing of the times contradicts golf at every turn. The contemporary husband can no longer, as men once did, dump the kids on his wife and vanish with a few golf buddies into the country club on a Saturday.

Still, the "rich man's sport" label has only grown more vivid. When the U.S. government, amid budget disputes, began shutting down state-run public golf courses, the unaffected rich flared with fresh passion for the little white ball—now nearly half of American golfers earn salaries above $100,000, a share up 40% from 2005. No wonder Donald Trump called for [making golf "elitist"](http://www.golf.com/tour-and-news/donald-trump-let-golf-be-elitist): the 18 top-tier golf courses under his name now once again have value as something for the American people to strive toward.

![In China, property prices around golf courses run more than 17% higher than those of ordinary housing developments.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608231511061EWqTYuGVbPIHS0m.png-w600)

### Where Have All the Young People Gone

A young person's basic requirements for a sporting event probably go like this: fierce head-to-head competition, a short and fast-paced format—even good-looking jerseys and shoes. The popularity of retro tennis shoes like Stan Smith among the young proves just how important good-looking apparel is to a sport.

They'd rather play golf at home on an Xbox, because sinking a beautiful putt on a real course is far harder than doing it in a virtual world.

The rise of every kind of entertainment has made the split between couch potatoes and sports lovers ever starker. There are [80 million](http://www.economist.com/news/christmas-specials/21636688-though-thriving-parts-asia-golf-struggling-america-and-much-europe) Americans who take part in no sport at all, but those willing to get off the couch tend to favor the more intense options: triathlons, kite surfing—far better stress relief than golf.

By any mass-market measure, young people are drifting further and further from golf.

Jordan Spieth, now the world's most valuable golfer, has just turned 23. The average age of active professional golfers is [35](http://rickyleepotts.com/2013/03/average-age-of-a-pga-tour-player.html), and the average age of American golf enthusiasts is 54. In 2013, 200,000 players under 35 abandoned the sport.

"Winning back—or keeping—golfers under 45 is harder now than it has ever been," [The Economist wrote](http://www.economist.com/news/christmas-specials/21636688-though-thriving-parts-asia-golf-struggling-america-and-much-europe).

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160823151012DotJKzbqF59ZT3GY.jpg-w600)

Jordan Spieth reached world No. 1 during the 2015 season and now sits third, but his $53 million in personal income owes not only to outstanding play—it also reflects how scarce charismatic young golfers are on the market. His endorsement deals include Coca-Cola, Under Armour, the telecom giant AT&T, the private-jet company NetJets, Rolex watches... According to the Forbes rankings, Spieth's endorsement income is 1.6 times his prize money, and he was the only golfer that year whose total earnings surpassed Tiger Woods's.

At the final deadline for the Rio Olympics roster on July 12, Jordan Spieth, while announcing "[the toughest decision to withdraw](http://olympics.nbcsports.com/2016/07/12/jordan-spieth-olympics-golf-zika-virus-rio/)," pledged to make the 2020 Tokyo Olympics his goal—if he still had the chance to represent the United States.

That promise, like the future of the sport of golf itself, looks too faint to hold.
