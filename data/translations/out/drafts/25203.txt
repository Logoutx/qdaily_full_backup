ASOS Exits China: Why Do Fashion E-Tailers Always Have Such a Hard Time in China?
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
Affordable fashion e-commerce faces too many shortcomings in China's environment: skewed product selection, adjustment cycles that drag on too long, and — above all — a complete loss of the "affordable" edge it enjoyed in its home market. Cheap goods are not in short supply here.
@@QD_BODY@@
Affordable fashion e-commerce faces too many shortcomings in China's environment: skewed product selection, adjustment cycles that drag on too long, and — above all — a complete loss of the "affordable" edge it enjoyed in its home market. Cheap goods are not in short supply here.

Log on to ASOS's Chinese website today and the featured slot on the homepage is still rotating a mid-season sale banner: everything from 50% off storewide. But by May 2016, this site will cease operations. If you want to buy ASOS after that, your only option will be shopping from its official UK website.

Everything goes back to what things were like before the Chinese site ever existed in 2013.

On April 8, ASOS issued a statement: "We will cease local operations in China and continue to serve our growing base of Chinese customers through [ASOS.com](http://ASOS.com) (the official UK website)." The statement explained the ending this way: compared with the 6,000 products currently available to Chinese customers, ASOS.com offers a choice of 80,000 products. That said, its Tmall flagship store has so far announced no changes.

Founded in 2000, ASOS is now the UK's largest fashion e-tailer, with more than 6 million active users, over 60,000 products in total, and its own in-house brands, selling more than 800 designer labels. After turning profitable in 2004, its business has spanned the globe, covering Russia, the US, Germany, France and other countries.

Since launching its Chinese site in 2013, however, ASOS has struggled in China. According to its financial reports, ASOS's China operations lost a total of 8.6 million euros. And closing the Chinese website, the distribution center, and the Shanghai office will generate one-off closure costs of £10 million plus an operating loss of £4 million in this fiscal year.

ASOS did make an effort for the Chinese market — at the very least, it built a Chinese website. To make more Chinese consumers aware of its existence, in its second year in China ASOS also opened an official flagship store on Tmall. Still, people found this fashion e-tailer that had crossed the ocean rather odd.

Under our earlier report, [*Judging from the New Arrivals, ASOS Is Still Testing Chinese Consumers' Taste*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/18377.html) (《从上架的新货来看，ASOS 在中国还在试探消费者口味》), readers once commented on its product-selection problems: "The range of styles is still a bit small — I'd rather buy directly from the UK site," "The styles on the domestic site lag the UK's by several seasons," and, "Please, no more full open backs or V-necks plunging to the navel."

On Zhihu, many people have also gathered to discuss ASOS's Chinese website. [User joyce commented](http://www.zhihu.com/question/20680737) that the outlook was not very promising: ASOS's design aesthetic still follows a relatively niche path in China. Without enough reverence for the designers, even the styles you fancy can easily be found on Taobao. And the styles on the site are not distinctive or striking enough. In a post titled [*How Do You Rate ASOS's Chinese Website?*](http://www.zhihu.com/question/21947376/answer/20921070) (《如何评价 ASOS 中文官网？》), user NINI wrote: although ASOS's Chinese site offers free international direct shipping above a spending threshold, its prices are actually higher than the UK site's, and most of the styles are leftovers from the UK.

In short, many people were skeptical about ASOS's Chinese site: either the styles tried too hard and didn't suit Chinese wearing habits, or — compared with the goods they used to hunt for on the UK site — the Chinese site's inventory updated far too slowly.

But these problems were not unique to ASOS. Any foreign fashion e-tailer entering the Chinese market suffers from some degree of failure to adapt, and that awkwardness and unfamiliarity quietly hides in one of the steps as you shop with relish, eyes glued to the screen.

SHOPBOP, the fashion apparel retailer Amazon acquired in 2006, once drew praise for its Chinese website. Everything looked "perfect" — until you had picked out your items and were ready to pay, filling in your name and shipping address. Only then would you discover that, although this was a Chinese website made specifically for Chinese consumers, the step required you to spell out your name in pinyin, like a foreigner. If you typed Chinese characters, the system would automatically prompt: "Our system currently does not accept non-English letters. We are working to improve this situation. Please resubmit your information using English letters (ABC...)."

Another point foreign fashion e-tailers often overlook: when browsing e-commerce pages, Chinese consumers like to open a new page for every new product they view, whereas foreign sites still tend to rely on forward/back arrows to flip through pages — which does not fit Chinese consumers' browsing habits.

Foreign fashion e-tailers may often forget that Chinese consumers were trained up by Alibaba. Educated by Taobao, these Chinese consumers are used to what is probably the world's cheapest — and possibly fastest — logistics system; even when buying a small item worth just 10 yuan, they get to see many detailed photos. On top of that, they can buy all kinds of "goddess same-style" pieces, "unmissable" deals, and "one jacket to last the whole winter"... Tacky as these phrases may be, they work — compared with dry product descriptions that say nothing at all.

All of these details are hard to find on ASOS's official Chinese site. Scroll down a product page and you see a few detail-oriented photos at most — for women's wear, there are only three photos of a garment on a mannequin: front, side, and back. Men's wear follows much the same pattern. Product descriptions are remarkably plain, merely listed under the brand name, such as "stretch jeans" or "fine-knit side-slit pullover dress." The point is: with comparable quality, ASOS's products are not cheaper than Taobao brands.

"For Chinese consumers, ASOS has no advantage in style, no advantage in price, and no advantage in brand. Its decision to close the Chinese site is easy to understand," Jenny, a veteran of the fashion e-commerce industry, told QDaily (a pseudonym, at the interviewee's request).

"ASOS's quality is average and its prices are fairly low. But in China, low-priced products are a dime a dozen. So why buy ASOS at all? Where is your competitive advantage?" Jenny said.

For foreign e-tailers newly arrived in China, most of their operating experience is transplanted from their home markets. There is nothing wrong with the imitation itself, but they adjust far too slowly in the face of Chinese consumers' demands.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702113749jTbWs2KfgL1xSVmX.jpg-w600)

In December 2015, ASOS opened a preview of its spring/summer new collection to the media in Shanghai. The styles looked plentiful at first glance: the women's wear was overwhelmingly the then-trendy '70s retro look, in bold, vivid colors, with plenty of sequins and woven structures, while the men's wear followed the style of the Yeezy line Kanye West did in collaboration with Adidas.

But in fact ASOS sold few products in China, and the share of third-party partner brands it brought in was very low (which is also why many consumers preferred to shop on the UK site). Only a very small fraction of products were sold on the Tmall platform, with no third-party brands at all — even among its own labels, only the affordable line was offered — and even the pace of new arrivals lagged the official site somewhat. To a degree, this reflected ASOS's understanding of the Tmall platform. But even on the Chinese official site, the range of styles remained smaller than in the UK, and the third-party brands it partnered with were not on the same order of magnitude.

At the spring preview, Daniel Jenks, then general manager for China, told QDaily: "We need to test the Chinese market; the styles and brands we originally brought in will be adjusted as our understanding of the market changes." ASOS also wanted to "narrow the size range, because the usual UK sizes 8–10 are not popular in China. We want to improve turnover efficiency by putting inventory into adding styles rather than into stockpiling sizes."

"When goods arrive, we have to shoot them, retouch the photos, and find models. Chinese customers aren't fools — they can tell at a glance what foreign sites look like. And for the Chinese site, the inbound process was long, tariffs were high, and new arrivals were slow. Fashion-loving customers don't like always buying old styles, and an e-commerce site can't keep discounting forever," said Lisa, a former employee of THE OUTNET.COM's Chinese site. Though she wasn't talking about ASOS, the problems are the same.

"ASOS's decision-making and communication cycles were too long," Lu Xiaoxia, vice president of overseas brand acquisition at Xiu.com, told QDaily. "It's a pity — they actually had first-hand data, but the Chinese market changes very fast, and it often took them years to carry out a single decision."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702113750wAH1jXaueDbRSzco.jpg-w600)

Over these years, foreign fashion e-tailers have come and gone in the Chinese market, but the perennial champion has been Alibaba.

Net-a-Porter Group SpA once launched a Chinese website for THE OUTNET.COM in 2009. In 2015, that discount Chinese site ceased to exist, folding directly into THE OUTNET.CN (the official website). And the Chinese online shopping site of US department store Neiman Marcus had an even shorter life: it withdrew from the Chinese market after just six months.

Between 2012 and 2014, foreign luxury e-tailers crowded into the Chinese market, with affordable fashion e-tailers close on their heels.

In those same years, Chinese people's understanding of fashion — especially their habits of luxury consumption — changed dramatically. In the past, spending from public funds was the main driver of luxury consumption in mainland China, but under the impact of the Chinese government's "anti-extravagance rules," that part of the spending shrank quickly, and Chinese luxury consumption gradually shifted to people paying out of their own pockets. Compared with public-funds spending, this kind of spending is, of course, far more cautious and picky. Consumers don't necessarily buy locally, and they don't necessarily use e-commerce either — they complete their purchases through overseas online shopping, or simply by traveling abroad themselves.

At the same time, right on the heels of the luxury e-tailers entering China, there were plenty of rival players of equal strength at home. Zara launched e-commerce in China as early as 2012; H&M started the same business in 2014. And the e-commerce arms of these fast-fashion brands, together with physical stores and Taobao, can all solve the very problems affordable fashion e-tailers try to solve — low price, speed, convenience, and style. They are all competitors of companies like ASOS.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016070211375063UnA940H1uwZQTx.jpg-w600)

Another issue affordable e-tailers run into is average order value. In retail, the size of the average ticket determines a brand's positioning. "At low price points, each order contains more items. For example, 1,000 yuan spent on 5 items versus 1,000 yuan spent on 1 item — the costs are not the same. To give an example: if product quality is poor but lots of people buy it, there will be a large volume of returns every day. That means warehousing and customer-service costs run high. First, someone has to pick out the 10 items of clothing and pack them; if you return them, someone has to put them back in their original place," Jenny said. So even if affordable e-tailers like ASOS sell a lot, they don't necessarily earn a lot.

All of this, if built on huge traffic, would not actually be a problem. ASOS and the many other sites that entered Tmall were plainly hoping to solve the exposure problem. But timing dictated that by the time it entered, Tmall already had a large number of homogeneous competitors, and the British were not willing to spend big on buying traffic — at the very least, we never saw their ads plastered everywhere.

It is worth noting that these are not problems only foreign fashion companies face when trying to enter or hold onto the Chinese market. All of our interviewees shared a similar line: "If these problems exist, Chinese e-commerce companies would die too."

Ren Guoqiang, senior partner at Roland Berger Management Consultants and vice president for Greater China, believes: "The people who liked ASOS were always a small band of trend-chasers. China has a huge population base — even awakening just one percent, one thousandth, or one ten-thousandth of its purchasing power would represent enormous potential. ASOS created its Chinese site precisely so that people who don't understand much English but want to chase trends could get to know the brand. From a business standpoint, closing the Chinese site was the right choice." After all, however well you polish the image, closing the Chinese site amounts to stopping the bleeding.

ASOS, which quietly withdrew three years after entering, may still have failed to understand what kind of market this is, what kind of users are on it, and — above all — who its real competitors were.

And ASOS may not be the only one that doesn't know the answers to these questions.
