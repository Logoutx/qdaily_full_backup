The question is whether it can solve its own problems in a much less forgiving environment.

Our company of the year is that e-commerce giant — founded some time ago, but listed in the US less than six months ago. It is controversial, yet impossible to ignore.

Founded by a man from Zhejiang, it rode the spread of new technology to [remake the logistics system](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/24169.html) and the way Chinese merchants large and small sell their goods, turning [more than 300 million users](https://www.alibabagroup.com/cn/news/press_pdf/p150129.pdf) into habitual online shoppers.

Its growth came with speed, and with [doubts raised again and again](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/28233.html).

Low prices were how it opened up the market: T-shirts for 9.9 yuan, rugs for 14.9 yuan, storage boxes for 5.9 yuan... These cheap goods on its homepage would set off alarm bells for you in a physical store. But a group of people who had only recently come online discovered the pleasure of buying cheap goods, and merchants found a new outlet for selling their wares on this new platform.

And beneath those loose entry thresholds, counterfeit goods were brought in by opportunists.

Across online communities, discussion of these fakes and knockoffs never stopped.

Under pressure, a customer-service team thousands strong was built to handle disputes between buyers and sellers. Meanwhile, [a reserve fund account](http://www.sohu.com/a/100095449_115267) was placed under the custody of ICBC: if a user found a problem after receiving goods, the merchant would not receive payment and might even be fined.

Along the way, its ads were planted into roadside billboards, subway carriages and even movie scenes. Advertising made a brand known, but it was also its way of proving that it had strength and credibility.

But the counterfeit problem was never solved, and it blew up fully after the US listing. Backed by internet giants, it burned through 1 billion yuan in three years, and even at listing it had not resolved the question of its profit prospects.

[More than one brand](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/4039.html) filed lawsuits, and [Muddy Waters, the well-known Wall Street short-seller](https://www.difubu.com/xinwen/zilanmu1/61.html) also set its sights on it. And the consumption upgrade that came with bringing in big brands inevitably angered existing merchants: [5,000 merchants besieged the company's headquarters](https://m.sohu.com/n/556293985/), and employees were afraid to wear their work badges to the office.

The post-IPO ride was bumpy — [it fell below its IPO price less than a year after listing.](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/13931.html) Its long pursuit of GMV rather than actual revenue, and the doubts cast on Double 11's numbers game, were all problems.

The company faces [no small challenge](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/15843.html) ahead; if it mishandles it, it will face a huge crisis. But it also faces a huge opportunity. Its growth coincided with [the birth and rise of a new consumer group](http://www.voidcn.com/article/p-hlzfwlde-bcd.html). Against a backdrop of [an economic slowdown](http://finance.people.com.cn/GB/6828223.html) and [weak foreign trade](http://finance.sina.com.cn/roll/20090120/02345779664.shtml), relatively cheap e-commerce may find more room to grow.

Had QDaily picked a company of the year at the end of 2014, everything above would have been our case for Taobao. But virtually every point applies to Pinduoduo as well.

In 2018, with different technology, different channels of spread, and a different audience, Pinduoduo repeated the growth — and the doubts — that Taobao began 15 years earlier.

But Alibaba's growth coincided with China's [middle class](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/4847.html) going from nothing to something, and with the rise of consumption in China. It caught a series of tailwinds after the 2008 financial crisis, as China shifted from a pure export economy toward domestic demand — and quietly, Taobao changed the way hundreds of millions of people shop, and their lives.

Pinduoduo also caught a similar great shift: 4G smartphones brought the internet to residents of third-, fourth- and fifth-tier cities; a new group of people (a sizable one) could now shop on their phones; and new trade disputes hit export manufacturing, forcing those factories to find new outlets.

Pinduoduo was born when everyone thought the e-commerce landscape was settled. As late as mid-2017, founder Huang Zheng still had to spend the first few minutes of every speech explaining what Pinduoduo actually was. Today, nearly six months after its Nasdaq listing, Pinduoduo had 386 million people place orders in the past year — more than JD.com, second only to Taobao. In this year's third quarter, its GMV over three months totaled 118.5 billion yuan, roughly one-third of JD.com's.

### Because the infrastructure was so different, Pinduoduo covered in three years the road Taobao took eleven years to travel

Pinduoduo was fast because Taobao had already walked the entire path, solving a number of fundamental problems of doing e-commerce.

When Taobao was founded, goods mostly went out by surface mail, and a single order often took a week.

Nobody yet knew how to [run e-commerce without taking commissions](http://www.sx2005.com/htmls/8wscy/10my.htm). In 2008, Taobao Zhitongche ads moved the search-advertising model into e-commerce; today even Amazon and JD.com have followed suit.

Starting before 2003, Alipay knocked on banks' doors one after another to enable quick payment. Before that, buying things online was not only high-risk but required complicated online-banking transfers, or even going to a post office to wire money and top up. For a long time, spending more than 500 yuan online in a day was something you could only do by completing paperwork at a bank counter.

More important was the consumer base. When Taobao launched, fewer than 100 million people in China were online, and all of them were sitting in front of computers.

![JD.com only stopped using Alipay in 2012 amid its conflict with Tmall](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181227055131ycdubj2kCDGva0HA.JPG-w600)

By 2015, when Pinduoduo was born — riding the spread of smartphones and China Mobile's 4G — 600 million people across China were going online via mobile, a figure today closing in on 800 million.

Today, setting aside the very young and the very old, the vast majority of Chinese are mobile internet users, and nearly all of them use WeChat. WeChat's social relationships and mobile payments are the foundation Pinduoduo grew from.

As an internet company launched in 2015, Pinduoduo also had a far better funding base than Taobao fifteen years earlier: it raised several million dollars in startup capital at inception, and by the IPO had taken in US$3 billion. When Taobao started in 2003, China's internet was not yet accustomed to businesses that didn't make money, and its early operating funds came entirely from Alibaba, which had just survived the dot-com bust.

### Alibaba and JD.com, listed and chasing high-margin business, left a market vacant

When Pinduoduo launched, China had 688 million internet users, rural internet users accounted for just 28.4%, and the largest e-commerce company, Alibaba, had 423 million annual active buyers — meaning several hundred million internet users had not formed the habit of shopping on Taobao. A sizable group of users was going unserved.

Meanwhile, social media already accounted for more than 40% of all mobile internet time, yet Taobao and Tmall claimed far less of it. Alibaba and JD.com, already listed in the US and under pressure over profit numbers, were both competing for higher-income users.

Huang Zheng described this scenario as a Pudong New Area with no malls: "The scenario is a bit like Shanghai building the Pudong New Area — the roads, bridges and other infrastructure are all in place, the people have already moved over, but there are no malls."

Alibaba only [figured out how to solve this problem in 2016](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/37018.html). Alibaba CEO Zhang Yong's formulation was that Taobao should become "more content-driven, more community-oriented, more local-services" — over the following years Alibaba gradually tilted more advertising resources toward a content-centric approach. He wanted users to find "the joy of discovery" on Taobao and to turn it into a genuine "time-killing" tool.

Social proved useful, as Alibaba later confirmed. According to CEO Zhang Yong's [comments](http://tech.qq.com/a/20160811/049396.htm) in 2016, after Taobao added social features, it was opened seven times a day.

At the time, Toutiao had sorted out its relationships with media and copyright and was at its growth peak. The redesigned mobile Taobao page replaced meticulous category displays with recommendation feeds, looking as if a mini-Toutiao had been stuffed inside.

Pinduoduo, from the start, had users "browse" rather than "search." Huang Zheng positioned Pinduoduo as the Facebook of e-commerce; later he switched to explaining it with Toutiao, the hotter Chinese app that likewise copied Facebook's news feed.

### Subsidized social sharing, no shopping cart, free shipping across the platform, cheap goods — these traits let Pinduoduo capture the people Alibaba and JD.com missed

Pinduoduo's purchase flow may be the simplest in Chinese e-commerce.

In April 2015, Pinduoduo launched its first social e-commerce app. The product, called Pinhaohuo, focused on team orders of fruit and fresh produce: users invited friends via WeChat, Moments and other social platforms to join a team purchase, which took effect once the required number of people was reached.

In the second half of 2015, Pinduoduo decided to turn the team-order model into a platform, and in September the Pinduoduo we know today officially went live.

Pinduoduo's functions are basically all built around WeChat. Many people first used Pinduoduo not in its app but through a price-cut or group-buy invitation sent by family or friends from a WeChat chat window; goods that were already cheap per unit could be a few yuan cheaper if two people teamed up. Many simply clicked the link, authorized WeChat login, completed Pinduoduo's registration process, and became users. The micro-merchant-style deluge of team-order messages was a major reason Pinduoduo was criticized in its early days, but it worked — today both JD.com and Alibaba have copied the so-called group-buying model.

Pinduoduo's goods are also extraordinarily cheap — specifically, across categories they run tens of percentage points below even Taobao, a platform itself known for cheapness.

QDaily compiled the average prices of the top five best-selling items in different categories on Pinduoduo, Taobao and JD.com twice this year, in July and December. It shows that as big brands have been introduced, Pinduoduo's prices have been rising, but they remain far below its competitors'.

![July 26](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181227094522dcHYGJUNaCRjM9Bx.png-w600)

On Pinduoduo you almost never see goods that don't ship free, and the platform has no shopping-cart function — both of which shorten users' decision time and decision process.

The shopping cart came from Taobao, designed to let consumers assemble enough items to qualify for free shipping, or buy several things at once. Although more and more goods now come with free shipping on their own, Taobao could never remove the cart — after all, "clearing the shopping cart" is now tightly bound up with Taobao consumption.

JD.com went to the other extreme: its app has no buy-it-now option at all, encouraging users to add everything they want to the cart and check out together. Because JD.com takes a cut of sales, the more users buy, the more revenue JD.com makes; and bundling orders into one checkout also reduces delivery costs for its self-run logistics.

One more detail shows how Pinduoduo prods consumers and shortens decision time. The day before this article was published happened to be Pinduoduo's joint promotion with Apple's Chinese distributors, where the best-selling phone was the iPhone XS Max, discounted by 1,500 yuan straight. To create a sense of purchase urgency, Pinduoduo built a flash-sale countdown page that resets every hour — basically, whenever you open the page during the day, you get the tense feeling that a flash sale is about to end within tens of minutes — though when the next hour arrives, the countdown starts all over again.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181227021011KSUqXc5Pwmuag4Jx.jpg-w600)

Ultra-low-priced goods, no need to compare prices, no side-by-side comparisons in a cart, and not even search appearing on the homepage as it does in other shopping apps... these traits dramatically lowered the barrier to shopping on a phone.

It is all about getting users to buy when they see a low price, compressing decision time as much as possible. Users need not think much — they see something they like, tap once, and the order is placed. In this way Pinduoduo users formed a shopping habit of high purchase frequency and low average order value.

Although JD.com also offered free shipping early on, and still has a free-shipping-above-a-certain-amount design today, under operating pressure JD.com does not want orders to be too fragmented; in the end, every aspect of its product design discourages offhand ordering. It is not just design: JD.com doesn't sell tissues and other daily necessities in small packages either, so as to reduce the proportion of shipping costs.

One app stimulates impulse spending, the other suppresses it; the difference in consumption habits accumulates day by day.

### Merchants were drawn to Pinduoduo by lower marketing costs, then pulled in more users by selling cheap goods

The difference from JD.com is this: on Pinduoduo, the costs of free shipping and low prices are borne by merchants.

Merchants are willing to bear them because the cost of selling on Pinduoduo is lower than on Taobao or JD.com.

In 2015, Alibaba's advertising revenue was 37.5 billion yuan against GMV of 3.092 trillion yuan, which means merchants spent an average of 1,213 yuan on advertising for every 100,000 yuan of goods sold. A year later, selling the same 100,000 yuan of goods cost 1,391 yuan in advertising. By 2017, that figure jumped to 1,657 yuan.

Pinduoduo happened, in 2017, to switch from taking sales commissions to the same model as Alibaba, charging merchants advertising fees and service charges.

Cheap goods and huge order volume rest on a logistics rhythm different from Taobao's or JD.com's.

Take 28 packs of tissues for 29.9 yuan. Of that, express logistics cost 0.125 yuan per pack, production cost 0.91 yuan per pack, and profit per order less than 0.9 yuan. What merchants value is "small margins, fast turnover" from one-time bulk orders.

Chen Jing, founder of tissue brand Zhihu, once told QDaily: when the brand joined Pinduoduo in March 2016, it had not expected demand to explode so suddenly. "Suddenly it blew up. Our factories couldn't keep up with capacity, and courier services couldn't adapt at once — we used to ship 5,000 or 6,000 orders, or 10,000 orders a day, but after getting on Pinduoduo we had to ship 100,000 orders a day."

To cut production costs, tissue brands Zhihu and Kexinrou both moved their factories into the pulp parks of raw-material supplier Lee & Man Group, compressing raw-material transport costs.

And users of cheap goods don't have high expectations for delivery times either. In first-tier cities JD.com has already compressed delivery to the point of ordering in the morning and receiving in the afternoon, and Taobao is increasingly approaching the speeds only SF Express could achieve a few years ago.

On Pinduoduo, buyers have 24 hours to form a team, and the minimum time to ship is limited to within two days — meaning that in Pinduoduo's early days, the fastest bulk goods shipped within three days of payment. This allows merchants to use the cheapest couriers, and merchants also have a clear expectation of order volume.

By the end of this year's third quarter, Pinduoduo's annual active buyers had surpassed JD.com's (305 million). Even on "Double 11," Alibaba's home turf, Pinduoduo became the company with the best growth outside Alibaba.

Fruit is one of the best-selling categories on Pinduoduo. Unlike fruit from ordinary fruit shops, merchants ship directly from the place of origin, cutting out middlemen and thus selling more cheaply than channels like fruit stores and Hema. There are also many such merchants on Taobao; the difference is that Taobao also helps promote the pricier fresh produce on Tmall Supermarket, while Pinduoduo only needs to recommend cheap goods.

In fact, the merchants are not fundamentally different from Taobao's, and neither are the goods. What matters is that Taobao's mechanism determines that it recommends merchants who can afford to spend more on advertising — often not the cheapest ones, but the most profitable for Taobao. As a new platform that started at the low end, Pinduoduo carries none of this baggage.

During Double 11, third-party data showed Pinduoduo was the e-commerce company with the largest absolute growth outside Alibaba, ranking fourth overall in scale. Pinduoduo did not disclose its total GMV, but stressed that more than 200,000 of the newest iPhones were sold on its platform.

Built around low prices, Pinduoduo users' consumption habits have largely taken shape. By time spent, the gap between Pinduoduo and Taobao is no longer large. The average active user spends 4 hours and 20 minutes a month browsing Pinduoduo — close to Taobao, and more than any other domestic e-commerce company.

In the most recent quarter, Pinduoduo's cost to acquire a new user rose sevenfold from a year earlier, to 89.71 yuan — but this is still far below what Alibaba and JD.com pay to acquire a new user. Correspondingly, the cost for sellers on the platform to acquire customers is also lower than what they would have to pay on Taobao or JD.com.

Meanwhile, despite having taken several rounds of investment from Tencent and recently officially entering the nine-slot grid of third-party services in WeChat Wallet, Pinduoduo's relationship with WeChat remains somewhat delicate.

By the first half of this year, 50% of Pinduoduo's traffic already came from its standalone app. Although Pinduoduo product shares can by default only go to WeChat, QQ and Qzone, opening such a share link inside WeChat leads to Pinduoduo's webpage — not, as with JD.com or Mogujie, to a WeChat mini program. It is still encouraging people to download the app rather than stay inside WeChat's mini programs.

All of this is Pinduoduo stimulating users to open its own app frequently and form shopping habits.

### Tolerating counterfeits was one reason for Pinduoduo's rapid growth; going public quickly left the trust crisis fully exposed

Compared with Tmall and JD.com, Pinduoduo's entry threshold is low: individual merchants can join with zero requirements, and the threshold for business merchants is also very low (1,000–10,000 yuan). This attracted a large number of merchants in a short time, but also brought the counterfeit problem, which will raise compliance costs after listing.

"Vivi," "vovo" and "Xiaomi New Product" were all once popular brands on Pinduoduo. Even after the criticism, Pinduoduo shut down thousands of stores and began selling proper brand-name products. But the comments under Pinduoduo product listings are often discussions of whether items are genuine.

Although Pinduoduo, starting in 2017, saturated the airwaves with ads like Taobao once did and sponsored variety shows, none of this offset the trust problem brought by fakes and knockoffs. As for Huang Zheng's own view that the media conflates "counterfeits" and "knockoffs," that did nothing to help win users' trust either.

Listed less than three years after founding, Pinduoduo repeated the counterfeit crisis Alibaba went through after its IPO. Questions about the authenticity of GMV data and fakes on the platform [gradually emerged over the year](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/15843.html) after Alibaba's listing, and Alibaba's stock price lost half its value within that year.

With that precedent in front of it, Pinduoduo did not have as much buffer time as Alibaba. [All its problems erupted in a very short span of time.](https://www.qdaily.com/articles/55820.html)

[Huang Zheng said Pinduoduo would not adopt the Tmall model](https://www.qdaily.com/articles/55820.html) — not now, not ever. "That model doesn't work for us," he said. "I have to come up with a different way to do the so-called brand upgrade; it should be something different."

But in fact, Pinduoduo is courting big brands to join, just like Taobao. The iPhone, which went on sale two months after launch — an exception — is a recent example. After big-brand partnerships came in, lookalike knockoff brands on Pinduoduo largely disappeared. These merchants had helped Pinduoduo win early users, but were disposed of as Pinduoduo moved upmarket. History always rhymes.

Meanwhile, Alibaba is making adjustments of its own.

On the surface, both Alibaba and JD.com have launched similar group-buying models. But the real effort to compete with Pinduoduo for users' attention is Alibaba's "content" e-commerce, launched long ago. Users now linger longer and longer, and Taobao recently integrated XiaoHongshu content — all to get users "browsing" on Taobao. And to counter the flow of small and mid-sized merchants to Pinduoduo, [at its most recent earnings analysis call,](https://tech.sina.com.cn/i/2018-11-02/doc-ihnfikvf0075704.shtml) [Alibaba announced](https://tech.sina.com.cn/i/2018-11-02/doc-ihnfikvf0075704.shtml) it would delay monetizing its information feed to better support merchants' operations. These are all moves to fight Pinduoduo for merchants and users.

Today, the average Pinduoduo active buyer spends 894.4 yuan a year on Pinduoduo. Alibaba's figure for the same fiscal year is 8,732 yuan. The difference in revenue is the gap between Pinduoduo and Alibaba — and it is also Alibaba's challenge in turning around to fight Pinduoduo for users: convincing investors to accept ever-lower per-user spending is always the harder sell.

But the transition Pinduoduo faces this time is more urgent. Problems that took Alibaba more than a decade of development and a year after listing to erupt have been compressed by Pinduoduo into a few months.

[The density of problems it has encountered](https://www.qdaily.com/articles/55820.html) matches the speed of its growth.

Before the summer of 2017, Pinduoduo was a company the media barely cared about; the IPO suddenly pushed it into the spotlight.

As a listed company, if Pinduoduo cannot solve its problems, the fallout will come faster and hit harder. Nor does it have Alibaba's luck: after Alibaba stumbled in 2014, the year it listed, it quickly rode a US stock bull market that lasted years, obtaining capital at low cost and pouring it back into e-commerce — and even the wars over offline retail.

In 2008, Taobao, five years into its operations, rode the spread of the internet together with China's growing middle class, gaining sustained growth amid shrinking export trade and government stimulus for consumption, becoming a force that today shapes the consumption habits of the entire country.

In 2018, Pinduoduo, three years into its operations, rides the spread of mobile internet together with the growing consumers of third-, fourth- and fifth-tier cities. Export trade prospects now face challenges again, but after years of property-driven growth and mounting debt, consumption is not as easy to stimulate as it was ten years ago, and the Chinese public's optimism about the future cannot match that of a decade ago. Can Pinduoduo solve its own problems in such an environment and completely remake the consumption habits of hundreds of millions of people?
