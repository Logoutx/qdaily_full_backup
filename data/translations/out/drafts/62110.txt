**This week’s top recommendation: articles in QDaily’s annual Books series on Japan ([Part 1](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61787.html) and [Part 2](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61789.html)) and [India](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61693.html).**

All three articles carry the flavor of “stones from other hills can polish jade,” hoping to use other countries’ experiences to reflect on the problems China is facing and will face.

The two articles on Japan examine the present situation and confront aging and population issues from the perspectives of people, society, and culture, covering in particular questions of population structure and the demographic dividend, welfare society, the division of employment, the middle class, the middle-income trap… The various problems Japan now faces are often seen as China’s future troubles. Is this really the case?

Most of these issues involve policy. Scholars like Liang Jianzhang have been beating the drum, hoping the top leadership will change policy. But, as [author Liu Zhizhen reminds us](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61787.html): “An overly urgent desire to change the status quo breeds excessively high hopes for the policy level; yet whenever we place hopes on the top leadership, we must always remember the power—or destructive power—of policy in an authoritarian state. While forcefully advocating ideas that we believe, and that may indeed be correct, we must be even more vigilant against their abuse. Put bluntly, in the realm of policy we must guard against Ceaușescu-style fertility policies.”

India and China are both large developing countries. In the process of rapid economic growth, they have also encountered many common problems, such as the middle class, social transformation, class mobility, justice… But because both are large, the problems are complex and hard to resolve, so these works can only offer some clues for understanding Indian society. Yet they are enough to make us reflect deeply—for example, on whether our attitude toward the poor is problematic. Compared with skyscrapers, this is the true measure of a society’s level of civilization.

“When we speak of tolerance, are we being kind and respectful? When we speak of solving problems, are we merely thinking, *Don’t bother me*? When we speak of high-end employment, urban renewal, transformation, and livability, are we only thinking about whether our property values will rise? They are a group of people who, despite living humble lives, have the same capacity for feeling as everyone else; their souls and their trust in humanity are equal to those of all others. What makes George Orwell unhesitatingly side with the poor is his faith in the goodness of human beings. They are not a problem to be solved; they are ourselves.” [the article writes](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61693.html).

Readers often complain that most of the books we recommend are translations, with very few works by Chinese authors. In fact, in the India article we also gave one reason: we do not have enough realist works that present social reality. This has to do with the space for public expression, and also with mentality.

For example, Liu Zhizhen notes that Indians’ feelings toward V. S. Naipaul are probably similar to Chinese people’s feelings about Lin Yutang’s *My Country and My People*. This week, we happened to recommend a book, [《林语堂传》 (*The Biography of Lin Yutang*)](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61948.html), which records the controversies Lin Yutang faced at the time. It feels as if some people today are still the same.

“There was one reaction I did not expect: saying that by writing *My Country and My People* I was ‘selling out my country and my people to make a fortune.’ The people who say such things are shameless. How can their minds be filled only with personal gain? How can they not believe that someone can make a sincere and deep analysis and interpretation of their own nation and culture? This kind of motive-based accusation is despicable. What counts as true and wise propaganda for China? Westerners are not fools. If you package China as a great beauty, perfect in every way, who will believe it? My attitude is to tell it like it is, emphasizing that China is a developing country slowly emerging from years of war and poverty. This mentality that cannot tolerate any criticism of present-day China is a form of inferiority complex that must go.” [Lin Yutang wrote](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61948.html).

Seen this way, the saying “stones from other hills can polish jade” is itself problematic. Because we are not necessarily jade either. Especially after China’s rise, learning to be the “stone” and humbly learning from the experience of “jade” and other “stones” may be the more pragmatic attitude.

**In addition, this week we also highlight two business stories.**

[One is the first installment in the series *What Exactly Is Antitrust Opposing?* (《反垄断究竟是在反对什么》)](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62044.html). We believe antitrust has once again moved to the center of the political agenda in the United States, with penalties escalating to breakups and targeting the largest tech companies—Google, Facebook, Amazon, and Apple. It has now been 69 years since the last new U.S. antitrust law was introduced, 25 years since the last breakup, and 21 years since the last major lawsuit. But the reason it has reappeared is the same as the original breakup of Standard Oil: a fear that individual freedom and rights are being violated, traceable directly to the liberties protected by the First Amendment to the Constitution. Its return also has to do with changes in companies. Today the five most valuable companies in the world are tech companies, controlling digital services embedded in the lives of billions. And what they affect is no longer just photos of cats and dogs.

[Another is a business history of the hedge fund, 70 years after its birth](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61958.html). We found that 70 years later, there are at least 10,000 hedge funds worldwide, with assets under management close to US$3 trillion. On average they apply 7x leverage, meaning the funds they actually deploy exceed US$20 trillion. Among them are those that specialize in equity hedging, those that bet on corporate mergers and acquisitions to earn stock-price premiums, those that wait for corporate bankruptcies and trade debt for profit, and those that engage in macro hedging…

In this process, financial markets gradually accepted the hedge funds’ complex trading logic. This also became one of the foundations for the later emergence of all kinds of elaborate financial derivatives. Some institutions created financial contracts that had never existed before, trying to perfect the market; investors could theoretically use these new tools to allocate assets and manage risk more efficiently. Finance also became incomprehensibly complex. Of course, we know that once it reached China, it became even more complex.

**Actually, sometimes “polishing jade” carries too much utilitarianism; stepping outside oneself to simply appreciate and contemplate “stones from other hills” is also a wonderful thing.**

[The 65-year-old artist Cindy Sherman held her first exhibition in China at the Fosun Foundation in Shanghai](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61863.html). She has won almost every honor and award an artist could desire, and *The Art Newspaper* also named her one of the “25 most influential artists of the twentieth century.” She has created more than 500 works over 40 years playing other women—or, more precisely, different “images of women”—which has led many to see her work as pioneering feminism and even as foundational to the theory of the “male gaze.”

[The 73-year-old artist Maggi Hambling held her first retrospective in China at the CAFA Art Museum in Beijing](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62006.html). The retrospective includes more than 60 works spanning oil paintings, prints, drawings, and sculptures, attempting to cover her career in full. She has been called “the grumpy old grandmother of the British art world.” In the interview, her temperament is also clearly visible—sexy, like her paintings. “Victor Hugo once got very angry at trees because they can live to be 300 years old. I’m a bit like that,” Maggi Hambling said.

[Beijing’s oldest live house, Yugong Yishan, has closed; we recorded its 15-year history](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61923.html). Yugong Yishan reflects not only changes in music—bands, audiences, and so on—but is also a microcosm of the development of live houses in China. Politics and capital shaped or destroyed it together. “Yugong Yishan began planning to end more than a decade of this management model, bring in outside investment, and open venues in cities like Guangzhou, Shanghai, and Shenzhen… There are also many problems that moving cannot solve. And before industry standards that can be referenced and implemented emerge, underground small venues remain in an undefined gray zone where they can be shut down at any time,” the article writes.

**By the way, this week the two series *The Great Little Studio* (《了不起的小工作室》) and *1999 Memoirs* (《1999回忆录》) both have new installments.**

The former is about photographer Ni Liang, who, wanting his images to be taken seriously, started a publishing venture called [《无像》 (*Imageless*)](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61750.html); the latter is the story of star IT journalist [Liu Ren](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61994.html) and the IT community DoNews he founded.

**Finally, a few more things worth noting:**

[Since the start of 2019, A-shares have risen for nine straight weeks](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61951.html). The Shanghai Composite has climbed back from 2,493 points to above 3,000; the ChiNext board has risen from 1,250 points to around 1,700, leading the world in gains. While everyone is cheering the rise, listed-company shareholders have also been announcing plans to sell stock. As of March 6, a total of 368 companies had issued share-reduction announcements, more than in the same period in previous years. Moreover, among them are companies whose shareholders plan to “liquidate” their holdings.

[Second-tier cities are becoming more aggressive in attracting “unicorn” companies to set up locally](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62093.html). In 2018, six cities absorbed 10 new unicorn companies, with the highest relocation subsidy reaching 65 million yuan. Defined as a company valued at US$1 billion, China had 161 such unicorns across 15 cities in 2018, with a combined valuation of about US$713.5 billion. More than 80 percent of the companies are based in Beijing, Shanghai, Hangzhou, and Shenzhen, and they account for 95 percent of the total valuation.

[The pharmaceutical industry kicked off the year with major M&A](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62070.html). Deals worth US$169.8 billion were completed within two months, close to the full-year 2018 record of US$163 billion. M&A transactions in the pharmaceutical industry have been very active, and many companies have grown into giants through constant acquisitions. Their M&A goals are the same as in other sectors: first, to speed up product launches and increase revenue and profit; second, for listed companies to buy innovative or frontier drug companies and give their stock prices a high valuation expectation.

[Zara parent company Inditex released its 2018 earnings report](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62079.html). Last year its sales were €26.1 billion; by any measure, this was its slowest sales growth in at least five years. [Adidas, meanwhile, delivered a report showing double-digit net profit growth](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62063.html), driven by 23 percent growth in Greater China. But Adidas entered a somewhat awkward period throughout 2018. The reason is that demand for the lifestyle sneakers that had won the company attention in recent years began to slow.

After days of back-and-forth, [the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) will follow the lead of other national aviation authorities and ground Boeing 737 MAX aircraft](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62068.html). The grounding order was issued in the early hours of the 14th Beijing time and took effect immediately. Aircraft in flight landed and were then grounded; the rest remained on the ground awaiting the FAA’s latest notice. The FAA said that after comparing the latest data, it found similarities between the Ethiopian Airlines ET302 crash last week and the Lion Air JT610 crash late last year, and made the grounding decision on that basis.

[K.T. is an Amazon “Vine Voice”](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/60850.html). Amazon gives her free products, and in return she tests them and writes reviews as a trusted professional reviewer. K.T. ranks among the most prolific of these reviewers. Over the years, she has several times cracked the top 100 of Amazon’s overall ranking of top reviewers. For every new product sent to her, she spends time researching it and writes a concise but complete review.

[On March 12, the largest college-admissions corruption case in U.S. Department of Justice history was made public](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62034.html). According to CNN, the BBC, and other media reports, 50 people were arrested and charged, including two SAT/ACT entrance-exam administrators, one exam proctor, one college administrator, 13 elite-university sports coaches, and 33 parents of students. Among them were well-known corporate executives, investors, and celebrities.

On March 15, students in more than 700 locations across 71 countries will skip school for a global climate march; [one iconic figure is 16-year-old Swedish student Greta Thunberg](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62005.html), who on August 20, 2018, skipped class and sat outside the Swedish parliament building, calling on the government to respond to climate change, thereby setting off a chain of global repercussions.

[Africa will become the next focus](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61986.html). *The Economist* describes the situation Africa currently faces as “the third power struggle” and explains how this one differs from the first two. Although Africa still faces rivalry among various powers at this stage, the article takes a more positive view of this competition, predicting that if Africa’s current wave of development moves toward greater transparency and democratization, it will help the continent benefit from it.

[Coral reefs around the world are dying, yet the reefs of Bonaire are well protected](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61728.html). The main reason is that the tiny island has a small population—only 19,405 people—which keeps development to a minimum. In addition, the island’s 40-year-old marine protection system and reef restoration efforts are why its coral reefs can thrive, making it a model worth learning from for other parts of the Caribbean.

[In Brooklyn, New York, there is a weathered warehouse where producers and set designers often come to this recycling center](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61746.html), looking for computers that technological history has thrown in the trash. The prop shop inside the warehouse reuses obsolete old electronics, renting them to film and television productions to help crews create a period feel. All the items were dropped off by their original owners.

[March 14](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62031.html) is physicist Albert Einstein’s 140th birthday and the first anniversary of Stephen Hawking’s death. To commemorate these two great physicists, we recommend two articles for each. The two on Einstein are [*Why Are We So Obsessed with Einstein—Sometimes Even Accepting Quotations He Never Said?* (《为什么我们如此迷恋爱因斯坦，有时就连他不曾说过的“名言”都照单全收？》)](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/51574.html) and [*Einstein Explained Everything with Relativity, and It Happened Exactly 100 Years Ago* (《爱因斯坦用相对论解释一切，这事恰好发生在 100 年前》)](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/18156.html); the two on Hawking are [*Hawking Has Died, but His Ideas Echo in the Universe* (《霍金去世，他的思想在宇宙中回荡》)](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/51099.html) and [*Stephen Hawking’s Posthumous Work Is Published, Pondering Ten Big Questions Facing Humanity* (《史蒂芬·霍金遗作出版，思考人类面临的十个大问题》)](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61947.html).
