Anger can be a tool for improving society, a means for merchants and politicians to profit, the motivation for revenge—it all depends on how you respond to it.

Anger is taking over people's public lives. Political scientists at Emory University found that in 2012, fewer than half of American voters felt intensely angry toward the opposing party's presidential candidate; by 2016 that share had risen to 70%. Surveys by the Pew Research Center, meanwhile, showed that only 8% of Americans were angry at the federal government in 2001—a figure that had tripled by 2013.

Anger was once defined as an emotion meaningless to modern life and of little benefit to social progress, a problem to be solved, something a mature person and a mature society should try to suppress. That was until 1977, and an experiment by James Averill, a psychology professor at the University of Massachusetts Amherst.

To understand how often this emotion occurs in ordinary people, why, and with what effects, James Averill chose a quiet small town and handed out questionnaires. Greenfield had only 18,000 residents, mostly comfortable middle-class folk, with twice as many churches as bars. James Averill expected that people would only lose their tempers occasionally and would feel embarrassed afterward. The results were precisely the opposite: most people got angry frequently, and over small things—who takes out the trash, what time the kids come home, politics at the dinner table. These feelings were usually expressed through restrained conversation and rarely escalated into physical conflict. More importantly, expressing anger actually helped solve problems: afterward both parties became more willing to listen and more relaxed. More than two-thirds of the targets of outbursts said "they realized they had been wrong."

From this James Averill concluded: anger is one of the most information-dense forms of communication, forcing us to listen to one another and face problems head-on. From then on, the study of anger became a focus for many scholars. Later findings included: people tend to regard those who express anger well as more competent and stronger, suited to leadership. Anger can motivate people to volunteer for difficult work, and even make them more creative.

But over the past year, anger has shown more of its destructive power. The Supreme Court confirmation hearing for Brett Kavanaugh and Christine Blasey Ford, the professor who accused him of sexual assault, lasted more than nine hours and concentrated the anger permeating American politics. From the October 27 shooting at a Pittsburgh synagogue to the November 7 shooting at a country bar in Thousand Oaks, California, the attackers had both posted hate speech online before acting.

In the January cover story of *The Atlantic*, author Charles Duhigg analyzes how this emotion became dominant, how it has changed American lives, written American history, and how people ought to deal with it. For those of us who are so often swept up in anger, this sorting-out has reference value too.

### "Righteous" Anger

In history, anger has often been an important tool of social movements—especially when it is legitimized, transformed from a personal emotion into a moral sense of right and wrong.

Cesar Chavez was one of those who wielded this emotion well. In the mid-1960s, the civil rights movement was in full swing in Southern California, while the Central Valley was very quiet. There, 250,000 farm pickers lived in hardship, most of them undocumented immigrants from Mexico or the Philippines who spoke no English and could only make a living picking grapes and melons in the scorching heat, facing dismissal every day if their work fell behind. This was also a group that unions could never reach. Too poor and helpless, even when brought to the negotiating table, some would quickly withdraw over a small favor from the employer.

Cesar Chavez appeared at this point as a leader. He worked logging by day, and gathered supporters on nights and weekends, founding an organization called the National Farm Workers Association, while also publishing a periodical called *El Malcriado* (The Brat), speaking, singing, and praying passionately at meetings. Cesar Chavez believed that if you wanted the organization to unite, it was not enough to have people fight for their own interests (back pay, better working conditions); you needed to set a goal that carried emotional resonance, to build solidarity through shared anger. The anger he harnessed was not the kind James Averill defined that could be released immediately, but something transformed into a righteous movement.

He then proposed that members of the National Farm Workers Association walk 300 miles to Sacramento, the California state capital, to attract more attention. On March 17, 1966, about 50 people set out carrying sleeping bags and luggage—the youngest just 17, the oldest 63. They were stopped by local police, but they also drew public attention. When they reached Sacramento, the crowd of onlookers had reached 10,000, and some stores in San Francisco and Los Angeles, after learning of their cause, stopped stocking grapes from Central Valley farms. Cesar Chavez then received a call from farm managers agreeing to raises and improved working conditions, and the plight of Central Valley immigrants finally entered the public eye.

Stanford researcher Hayagreeva Rao once tried to study how some successful social movements turn personal anger into a powerful moral appeal. He found that the 1857 uprising in India grew out of a tiny change. The British East India Company had existed for decades, and local soldiers, though badly treated, had never protested. The change came from a rumor about rifles: the cartridges for the upgraded rifles were greased and had to be bitten open, and the rumor said the grease was made from beef and pork fat. For soldiers who believed in Hinduism or Islam, this turned everyday grievance into a moral provocation—anger had been "righteous-ified."

### The Merchants Who Make Money Off Anger

After James Averill's findings were published, a series of follow-up studies caught the attention of big companies. Stanford professor Robert Sutton found that managers at a debt-collection agency had developed a set of communication scripts based on them. The company had 200 employees making 80,000 collection calls a month. On the phone, employees would first fake anger, speaking with hostility. Once the debtor broke down emotionally, they would turn gentle, offering comfort and understanding. The debtor would then breathe a sigh of relief and become more cooperative.

Of course, it wasn't only debt-collection companies that understood this. Harvard Business School now offers a course specifically teaching how to use anger effectively in negotiations—for instance, making employees angry at a common enemy and thereby uniting them. The most typical example: in 1997, when Apple was in dire straits, Michael Dell suggested it might as well declare bankruptcy and return the money to shareholders. When someone at a company meeting asked Steve Jobs for his comment, Jobs simply said: "Fuck Michael Dell."

Corporatized outrage works, but it is essentially a form of manipulation of people, and it tends to sacrifice the weak to protect the interests of the rich and powerful, having little to do with social justice. This shows up even more sharply in the media industry.

In 1987, a daytime talk show hosted by TV journalist Geraldo Rivera had dismal ratings. To generate controversy, he invited white supremacists, skinheads, Black people, and Jews to sit on a panel together. As one might imagine, the scene escalated from quarreling to brawling; the set was smashed and Rivera's nose was bloodied. But the show became a hit.

In 1996, cable channels like Fox News and MSNBC carried this approach "to new heights." A former Fox producer believed that if watching people lose their temper was fun, joining in might be even more fun. "The O'Reilly Report" was born, in which host Bill O'Reilly railed against elite scholars and liberal media institutions every episode—and even against people who said "Happy Holidays" instead of "Merry Christmas." In other words, the show turned the disgust viewers wanted to vent into the main course.

In 2013, the Pew Research Center said that as much as 85% of MSNBC's programming was commentary, with only 15% news. At Fox News, the share of news reporting was also only 45%. In other words, anger was used to manufacture talking points and hold ratings. Producers wanted to keep the audience perpetually angry; as for what consequences stoking that emotion would bring, or how to defuse it, they took no responsibility.

In the social media era, anger became even more public. The targets of people's rage turned into strangers who would rather fight than listen. This became an important source of business for social media companies, but it also built a vicious cycle of emotion.

Politicians are good at exploiting this emotion for their own gain. Explaining campaign techniques, Harvard Kennedy School professor Steve Jarding said, "If you can figure out what voters are afraid of, and convert that fear into anger by pointing to the moral failings of your opponent, they'll vote for you. The essence of campaigning today is anger and fear. That's how you win."

Speculating on fear serves merchants and politicians well, but it erodes American democracy and makes public sentiment even more uncontrollable. A *Washington Post* survey found that in the 2018 midterm elections, 35% of voters in battleground districts said "anger" best described their feelings about the election, while 24% chose "patriotic."

### When Anger Gets No Response

Righteous anger can elevate complaints into a pursuit of a fair world. But when anger gets no response, it leads people to believe fairness is unattainable, and triggers retaliatory behavior.

The wave of teacher strikes across American public schools that ran from last year into this year is a case in point. Larry Cagle, an English teacher in Oklahoma, planned a protest last fall: have 50 teachers from different local schools call in sick on the same day, and contact TV news to cover it—"Let me tell you why these teachers are so angry."

Larry Cagle's school had seen its state funding cut by US$300 million over the past 10 years. Although it was already the best state school in the area, teachers dared not use the air conditioning in winter or summer, and many schools in the state held classes only four days a week to save costs. Larry Cagle's own salary was US$1,980 a month, whereas he had previously earned twice that teaching in Florida. "I have three kids in college and a 14-year-old car I can't afford to replace. We can't live like this."

After the Oklahoma teacher strikes made the news, teachers in West Virginia, Colorado, and Kentucky began striking one after another.

Anger didn't solve the problem, but it formed a cycle of blame. Researchers call this phenomenon "rumination," and believe it brings the desire for revenge. Thomas Tripp, a professor at Washington State University, explained: "If people feel that the fairness mechanism or procedural justice has collapsed, revenge becomes more common."

### How to Deal With Anger

*The Atlantic*'s answer is simple: examine the source of your anger. "If we can stop and see that our anger is so often being exploited by others, we can gradually learn to free ourselves from their control."

A group of Israeli social scientists ran a fascinating experiment in Giv'at Shmuel, a conservative suburb of Tel Aviv. The community's residents were all devout Jews who supported right-wing politicians. The scientists' goal was to persuade them to let go of their hatred toward Palestinians and agree that Israel should stop building Jewish settlements in Gaza and the West Bank.

Rather than trying to point out these residents' errors, they went along with their thinking, posting online ads in support of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, praising "fighting for the sake of fighting." One ad printed the head of a well-known Israeli war hero with the caption: "Without war, there would be no heroes. For the sake of heroes, we need war." The ads ran for six weeks, ensuring all 25,000 residents would see them.

Post-experiment surveys showed that the share of residents who believed Arabs bore full responsibility for Israel's past wars fell by 23%, and the number who believed Israel should be tougher on Palestine dropped by 17%. Although the ads never mentioned "Jewish settlements," 78% of residents believed Israel should consider halting construction.

"No one wants to be stuck in anger all the time," one researcher explained. "Sometimes, if no one points it out, it's hard for us to realize we've already turned into extremists."
