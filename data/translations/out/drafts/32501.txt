For many people, "passive travel" — things like "tacking on some sightseeing around a business trip," "visiting your long-distance boyfriend/girlfriend in another city," or "doing volunteer teaching and exploring the villages nearby" — may be closer to their normal way of traveling.

When it comes to travel, we've found that some things have "gone out of style."

Take travel agencies. According to [data](http://www.chinanews.com/cj/2016/09-08/7998332.shtml) from the National Tourism Administration, the total profits of travel agencies nationwide fell 34.14% year over year in 2015. Domestic tours in particular drew complaints over cookie-cutter so-called features and thoroughly indifferent service; the [proportion](http://www.cnta.gov.cn/xxfb/xwlb/201604/t20160401_765499.shtml) of visitors entering scenic areas through travel agencies dropped from 60%–70% in 2010 to 20%–30% in 2015.

Online travel service providers like Ctrip and Tuniu are part of the reason travel agencies are losing money, but their second-quarter 2016 financial reports show these companies are themselves racking up [losses overall](http://it.sohu.com/20160905/n467694877.shtml). Whether they're being cold-shouldered or burning cash, travel agencies and online providers alike are searching for direction.

At the same time, many new things are emerging or becoming more popular.

People are no longer content with plain tourism, which is why programs and services now let you work, take classes, volunteer, or practice meditation while you travel; people pursue personalization and localization in every domain — staying in an Airbnb run by a local resident, eating at the little neighborhood restaurants the host recommends, walking through local residential blocks dressed no differently from the locals. All kinds of startups also hope to develop apps that let a person solve every problem of travel — planning itineraries, booking services, writing travel journals — with a single smartphone.

But judgments about how young people travel, and the supposedly attractive services built on them, are very often wrong:

Young people all have a "pack up and go" romantic streak — they'll quit their jobs on a whim and take a gap year.

Young people are all trying their hardest to travel on the cheap, visiting as many places as possible for as little money as possible.

Young people are easily influenced by social networks and trust them most when making travel decisions and looking for information?

...

To be sure, social networks, consumption upgrades, and the pursuit of individuality form the backdrop of young people's travel. But to understand this market, the stereotypes above offer no genuinely useful advice.

On this basis, we reviewed QDaily's coverage — from travel industry conferences and companies in the business to all kinds of young travelers (such as young people who have been through a gap year) — and also interviewed new-style companies and projects like Qyer Private Travel, Hive, and Daydream Travel. At the same time, to learn more about young people's views, the QDaily Research Lab conducted a number of surveys on travel spending, personal travel experiences, views of tourist attractions, and travel styles.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160923150259bGs1vrWhP7tfi5KC.jpg-w600)

Our main finding is that although young people are always labeled as loving to play and being good at travel, and travel KOLs typically target them with marketing like "go to an island and start the world's happiest job" or "a young person's first jungle trek," young travelers are not all spontaneous so-called travel experts.

For many people, trips like "tacking on some sightseeing around a business trip," "visiting your long-distance boyfriend/girlfriend in another city," or "doing volunteer teaching and exploring the villages nearby" may be closer to their normal way of traveling — and we've given this approach a name: passive travel.

### A big reason for passive travel is being busy

In a [report](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/31904.html) we previously covered, the millennial generation — born from the early 1980s to the turn of the 21st century — was described as a harder-working cohort: due to a weak economy, the sense of crisis that comes with entering the workforce, and technology blurring the line between work and life, 43% of them are workaholics, compared with just 29% across all age groups. They work harder than their parents' generation, feel a greater sense of crisis in the workplace, and take a more "long-term" view of their careers.

In China, young people on the 996 work schedule (9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week) don't have much time to take dedicated annual leave. A QDaily Research Lab [survey](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/1206.html) of domestic readers also showed that the two main reasons young people fail to use up their annual leave are that no one can cover their work while they're away and that they can't align their leave with the people around them to travel together.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160922173439MiBYsTlJvHLZK2zF.jpg-w600)

On top of that, young people actually hate traveling during the packed Golden Week holidays, because going anywhere during that window means being submerged in Chinese tourists.

Young people not long out of school still remember how they always took advantage of their enviable vacations (and skipping class) to travel off-peak. After starting work, they want to keep traveling without jammed trains or counting heads at scenic spots. So many young people resolutely skip Golden Week travel; their ideal vacation is stitching together a long weekend out of regular weekends plus annual leave, and booking bargain flights well in advance... though a perfectly planned trip sometimes falls through when they can't get the time off after all.

Sometimes they can only travel in the gap between two jobs — a gap that might be a break they give themselves, or might be the stretch after quitting without a new job lined up yet.

As for business trips, research abroad on youth travel trends uses a word, [bleisure](http://www.cnn.com/2015/10/13/travel/bleisure-travel/) (business + leisure): young professionals, full of drive and fresh curiosity, treat a business trip as a chance to get out and about within work, whether or not there's any actual sightseeing involved.

For young people still in school, studying abroad or on exchange, long-distance relationships, and business trips have also become triggers for various kinds of passive travel. The ever-growing cohort of international students and exchange students almost always had at least some sightseeing built into their original purpose for going abroad. Young people in long-distance relationships contribute a considerable share of their income to the transportation and travel industries.

So for many young people, passive trips outnumber active ones over the course of a year. The "pack up and go" romantic streak is more of a gimmick; these frequent passive travelers need reliable services that actually meet their needs. Here are some of our observations about passive travel:

### **The typical signs of passive travel look something like this**

1. Not all gap years are fully prepared or even self-determined. Gap years in real life very often happen because someone didn't get into a school, hasn't found a job, or can't find a job — more like a Year spent struggling inside a gap in life.

2. Some people simply want to escape, throwing themselves first into a completely foreign place; others are forced into idleness after a temporary failure in job hunting or school applications.

3. Young people doing working holidays sometimes complain that they're "working the whole time, with no time to travel."

4. The disorientation of living somewhere new, harsh conditions, and language barriers can all become problems. But for young people who join volunteer projects full of expectations, the biggest frustration centers on "is what I'm doing meaningful?"

5. During a gap year or volunteer teaching, with no pressure and plenty of scattered free time, demand for two- or three-day trips to nearby areas is greater.

6. When applying for overseas exchange programs during study abroad and university years, there are many opportunities for in-depth travel with peer groups. This period may become the longest stretch of travel in the past five or ten years.

7. Students on study tours and international students have another travel annoyance: when parents or friends come to visit, they don't know where to take them or can't enjoy the same things together.

8. Young professionals fresh in the workforce like business trips more than anyone else; no matter how little time there is, they look forward to sneaking in some fun on a business trip, though it doesn't always work out.

9. Travel enthusiasts on business trips don't always get the chance to go out and play, but they put real effort into food and hotels, seeking out locally distinctive things without spending much extra time.

10. What long-term business travelers want most is for travel to disrupt their daily habits as little as possible; they may prefer the standardized service of chain hotels, and after being treated as a regular they'll post on Moments with feigned resignation to express their quiet delight.

Beyond passive trips, we also raised and tried to answer the following questions about how young people get out and travel.

### How do young people choose a travel destination?

1. It's not only middle-aged and older people who like vacations. In our [survey](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/315.html), "I've been a work dog for most of the year and want to treat myself better" surpassed both "go see the world" and the "pack up and go" romantic streak as young people's biggest motivation for travel.

2. Recommendations that aren't like travel-destination recommendations are more popular — an official-account article or a hot Zhihu post that vividly brings to life just one of Japan's cuisine, the friendliness of Taiwanese people, or the seclusion of a mountain guesthouse is enough to make young people's hearts flutter.

3. They've long been tired of stale, formulaic attractions; "little known" has become one of the best accolades for a tourist destination today.

4. For travel guides, Mafengwo and Qyer used to be mainstream; now people prefer Zhihu, or even Moments — on such platforms it seems easier to find "little known" attractions and reliable ways to play.

5. They're plagued by information overload when planning itineraries and wish there were a reliable reference that solves all their problems at once, tailored to their own needs.

6. That reference might once have been Lonely Planet — but after one use, it gets ruthlessly discarded because its travel information is too out of date.

7. The biggest problem with UGC travel journals is too little substance. QDaily Research Lab once ran a survey, "[What can't you stand when looking for travel guides](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/697.html)?" — 1,036 people couldn't stand travel-mood UGC guides with no substance, and 393 couldn't stand "you recommend a restaurant without even giving the address."

8. Even in the age of social networks, when deciding on a travel destination people still first think of the places depicted in books and films they love.

![In the QDaily Research Lab survey "[How do you choose a travel destination](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/1200.html)?", among 3,650 responses, the most-agreed option pointed to this choice.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160926181412fa5sdYx4hv6mAzFK.jpg-w600)

9. Many people may not have a specific destination in mind before deciding on an overseas trip; ready-packaged products lower planning costs and so influence travelers' decisions. (@Bai Yifan, head of Qyer's "Private Travel" business)

10. After becoming familiar with a destination, the second and third visits tend to center on interests when choosing where to go — cycling, diving, skiing, and so on — because they'll know the local best dive spots and ski resorts. (@Jing Yu, travel designer at Daydream Travel)

### What elements attract young people to a trip?

1. Young people wish, more than anything, that travel could bring them as close as possible to local people's lives — they don't want to look obviously like tourists, all loud and gawking.

2. In a related QDaily Research Lab [survey](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/1079.html), more than one in five respondents said they pay special attention to niche attractions and foods beloved by locals.

3. Between a place on review sites beloved by tourists from all over the world and a place where local elderly line up, there's no question they'd pick the latter — it feels cheap and special.

4. Another way to play local when eating is browsing local vegetable markets, fish markets, and wild-mushroom markets, then buying ingredients and seasonings no one has tried — far classier than buying those mass-market tourist souvenirs.

5. But there's one situation that makes them break their own preferences: traveling with their parents.

6. Choose a stylish local guesthouse — otherwise, what's the difference from staying in a chain hotel on a business trip?

7. Sometimes the main point of staying in a youth hostel or an Airbnb is to satisfy the need to "meet locals" without using apps like Tinder. But sometimes they're not particularly satisfied with the service itself — uncomfortable beds, for instance.

8. They can of course be drawn by distinctive things — design, the quality of toiletries, a spa, a gym — but cleanliness remains the basic requirement for a hotel.

9. If the hotel is beautiful enough, they don't much care about what to do locally — they may not have planned any itinerary before the trip at all — yet they care intensely about the quality of the accommodation.

10. They try local public transportation, including the kind where they can't understand a word of the language or read a single sign. Maybe they grab an Uber and learn about the local economy and everyday life from an English-speaking driver — not to save money, but to stay grounded.

11. When it comes to accommodation, men care most about air conditioning (76%), a free minibar (61%), a large-screen TV (52%), a balcony/terrace (34%), and daily housekeeping (27%). (@Data from UK online booking site Sunshine)

12. Women care most about an ocean view (85%), a bathtub/en suite bathroom (59%), and a comfortable bed (56%). Women's imagined use of a hotel is: "Ah, this beautiful view, ah, this cozy life, ah, this wonderful existence." (@Data from UK online booking site Sunshine)

13. Travel is a personal, casual affair; young people aren't interested in, or have never even heard of, government-run so-called "tourism festivals."

14. Young people certainly care about varied experiences while traveling — given a certain investment of time and money, they hope to also work, take classes, or volunteer while on a trip.

15. Customized-tour consumers are mainly honeymooners and family travelers — for instance, young couples who don't travel abroad often, and parents who want to take the elderly and kids traveling but don't want to spend the energy being in charge. (@Former Hive employee Zhou Xingxing)

16. Student groups in non-first-tier cities are more easily drawn to experiential travel services offered by institutions (with that eyes-lighting-up feeling). (@Former Hive employee Zhou Xingxing)

### Who do young people want to travel with?

1. They don't much like travel companions, but they want companion-style services. Young people pursue freedom when traveling, but that doesn't mean they'll refuse the accompaniment of various services — especially for solo travelers and those with no time to plan itineraries.

2. In a call for entries titled "[What small incident makes you feel the whole trip is ruined](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/1177.html)?", we found that more than half of the responses came from dissatisfaction with travel companions.

3. The criteria for a suitable travel companion: matching you in preferences for attractions, shopping, and physical stamina.

4. Young people also treat travel as an important test of whether a relationship can continue.

5. They don't mind traveling alone — the internet can solve most problems. But solo travelers do run into some troubles: mainly hotel costs with no one to split them, seeing a great view but only having a selfie stick for photos, and going to the bathroom at stations and attractions being a real problem too.

6. They search for places suitable for solo travel. Searches for "solo travel ideas" online have increased by 50%, and searches for "solo travel destinations" grow by more than 60% every year. (@Google data)

7. Chinese women actually love solo travel even more — at least 43% of Chinese women have now chosen to travel alone, slightly above the global average of 41%. (@TripAdvisor data)

8. Women are inherently more interested in travel than men — including solo travel, and including the pleasure of preparing meticulous, down-to-the-detail itineraries.

9. But in actual travel, women care more about a sense of safety.

10. Young people like independent travel, but that doesn't mean they hate group tours. The main reasons they dislike traditional tour groups are the formulaic guides, attractions, and shopping stops.

11. One reason for joining a tour group is a "sense of security" — psychological comfort for themselves (and for students, for their parents).

12. If a tour group avoids the hated spots, can recommend niche attractions young people like, and can solve problems like taking photos and eating together, young people have no reason to hate them.

13. Sometimes, compared with the crowd of companies trying to win your business before the trip, what to eat, drink, and do during the trip, and where to seek help when small problems arise, is a blank spot — there's often that feeling of not knowing who to turn to all of a sudden.

14. If you want young people to post your travel service on their Moments, beyond offering something different from what they already know, the more effective move is to offer something that lets them broadcast "I'm having a great life."

### How do young people spend money when traveling?

1. Strictly speaking, "budget travel" isn't popular anymore. Young people will also adopt a "since we're here anyway" mindset and splurge on some things while economizing on the road.

2. More than saving money, what they care about is that the one or two key services they value most don't degrade, and that the ways of saving money fit their own habits and preferences.

3. They pinch pennies shrewdly — even for overseas trips they can get visas processed and book various services on Taobao, cheaper than buying locally, and even a duty-free store membership card can be sorted on Taobao for a small sum.

4. When they don't know where to go, a bargain flight is a way to make the decision — but if they've bought a bargain flight, "then you have to treat yourself better elsewhere, like eating better."

5. They don't stint on food. In QDaily Research Lab's "[How do you blow your budget when traveling](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/1163.html)" survey, the food option "since we're here anyway, we have to try everything from Michelin-starred restaurants to street stalls" ranked third in votes.

6. They're tired of formulaic tourist souvenirs, but keen on the internet-famous local specialty foods that show up online all the time.

As the travel market grows, young people's choices have become richer. They're certainly not the traditional "since we're here anyway" stamp-collecting types; the "measuring the land with your own feet" and "making friends on the road" trends that emerged a few years ago now have younger, richer versions. Today's young people, in actual practice, have discovered many new travel needs and options, unlocking a more diverse set of hidden travel personalities.

Young people love to play, know how to play, and want to see the world; they're also careful calculators who go with the flow and enjoy life. In many cases, they'd rather their travels look like a carefully staged "life elsewhere."
