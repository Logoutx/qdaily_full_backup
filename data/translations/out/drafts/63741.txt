@@QD_BODY@@
Lin Yutang, who embodied the three roles of liberal critic, lyrical philosopher, and cosmopolitan critic

Qian Suoqiao decided to take up Lin Yutang studies in 1994. Over the past 25 years, his main scholarly output has been on Lin Yutang, including two monographs, *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth* and *Liberal Cosmopolitan: Lin Yutang and Middling Chinese Modernity*, as well as the edited essay collection *The Cross-Cultural Legacy of Lin Yutang: Critical Perspectives*.

Like the generation of May Fourth intellectuals to which Lin Yutang belonged, Qian Suoqiao, shaped by the atmosphere of the 1980s, also harbors an obsession with China—concerned with big questions such as where China is headed, how traditional Chinese culture can be reborn in modern times, and who the Chinese are. For him, then, Lin Yutang is a "chess piece." He tries to excavate this "piece," buried by the mainstream discourse of his own upbringing, in order to sort out his own history and discover himself: Might China originally have had another path to modernity? Could there be another way to live, now and in the future?

Qian Suoqiao is currently Chair Professor of Sinology at Newcastle University in the United Kingdom; his main research areas are Chinese modernity and Chinese-Western cross-cultural studies. Born in 1963 in Changzhou, Jiangsu, he grew up during the Cultural Revolution. When he took the college entrance exam, it was the early years of reform and opening up. "The so-called reform and opening up meant accepting the Western world, so learning English was the most reform-and-opening-up thing you could do. At that time, our teachers said Beijing Foreign Studies University was the best place in the country to study English," so he enrolled in the English Department at Beijing Foreign Studies University. After graduating with a bachelor's degree in 1985, he went to teach in the Foreign Languages Department at Shenzhen University.

At that time, like many Chinese, he was fascinated by Western theory, and translated the study of Michel Foucault, *Michel Foucault: Beyond Structuralism and Hermeneutics*. He also wrote to the book's two authors, Hubert Dreyfus and Paul Rabinow, hoping to study at the University of California, Berkeley, where they taught. They helped Qian enthusiastically, writing a recommendation letter to the chair of the university's Department of Comparative Literature. So in 1990 he went to the United States, and successively earned an M.A. (1992) and a Ph.D. (1996) in comparative literature from UC Berkeley.

In his first years at Berkeley, Qian's interest was still in Western theory. But, as he writes in the afterword to *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth*, "As my first-hand experience in the United States accumulated, I increasingly felt that Western theory could not seamlessly connect with my ever-strengthening obsession with China. To understand modern China, one must have a Chinese voice. ... I plunged into the Berkeley library and checked out all of Lin Yutang's books (some shelved under Asian studies, others under American studies). I felt I had found a Chinese voice, and gradually discovered what my fathers' and grandfathers' generations had actually done, thereby bringing me a new China in which I could thrive."

[The renowned German sinologist Wolfgang Kubin once commented](http://book.sina.com.cn/news/whxw/2019-04-16/doc-ihvhiewr6438960.shtml) that he is "the foremost Lin Yutang scholar in the world today" and that "a conversation with Professor Qian Suoqiao is never boring." The praise sounds high, but in fact Lin Yutang has not been highly regarded in either China or the United States, and there is little really good scholarship on him. In China, people generally see him as an important essayist in modern Chinese literature, emphasizing things like humor and sensibility; in the United States, many regard Lin Yutang as a Chinese American writer, often criticizing him for failing to conform to the supposed imperative that Chinese American writers should take America as their home—a kind of political incorrectness—and focus mainly on the single novel *Chinatown Family*.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190514170418yiGfnMCvBcpYeo2R.jpg-w600)

Qian Suoqiao finds all of this one-sided; it fails to see Lin Yutang's full picture and value. In his view, Lin Yutang, Hu Shi, and Lu Xun represent three coordinates of modern Chinese thought. "Their legacies are all important intellectual resources for twentieth-century China. But I have a premonition that in the twenty-first century, China is bound to rise as a major world power, and Lin Yutang's legacy will be especially useful and inspiring for China—and indeed the world—of the twenty-first century."

He believes that the two coordinates of Hu Shi and Lu Xun both need to be re-examined today. "The all-embracing 'scientific method' is very close to scientism—arrogant and dangerous, as if the meaning of human life were reduced to nothing but experiment and proof. ... Hu Shi's advocacy of wholesale Westernization (later revised to full Westernization) has always been met with doubt and resistance in modern China. From the perspective of postcolonial critics, one could say that Lu Xun turned a blind eye to the hegemonic nature of Western missionary discourse, while Hu Shi never acknowledged the existence of colonialism at all."

"Compared with the intellectual legacies of Lu Xun and Hu Shi, Lin Yutang's intellectual journey stands out in the following three respects. First, like Hu Shi and Lu Xun, Lin Yutang saw criticism as the mark of the modern intellectual. But as a liberal critic, Lin Yutang defended Mr. Democracy most forcefully, most steadfastly, and most eloquently—especially against authoritarianism's trampling of human rights; his criticism was at its sharpest and most unsparing."

"Second, as one of the advocates of the New Culture Movement, Lin Yutang was far less anti-traditional than Hu Shi or Lu Xun. Although he too criticized traditional culture, his attitude modified many of the New Culture Movement's vehemently anti-traditional arguments. Lin Yutang rediscovered resources in traditional Chinese culture, developed a lyrical philosophy, and carried it to the world with great success, thereby proving that traditional Chinese culture still possessed usable resources and vitality for China's path to modernity."

"Finally, unlike Hu Shi and Lu Xun, Lin Yutang's cross-cultural journey—whether in life experience or critical scope—highlights its transnational and global character. This is not merely the so-called question of translating or introducing Chinese culture to the world; rather, in this cross-cultural act of translation, Lin Yutang assumed the role of a cosmopolitan critic whose critical vision was directed at modern civilization as a whole (with the question of Chinese modernity as one part of it), rather than judging 'the writings of the universe' solely from the perspective and concerns of Chinese nationalism."

Yet Qian Suoqiao feels that discussing Lin Yutang and the history of his modern intellectual thought today still cannot be fully developed in many areas. "Today Lin Yutang has gained recognition as an important modern Chinese writer, and his works can be reprinted and published, but it is best not to discuss his political views and practices," he writes in *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth*. In earlier periods, Lin Yutang was entirely off-limits: for example, there was once a group photo of Lin Yutang with Lu Xun, Xu Guangping, Sun Fuyuan, and others, but when domestic publications printed the photo, Lin Yutang was erased. "From another angle, this also proves that Lin Yutang belongs to the twenty-first century, to our own era—he has not dated at all," he told *QDaily*.

### **One Writ of Habeas Corpus Is Worth All the Confucian Classics**

**Q: On the level of the liberal critic, you say: "Like Hu Shi, Lin Yutang was a liberal critic all his life. The core of Lin's liberalism is a philosophy of tolerance developed through his creative interpretation of traditional Chinese culture." In your view, although both are representative figures of liberalism, what is the difference between Hu Shi's and Lin Yutang's liberalism? For example, Princeton University professor Zhou Zhiping believes that Hu Shi and Lin Yutang represent two aspects of liberalism: the former emphasizing "freedom in law," the latter "freedom in spirit." What do you think?**

**Qian:** I think this view is quite interesting and points to something, but it is not entirely accurate. First, I want to emphasize that Lin Yutang placed special importance on law. For example, when discussing the characteristics of Chinese culture and the Chinese, he noted certain failings—such as being evasive, conciliatory, and loose. He said the key to many of these traits is that Chinese history has had no constitution protecting freedom of speech. If you speak too much and offend people, you suffer for it, so people learn to be evasive. With guarantees, the Chinese would be just like Westerners: they would protest, lobby, and fight for their own interests. But without guarantees, when people speak up, they not only suffer losses but may also end up in prison.

Therefore, Lin Yutang in the 1930s placed special emphasis on the rule of law. For instance, regarding the so-called legacy of Confucian culture, he believed that Confucian culture could be transformed in the political sphere and had democratic genes; he emphasized the humanism of Confucius and Mencius. In his view, tradition always contained elements of resistance. "Confucius wrote the *Spring and Autumn Annals*, and treacherous ministers and rebellious sons were afraid"—how powerful! Confucianism contains many such genes, and he set out to articulate them. His *A History of the Press and Public Opinion in China* was an effort in this direction. But he made it clear that, compared with the West, Confucian culture was obviously inadequate.

There is a very important statement by Lin Yutang: "One writ of habeas corpus is worth all the Confucian classics." A writ of habeas corpus is the foundation of British constitutional democracy. In the Middle Ages, when the nobility contended with the royal house for power, it was said that one cannot arrest people arbitrarily; if someone is arrested, one has the right to ask in court where that person is being held. One cannot simply seize a person and make them disappear. Lin Yutang said this was a major advance in the progress of human civilization. By contrast, in the late Ming, no matter how much you resisted, a eunuch could have you thrown into prison at will; there was no such guarantee. The other side said that even if you were imprisoned, at the very least they had to tell us where you were, and you had to have reason to argue your case. This was the method they devised, but Lin Yutang believed it was precisely the path Confucianism had explored, and that we could continue from there. So he in fact also strongly emphasized constitutional law. His involvement in the China League for Civil Rights was very enthusiastic; he hoped for civil rights and constitutional protection.

At the same time, I think the difference between him and Hu Shi is this. In his later years, Lin Yutang said that during the Debate on Science and Metaphysics, Hu Shi was basically of the Anglo-American pragmatist school, emphasizing logic, evidence, and empiricism. But Hu Shi had no understanding whatsoever of the vital force (élan vital) of the Bergsonian school in France—this kind of spiritual thing. Lin Yutang had studied in Germany and loved Heinrich Heine and Henri Bergson; moreover, in temperament he was actually a poet. This is a point of affinity with Lu Xun. So when he sorted through Chinese culture, he placed special emphasis on late-Ming figures such as Li Zhi, Yuan Zhonglang, and Jin Shengtan; all of these free-spirited literati were symbols of freedom for him. Of course, his model personality was Su Dongpo. He believed these people possessed a free spirit and emphasized spiritual temperament. Therefore, the philosophy of tolerance, magnanimity, and open-mindedness all spoke from the spiritual side.

We can make up for what was not done in constitutionalism. But at the same time, one must also have the free spirit of the literati; this too is a precious heritage we can inherit.

**Q: Similarly, we know that Lin Yutang and Lu Xun had many differences—the most obvious being their political tendencies. In your view, what did Lin Yutang and Lu Xun have in common?**

**Qian:** I think the relationship between Lu Xun and Lin Yutang was actually a case of "opposites attract"; fundamentally they seemed very different. Lu Xun came from a declining gentry family in Jiangnan, steeped in Chinese culture; he felt the negative aspects of Chinese culture very deeply and hated them intensely, and pursued the new. Although he studied in Japan, he especially admired Westernized things. He studied Western medicine; spiritually he wanted innovation. In *Diary of a Madman*, he hoped for a new human being to bring about liberation.

Lin Yutang was completely different. He grew up in a Christian family in China, from childhood steeped in the gentle aspects of Christianity, God, and so on; he attended St. John's University, where classes were in English and he received a Western education. However, he also always cared about Chinese culture while at St. John's, but his behavior and dress were very Westernized. This was quite rare at the time.

After Lin Yutang returned from studying in Germany, they came to know each other in Beijing, I think. The *Threads of Talk* circle had a gathering every weekend near Central Park. From Lu Xun's perspective, Lin Yutang may have been a flashy, Western-suited new-style figure, much younger than him—14 years younger. Chinese people could be so Westernized! Was this not the state he imagined Chinese people should also be able to reach? From Lin Yutang's perspective, the Zhou brothers had the bearing of typical Chinese literati. Their whole intellectual aura was that of Jiangnan. But as I said, although Lin Yutang was Westernized, he was in fact always exploring Chinese cultural knowledge. Of course, Lin Yutang's field of study at the time was linguistics, and linguistics was in the Chinese department, so literary work was all connected.

But another characteristic—and I think the most important—is what I just mentioned. In temperament, Lin Yutang was actually very different from Hu Shi. Hu Shi could be called a true gentleman, whereas Lin Yutang had a side that emphasized spirit and a poetic temperament; his critical angle was sometimes very sharp, and in this he had something in common with Lu Xun. We should not forget that the monograph *Lu Xun* (*Lusin*, *The China Critic*, December 6, 1928) was written in English by Lin Yutang in 1928—not the earliest, but certainly a very early one. So the two had a mutual attraction. Both were strong personalities. As Lin Yutang said in the 1930s, the most important thing was personality. They had backbone; they had personality; they had the air of freedom.

### **The 1930s Were the Culprits of the 1950s**

**Q: You mention that Lin Yutang held a conviction: to work toward seeing a democratic and free China, one must guard against dangers from both the far right and the far left. So in the 1930s, Lin Yutang was both anti-right (the quasi-fascist rule of the Kuomintang) and anti-left (communist ideology). I think this was very difficult to achieve at the time, so I would like to ask why you think Lin Yutang was able to do this?**

**Qian:** I don't think it was difficult. Many of our ways of thinking today have been conditioned, so this seems difficult. But at the time it was not like that. Look at history: Chinese liberalism had a very strong background. In the late Qing, Confucians such as Guo Songtao went abroad and saw England, and felt this was what Confucianism wanted—we want democracy. Communism and fascism were two forces that rose later. During the Republican era, I think liberal intellectuals were not a minority. In the 1930s, communism or the left wing in the realm of public opinion charged ahead and tried to overwhelm everything; it was very aggressive and seemed very important. But if you really counted heads, it was not the majority.

Lin Yutang's political attitude was consistent throughout his life; he never had any interest in the worldwide communist movement. Communism was a major phenomenon of the twentieth century; it affected not only China but was a global movement. So when it came to China, its influence was enormous, and many intellectuals were attracted to it—Lu Xun was too. Lu Xun cannot be considered a Marxist, but he was certainly attracted. Even Hu Shi was somewhat attracted; when he visited the Soviet Union, he wondered whether this might be a path for humanity. He also had some relatively positive responses. Lin Yutang did not; he never had much goodwill toward what the Soviet Union was doing. This probably had a great deal to do with his Christian background and his education in Europe and America.

So in the 1920s, he supported the National Revolution. He wanted revolution; Hu Shi opposed it. But we must be clear: the National Revolution was the revolution of the Kuomintang, and it pulled the Communist Party along. The Beiyang government was already a republican system, but the Kuomintang saw it as warlord rule and wanted to establish a Nanjing government itself, so he agreed. But in the end, the Kuomintang and the Communist Party fell out during the Northern Expedition ... The National Revolution ultimately came to nothing.

After the Nanjing Nationalist government was established, it was not, in Lin Yutang's view, so perfect—not the ideal that the revolution needed—but after all it was still the National Revolution. In the 1930s, the Communist Party wanted to continue the revolution and overthrow the Nationalist government. Even when the Kuomintang was riding high, Lin Yutang was by no means affiliated with the Chiang Kai-shek faction. As a liberal intellectual, he did not want to overthrow the existing government; he wanted to criticize. If criticism was not allowed, he would use humor and mockery. In the 1930s, unless left-wing writers began to surround and attack him, he would not counterattack; he never initiated criticism.

As a liberal intellectual, you must have judgment and grasp of the whole situation, context, background, and critique; you must have wisdom. At the time, Lin Yutang and Hu Shi were regarded as China's two foremost critics, but in fact there was a large group of them—not a small number.

**Q: In an interview, you mentioned that the generation of May Fourth intellectuals was obsessed with China and prescribed for it the remedies of Mr. Democracy and Mr. Science. I want to understand: how did Mr. Democracy and Mr. Science later lead to radical revolution? Do you think you have figured it out now?**

**Qian:** When Lin Yutang was in Taiwan in his later years, he wrote many reflections on the Chinese literary world of the 1930s, wondering what had become of figures such as Mao Dun and Lao She. Because at that time, the 1950s and 1960s, all of these writers were being persecuted, knocked down one by one, and also turning on one another. This was a terrifying thing. So Lin Yutang made a statement I find very interesting, which I quote at length in the final chapter of my book. He said, "The 1930s were the culprits of the 1950s"; these seeds were all sown in the 1930s. The intellectual leaders of the 1930s bore responsibility for the fate of Chinese intellectuals in the 1950s. At that time, they were all calling on youth to turn left: don't read old books, old books are poisonous—all of this was very radical. He said you caused the radicalization of the next generation of intellectuals. They were very young at the time, and later they all went to Yan'an. Their knowledge structure was inadequate in Western culture, and Chinese culture was even more out of the question—it had all been discarded—so all that remained was to rush toward Marxism.

So, in the process of Chinese modernity, it is not easy to find a reasonable path between Chinese and Western culture, and this kind of result often emerges. Because Marxism also came from the West; it was not native to China, and it was the most alien to the native Confucian system of thought—Confucianism has nothing so radical. Therefore, Lin Yutang believed that the roots all lay in the 1930s. We should not talk about how the Cultural Revolution was; the roots of the Cultural Revolution are not in the 1950s but must be traced all the way back to the beginning of the 1930s. Many radical manifestations were exactly the same as the methods of the 1930s left wing. In culture, they would surround and suppress; if someone emerged, they would not let you speak ... So you see those methods, many seem familiar. By the time of the Cultural Revolution, it became extreme.

### **Lin Yutang's Contributions During the War of Resistance Were Crucial, but Are Now Completely Underestimated**

**Q: On the two levels of lyrical philosopher and cosmopolitan critic, I am very interested in Lin Yutang's American years. You mention that during these 30 years (1936–1966), Lin Yutang, together with Pearl S. Buck, became the most influential Chinese voice in America—speaking to the Western world about China, both culturally and politically. Because you wrote this biography with the attitude of a historian, you express relatively few opinions of your own. How would you evaluate this period of Lin Yutang speaking to the Western world about China? Do you consider it successful?**

**Qian:** First, I think this period is a very important and interesting episode in the history of Sino-American exchange, and many people now seem to have overlooked it. At that time, people-to-people exchanges between China and the United States were actually at their closest. Look at how he went on the radio and called on Americans and Chinese to write letters to each other. This kind of gesture basically no longer exists today, but at the time it was actually possible, and he was able to do it. Moreover, I think his friendship with Pearl S. Buck and her husband Richard Walsh is also worth remembering. Intellectuals can have this kind of cooperative and interactive relationship. How many such relationships exist now, I am not sure.

As for whether it was successful or not, one can say it was very successful at the time—I think even politically. You know, an Indian writer in America once said that during the War of Resistance, it was worth more than gold for China to have a writer like Lin Yutang who could go to America and write. The Japanese did not have one. Think about it: if the Japanese had had a writer like Lin Yutang speaking in America, the picture would have been very different. So Lin Yutang's contribution to the War of Resistance has not been given enough attention; it is completely underestimated. But Lin Yutang's statements during the War of Resistance were extremely important at the time. Don't underestimate his letters to the "Letters to the Editor" column of *The New York Times*—that was no small matter. When Lin Yutang wrote, they published it. Look now: if you write, who will publish your letter to the editor?

**Q: In the 1930s, Lin Yutang opposed the quasi-fascist rule of the Kuomintang, but later he in fact took a pro-Nationalist government stance. This was an important reason for the later estrangement between him and Pearl S. Buck and Richard Walsh, and also a point of divergence between him and American public opinion at the time. Lin Yutang said then that "the collapse of the Nationalist government would mean the failure of liberalism in China," that "Chiang's failure is the failure of our generation," and even wanted Chiang to bestow on him the four characters "serve the country through writing." Is there not some contradiction between the earlier and later positions? How should we understand the complexity?**

**Qian:** Your question is really important, and I don't think there is any contradiction at all. First, we often have a very naive attitude toward the role of the intellectual and the intellectual's speech, especially from a post(-modern) theoretical perspective: we tend to think that the intellectual simply says no to power. No matter the time, occasion, or situation, as long as I protest, I am great, glorious, and correct, and my own status remains forever above it all. This is too simple. When intellectuals speak to power and criticize, they must look at context, history, and background; Chinese historical development is so complex, and each stage is different, so you must have different wisdom at every moment. Of course, you have your own standpoint, and that is revealed here. But it is not the case that because I am a liberal, I always say no to power. Such a liberalism does not exist. There is no baseless vacuum of an environment in which to speak; everyone lives in reality and must make judgments about things.

From this perspective, in the 1920s, Lin Yutang's political attitude was very different from Hu Shi's. Hu Shi did not want revolution; he opposed the Kuomintang and the Communist Party uniting to carry out revolution, believing that Chinese democracy should come step by step. You say the Beiyang government was corrupt, but you still have to rely on rules and teach it step by step. But Lin Yutang, Lu Xun, and the like supported revolution. By the 1930s, Lin Yutang and Hu Shi were together again; they both opposed continuing the revolution, while Lu Xun wanted revolution. So each era is different.

The political tendencies of Hu Shi and Lin Yutang in the 1920s—I think at the very least are a matter of opinion, each with its own merits. Hu Shi often had many correct aspects. Look: the revolution—you two succeeded, although you then fell out, but the Kuomintang still came to power. After coming to power, the Kuomintang's control over intellectuals' speech was even stricter than the Beiyang government's; in this sense, democracy regressed. So in the 1930s, Lin Yutang kept a certain distance from the Nationalist government, but he did not want revolution—this must be clear—and this was very different from the attitude of the left wing and Lu Xun. He had reservations about the Nationalist government, but as a liberal intellectual he wanted to exercise the right to criticize, which of course was often dangerous. Because at that time, once you criticized, you became suspected of being a Communist. So he even faced threats to his life.

But when the War of Resistance came, the situation was completely different. At the very least, the Kuomintang and the Communist Party were cooperating again; this was the great cause of the nation. So Lin Yutang believed that through the War of Resistance, China could survive by a hair's breadth and give birth to a new China. He was again hoping for the birth of a new China. At that time, Chiang Kai-shek was already leading the war effort and was the only leader of China during the War of Resistance. Lin Yutang was internationally famous and highly respected, and Soong Mei-ling in particular regarded him with new respect. His return was of course for the national interest; he had already spoken out abroad for the War of Resistance before returning. Soong Mei-ling said it would be wiser for him to go abroad and propagandize for us. I think all of this was very wise. Later, the idea of "serving the country through writing" is quite interesting to me: he wanted to show that he had contributed to the War of Resistance, and he always feared people would say he was outside the country and not on the battlefield or the front lines. This kind of accusation is very narrow. I think in the book I have made it clear how much Lin Yutang did during the War of Resistance; this is obvious to all. I also don't understand why Chiang Kai-shek did not bestow those characters—it's strange—but later Chiang wrote him the character for longevity on his seventieth birthday.

### **The 1980s Were Like a Return to the Passion of the May Fourth New Culture Movement**

**Q: I have seen some criticism of *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth* suggesting that you are a bit of a fan, too identified with Lin Yutang. What do you think of this criticism? Are there things about Lin Yutang that dissatisfy you?**

**Qian:** I think the word "fan" is too vulgar; I am not anyone's fan. I think my relationship with Lin Yutang cannot be explained as fan and idol; one could say it is a spiritual companion, a friend. The key is that people like Lin Yutang no longer exist today, so I can only look back into history to find a kindred spirit, a mentor.

I certainly think he had shortcomings. You can see this in the narrative. For example, I basically did not write much about many of his later novels. If not for the fact that he ran out of money because of the typewriter, he should have had the energy to write something better. He kept saying he wanted to write a masterpiece. One could say *Looking Beyond* is a vision, but I feel there should have been more and better works, rather than those novels. Writing these took up a lot of his time, mostly because of the need to earn money and make a living. But this is understandable; he too had to support his family. This is the most basic requirement for a liberal writer: you must be economically independent.

[Note: According to *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth*, the Mingkwai typewriter, which Lin Yutang successfully invented in the 1940s, could type English, Japanese, Russian, and Chinese, and could print 90,000 Chinese characters. Typing a single character required only three keystrokes, and it could type 50 characters per minute. He also applied for and obtained a U.S. patent for the typewriter. But because the Chinese market was unstable at the time, the typewriter never went into mass production. He was also heavily in debt because of the typewriter invention—"This venture cost me US$120,000 and wiped out all my savings." In addition to losing all his savings, Lin Yutang took out a loan from Chase Bank in the United States and borrowed money from his friend C.T. Loo. His wife, Liao Cuifeng, pawned her jewelry and even sold their Manhattan apartment. Although Lin Yutang eventually paid off his debts with royalty income, he never escaped financial difficulty and had to rely on writing to barely make ends meet.]

**Q: I remember you mentioned that his greatest literary achievement was not fiction, but prose.**

**Qian:** Yes. It lies mainly in his critical insight; he was a cross-cultural critic. For him, the most important element of modern culture was criticism. This is a very important point. What our era needs most is also people with cross-cultural knowledge, with solid grounding in both China and the West, and on that basis to have comments on anything, big or small. Your commentary can influence many people.

**Q: At first he wrote small critiques, later big critiques.**

**Qian:** Yes. *My Country and My People* and *The Importance of Living* are a kind of big critique. His critique of world civilization, his desire for a "philosophy of peace," could have been developed more deeply; it did not necessarily have to be written in the form of a novel. But he always felt that the novel form could sell better.

**Q: Besides *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth*, you also wrote *Liberal Cosmopolitan: Lin Yutang and Middling Chinese Modernity*. Could you introduce what problems you want to discuss in this book? The afterword of *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth* only mentions abstractly that you try to outline "liberal cosmopolitanism" as an alternative voice in the discourse of Chinese modernity, highlighting the significance of Lin Yutang's literary practice within the context of modern Chinese literary and cultural criticism.**

**Qian:** Because *Lin Yutang and China's Search for Modern Rebirth* is a historical narrative, I tried not to express my own opinions. My first monograph is a cross-cultural critique in which I had to step forward and say a lot. From Lin Yutang's cross-cultural practice, I outline another possible path that Chinese modernity might take.

Chinese modernity has two stages, with the First Sino-Japanese War as the watershed. I basically revolve around the axis of liberal cosmopolitanism to explore the relationship between nationalism and cosmopolitanism within Chinese modernity. From Lin Yutang's practice, we can see whether liberal cosmopolitanism offers us any insight. Because for the twentieth century, there were two major debates within Chinese modernity: one was the revolutionary cosmopolitanism of communism, the international communist movement; the other was the nationalism initiated by Sun Yat-sen. I believe Lin Yutang represents a liberal cosmopolitanism, but this liberal cosmopolitanism is in the same vein as liberal nationalism; it acts as a check on nationalism and must also have a global dimension. One cannot simply let nationalism expand unchecked. So, from this perspective, the liberal cosmopolitan school represented by Lin Yutang stands for another possible path for Chinese modernity.

**Q: When analyzing modern Chinese intellectuals, you pay close attention to the differences in their intellectual formations, which also lead to differences in the intellectual legacies they leave behind. If you were to analyze your own intellectual formation, what conclusion would you reach?**

**Qian:** I am quite self-aware. Our intellectual formation is one that grew up in the "iron house." We started primary school during the Cultural Revolution, and from childhood our education was "Long live Chairman Mao." After primary school, the Cultural Revolution happened to end, so we continued to middle school, and when we started taking the university entrance exam, we happened to catch the era of reform and opening up. Ideologically, this indeed raises many questions. Because the discourse of reform and opening up was completely different from what I had received since childhood, the larger environment prompted me to reflect: who was right? This is very important.

The 1980s were very interesting. They were passionate, very reform-and-opening-up. From a historical perspective, it was like repeating the sentiments of the May Fourth period. And Chinese history is truly sad: after 70 years, it was like going back again. But it's not too late! After the 1980s, from the perspective of economic and social development, it actually picked up many things from the 1930s. But culturally and intellectually, it was as if it had returned to the passionate state after May Fourth, when everyone was a poet, but much of it was very one-sided and radical. I personally became aware of this problem in the mid-to-late 1980s, so when I read Lin Yutang I found him quite interesting, somehow different. Like him, I was concerned with the so-called China question, the question of Chinese culture, who am I, what does it mean to be Chinese ...

**Q: In essence, your study of Lin Yutang is also a way of sorting out your own history and discovering yourself through excavating him. After more than 20 years of Lin Yutang research, how has your understanding of yourself changed?**

**Qian:** I think Lin Yutang is a spiritual companion for me; I am looking for a "father," a mentor from the father's or grandfather's generation. Through research, by uncovering this buried and obscured history, I have become more sober and able to handle things more calmly. For example, the fate of China after 1949 was, in many respects, not simply the Chinese people's own affair; it was decided by the Americans. Many things are actually not so simple.

Lin Yutang later became homeless in a sense; our generation can now learn from his being at home everywhere. His attitude toward the intellectual in politics, as an individual, in personality, and in life—I think is still very illuminating today.

### **Ku Hung-ming Was the Greatest Counterrevolutionary in Modern Chinese History**

**Q: You are working on research on Ku Hung-ming. Why are you interested in him?**

**Qian:** I can't reveal too much to you (laughs).

**Q: I have the feeling he could be considered a precursor of modern Chinese conservatism.**

**Qian:** You are right. I just said that Chinese modernity can be divided into two stages. From the year Lin Yutang was born (1895, the year of the First Sino-Japanese War) onward, China entered another stage, and the mental state of the Chinese literati underwent enormous change. From then on, revolution was unstoppable, one wave after another. But before the First Sino-Japanese War, the West came in; how did we respond? So doing research on Lin Yutang basically addresses the latter period, while doing research on Ku Hung-ming basically addresses the earlier period.

And I'll tell you, Ku Hung-ming was the greatest counterrevolutionary in modern Chinese history. He opposed things all his life. At the very beginning he opposed Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao; he felt Kang and Liang were Jacobins. Needless to say, he opposed the Kuomintang's 1911 Revolution. In his later years, when he died in 1928, he opposed the communist revolution even more. So if you want to find a true counterrevolutionary, it is Ku Hung-ming. I think he is a very interesting case that can open up the question of early modernity and also China's first response to modernity.

Let me add one more point. Ku Hung-ming had a moment of popularity in the 1990s, when some scholars translated his works into Chinese, but in fact it was far from enough; the materials are simply not complete. The reason Ku Hung-ming was brought out at that time was nationalism and patriotism. Ku Hung-ming was very patriotic and criticized colonialism and imperialism, although he wrote all of this in English. Ku Hung-ming was very erudite; his education was entirely English-style, and he especially loved to quote famous lines and aphorisms from British celebrities and literary masters. His favorite line was Samuel Johnson's "Patriotism is the last refuge of a scoundrel."

So, this still challenges many of our ready-made concepts and values today.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190514173436UHOEmXte2bBrWGFu.jpg-w600)

**Q: This year is the centenary of May Fourth. What would you like to say about reflecting on the legacy of the May Fourth centenary?**

**Qian:** The most important thing about May Fourth was that it proposed two things to establish: Mr. Democracy and Mr. Science. So let's look at how much we have accomplished today? I think Lin Yutang's lifelong efforts in this regard are very illuminating. His persistence on Mr. Democracy, and especially his critique of the authoritarian tendency that ran rampant throughout the world in the twentieth century—this is valuable.

Science goes without saying—how many Chinese literati were inventors or scientists? Lin Yutang rolled up his sleeves and did it himself, inventing the typewriter. At that time, inventing a typewriter was like later inventing the computer. He was thinking about how to organize the Chinese script so that it could be output. This was a very important effort.

On the other hand, May Fourth also had a destructive side: it wanted to knock down Confucius and Sons and carried out a very radical critique of Chinese culture. I think this has done endless harm. At the time there may have been a need for it, but look at how it continued down the line. When we were children there was also a movement called "Criticize Lin Biao, Criticize Confucius." What did Lin Biao have to do with Confucius? But the movement was called "Criticize Lin Biao, Criticize Confucius"—Confucius really had a hard time of it. But now Confucius is again being elevated to a very high status.

So, I think many people at the time of May Fourth had a nihilistic attitude toward Chinese culture; Lin Yutang, on the contrary, did not. He had many criticisms, but also many constructive proposals. How should we proceed? Confucianism, Daoism, Legalism, as well as literature and writing—specifically, how should we move forward? For example, he had his own views on simplified characters and literary style, not to mention his reflections on religion from the perspective of Chinese culture. So, I think these things are very illuminating.

For Chinese culture to be reborn, Lin Yutang's most basic view was that China must have constitutional government and constitutional protection before there can be freedom of speech, and only then will people speak out. Once people speak, there is momentum and energy. This is the most fundamental point. He said, half a *Han Feizi* governs the world; to truly realize the rule of law ... Western intellectuals do not talk about this, because they already have it, but for us it remains unfinished, an ongoing, tangled effort.
