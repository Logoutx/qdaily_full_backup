Over the National Day holiday we invited eight young writers to recommend books they consider good. Each has made considerable achievements in their own field and enjoys sharing new knowledge and reflections. This is the first of them, Mu Yao.

It's time to recommend books again.

Around this time last year, QDaily invited a number of interesting people from major accounts on Weibo, WeChat, and Zhihu to recommend a batch of books for us. They included [Zhihu founder Zhou Yuan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15357.html), [Guokr founder Ji Shisan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15272.html), [Zhang Wei, founder of the WeChat official account "Shixiang"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15320.html), [Wu Miao, founder of the WeChat official account "Leviathan"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15303.html), and [screenwriter Shi Hang](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15617.html), among others. The invitation at the time went: please recommend a book you think is good that you have read within the past year — it may be helpful to your profession, or it may simply be good. Chinese and English equally welcome.

This year, we've decided to change the format slightly.

In any era, professional readers are a scarce resource. These people's judgments about the value of book recommendations and reviews can become an important reference for our own book selection — and the reviews themselves can be books worth reading, like Tony Judt's *Reappraisals*.

Of course, not all of the writers we invited engage in such a weighty re-examination of twentieth-century history as Tony Judt does in his book. They are younger, more interesting, and sharp-eyed, and they are not professional writers; they hold a certain influence in their respective fields and enjoy sharing new knowledge and reflections.

We hope to introduce them to you through this "book recommendations + long-form review" format, offering a bit of reference for the small matter of reading.

Since personal experience differs, each person's book picks and reviews naturally vary in style — and puzzling over those differences is itself rather fun. From October 1 to October 8 we will publish one writer's reading list and review each day; we hope they are useful to you. If you have come across other good professional readers around you, please leave us a comment — you may see their article here before long.

Let's start with Mu Yao.

Mu Yao holds a PhD in mathematics and works as a software engineer; he lives in Shanghai. He is one of the creators of the [China Political Compass test (Peking University Weiming edition)](http://www.mwjx.com/mwjx/iq_tests/tests_politics.html). Through fifty questions, the test measures where respondents stand on the "left-right" coordinates of political, economic, and social views, and it is regarded as one of the most influential political questionnaires on the Chinese internet. Having studied and worked in the United States, Mu Yao will now interpret Chinese and American politics and history from his own perspective.

### **Book Recommendations**

**Kissinger's Memoirs, Vol. 1: *White House Years***

Author: [US] Henry Kissinger

Translators: Fang Huisheng / Zhao Zhongqiang / Wu Jigan

Publisher: Shanghai Translation Publishing House

Publication date: July 2016

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E7%99%BD%E5%AE%AB%E5%B2%81%E6%9C%88-%E4%BA%A8%E5%88%A9%C2%B7%E5%9F%BA%E8%BE%9B%E6%A0%BC/dp/B01JACE4DW/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475240849&sr=8-1&keywords=%E5%9F%BA%E8%BE%9B%E6%A0%BC%E5%9B%9E%E5%BF%86%E5%BD%95)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201610010020310ad9UjGuwsIvXHyV.jpg-w600)

Kissinger wrote prolifically. His memoirs of his years as Secretary of State run to three volumes: *White House Years* covers Nixon's first term (1969–1972); *Years of Upheaval* covers Nixon's second term up to his resignation over Watergate (1973–1974); and *Years of Renewal* covers the Ford presidency (1974–1976). Each of these volumes is a massive tome of over a million characters — lined up on a shelf, they can fill an entire row. The first two volumes were once published in China in translation for internal circulation in the 1980s; this year Shanghai Translation Publishing House has reissued the first volume, and the second and third volumes should follow in due course.

Why so long? It has to do with Kissinger's writing style. His memoirs are written in a mode that blends narrative with commentary: each chapter centers on a particular theme, and besides recounting his experience as a participant, it includes his observations of other world leaders, his depictions of the social psychology and public mood of the time, and the reflections — even confessions — he made while writing these memoirs. Kissinger's recollections of events are often meticulous and draw on a wealth of detailed sources, including the memoirs of contemporaries for cross-reference; in richness of content they far surpass the memoirs of most politicians.

But that only shows the book's value as historical material. Why recommend it to ordinary readers? Because for all its bulk, it is anything but dull. Kissinger's narration of historical events is as vivid as a literary writer's, often making you feel you are there in person. The humor in his prose (especially his self-deprecation) is superb — at times laugh-out-loud funny. And when he narrates tragic events (say, the tearing of American social psychology by the Vietnam War in volume one, or the wretched psychological state Nixon fell into during Watergate in volume two), his tone takes on a somber, deeply moving gravity. The most precious thing about this book, though, is his analysis of many historical events from the vantage point of one of the twentieth century's most important theorists of foreign policy — often incisive, every word worth its weight. You may disagree with Kissinger's political stance; you may object to, and sharply criticize, the policies he personally drove while Secretary of State. But even his enemies would agree that many of his historical insights have not gone stale half a century later.

Chinese readers will naturally focus on the chapters about China. But the book is full of gems — the chapters on the conflicts of the Middle East, for instance (especially the chapters on shuttle diplomacy in volume two), are splendid and equally not to be missed.

***Reading Chiang Kai-shek's Diary from a Macro-History Perspective* (《从大历史的角度读蒋介石日记》)**

Author: Ray Huang

Publisher: Jiuzhou Publishing House

Publication date: May 2011

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B004YG8NE62331337/?asin=B004YG8NE6&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B004YG8NE6&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161001002926UmJgwztVWZ8QC15d.jpg-w600)

Most of Ray Huang's works share a similar theme: China from the Ming and Qing onward could not adapt to the modern world because it lacked the capacity for management by numbers. His works on ancient history mostly dwell on detailing this deficiency, while his works on modern history (such as *Yellow River and Blue Mountains* (《黄河青山》) and this book) focus instead on discussing how a vast country without a tradition of numerical management adjusted itself — even reinvented an entire regulatory system — when forced into the modern world.

This book is his commentary on the diaries of the first half of Chiang Kai-shek's life, but its focus is not on Chiang the individual. What he tries to sketch is this picture: after the ruling system of traditional China collapsed from top to bottom in the first decade of the twentieth century, the Communist Party and the Nationalist Party took different paths to rebuild the machinery of state from the ruins. The Communist path was to build, through land reform, an agricultural system that could adapt to the modern world; the Nationalist path was to cobble together, top-down, a central government in the modern sense. In Huang's view, though each path had its own contingency, by a twist of fate the two proved complementary. When the Nationalists retreated from the mainland, the Communist Party inherited the architecture of top-level elite control — completing, as it happened, the framework of modern China's reconstruction.

Most of the book's chapters therefore explain Chiang's efforts to conjure, nearly out of thin air, an unprecedented central government. The author reminds us that many things and concepts we take for granted today did not exist in Chiang's time and had to be created by his own hand. One example: the author notes that in a personal letter to H. H. Kung (Kong Xiangxi), besides discussing family matters, Chiang asked him to procure large quantities of arms for the Nationalist army in Hong Kong — in the casual tone of relatives chatting about everyday affairs. To modern eyes this is doubtless a highly unprofessional practice, a thorough confusion of public and private. But at the time there was no professional civil service in the modern sense, nor any clear boundary between official correspondence and private letters. It was in such an environment that Chiang pieced together a central government by hook and by crook. That was his achievement — and his tragedy.

Is this a good lens for viewing modern Chinese history? Opinions differ. But the book at least hangs together (though at times the reader may feel, as with most of Ray Huang's works, that it hangs together a bit too well). The pity is that Huang never saw the bulk of Chiang's original diaries in his lifetime — the first-hand diaries only fully opened to researchers in 2009 — so he often had to rely on secondhand citations. Had he been able to consult the first-hand material, this book would surely be more valuable.

***The Glory and the Dream***

Author: [US] William Manchester

Translator: translation team of the School of Translation Studies, Sichuan International Studies University

Publisher: CITIC Press

Publication date: March 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E5%85%89%E8%8D%A3%E4%B8%8E%E6%A2%A6%E6%83%B3-%E5%A8%81%E5%BB%89%C2%B7%E6%9B%BC%E5%BD%BB%E6%96%AF%E7%89%B9/dp/B00T2DK826/ref=sr_1_2?ie=UTF8&qid=1475240721&sr=8-2&keywords=%E5%85%89%E8%8D%A3%E6%A2%A6%E6%83%B3)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609302110533esJAhqRkSyH5ZKa.jpg-w600)

*The Glory and the Dream* is an old book. Published in 1975, it covers America across the forty years from 1932 to 1972. More than four decades have passed since 1972, and in that time it has been recommended countless times for all kinds of reasons. Its novel structure, gorgeous prose, sweeping ambition, and fine-grained insight have left wave after wave of would-be imitators in awe. Whatever words of praise I might pile on here, someone will have said the same before me.

But I still want to recommend it once more. In a sense, it's because I feel we need a book like this today more than ever. One of its great distinguishing features is that while narrating the storms and shifts at the top, it also reserves considerable space for ordinary people — not "ordinary" in the statistical sense, but flesh-and-blood individuals. Their food, clothing, and daily needs, their loves, hates, and sorrows are all vividly, minutely visible. The book shows better than any other why politics is not merely the scheming of politicians and the shifting fortunes of war. In today's era of highly polarized ideology, political narratives often degenerate into clamor, division, and struggle over one sensitive issue after another. If Manchester could come back to life and write a *Glory and the Dream* covering 1972 to 2012, what a blessing that would be.

If I had to single out one part of the book, read the section on Franklin Roosevelt's death, *When the Courtyard Was Full of Lilacs* (《曾是丁香满庭时》). It is the finest textbook of prose there is; my own reading taste and writing ability were deeply shaped by it. It doesn't matter if you read it in translation — its beauty cannot be hidden.

***Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind***

Author: [Israel] Yuval Noah Harari

Translator: Lin Junhong

Publisher: CITIC Press

Publication date: November 2014

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E4%BA%BA%E7%B1%BB%E7%AE%80%E5%8F%B2-%E4%BB%8E%E5%8A%A8%E7%89%A9%E5%88%B0%E4%B8%8A%E5%B8%9D-%E5%B0%A4%E7%93%A6%E5%B0%94%C2%B7%E8%B5%AB%E6%8B%89%E5%88%A9/dp/B00NSGUFCQ/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475240655&sr=8-1&keywords=%E4%BA%BA%E7%B1%BB%E7%AE%80%E5%8F%B2)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161001002159YXhUPZAKeuoyx1CR.jpg-w600)

This is not a history book in the usual sense. The name "A Brief History of Humankind" may mislead readers into thinking it tries to start from the mythical age of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors, condensing the rise and fall of the dynasties of every people down to the modern day. But that is not remotely this book's subject.

Its perspective is far loftier. The questions it tries to tackle are not why a particular nation adopted a particular way of operating a state, but why nations need states at all; not why a certain religion became a certain country's established faith in a certain year, but why something like religion is possible in the end; not what achievements humanity has made, but why humans — and not some other animal — made them. Take one example: why did humans shift from hunting civilization to farming civilization? You might think the answer to this question obvious. But the book argues convincingly that this process may have been nothing like the evolution you imagine — in fact it was actually a kind of regression — and that it happened irreversibly because of nature's path dependence. The author was not the first to advance this view, but he is one of those who have explained it best.

In many ways the book will remind you of *Guns, Germs, and Steel*, that famous work of anthropology; in a sense it can be said to be its worthy successor. It reads wonderfully well. In fact, you can easily spend several hours reading it straight through, and even if you pause, it will mostly be to close the book and ponder — not out of fatigue or distaste. Its arguments are of course far from flawless (in this field, nothing is), but after finishing it you will surely find that the way you look at the world has been made new.

***Men Without Women***

Author: [Japan] Haruki Murakami

Translator: Zhu Jiarong et al.

Publisher: Shanghai Translation Publishing House

Publication date: February 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E6%B2%A1%E6%9C%89%E5%A5%B3%E4%BA%BA%E7%9A%84%E7%94%B7%E4%BA%BA%E4%BB%AC-%E6%9D%91%E4%B8%8A%E6%98%A5%E6%A0%91/dp/B00SY03XIO/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475240779&sr=8-1&keywords=%E6%B2%A1%E6%9C%89%E5%A5%B3%E4%BA%BA%E7%9A%84%E7%94%B7%E4%BA%BA%E4%BB%AC)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930211158j2v8FCOWDknrlh5w.jpg-w600)

This is a newly published collection of short stories by Haruki Murakami. It bears this name only because every story more or less involves the story of a single man; beyond that, the pieces have no essential connection. (Hemingway also has a collection of the same name, but the style is entirely different.)

My favorite piece in this collection is "Kino." Kino is a mediocre salesman who, coming home one day, walks in on his wife and a colleague having an affair. He silently turns and leaves, quits his job, and rents a small room in a corner of the city to open a bar. The bar does well, and he feels he has quickly forgotten the hurt he suffered. But things gradually begin to grow strange: the cat that often visited the bar suddenly stops coming, and many snakes begin appearing around the bar, craning their heads about. A mysterious regular customer tells him: leave this place quickly, wander, don't stay.

He obeys — but on the road he finally grows weary and slowly retreats into a small inn. He begins to understand that, because he dared not face the searing truth, the great hollow left inside him has never closed, and the snakes have realized what a fine dwelling it makes. At the end of the story, he is woken on a rainy night by a knocking that never stops — a sound that rings directly in his ear, leaving him nowhere to hide.

Time never seems to flow fairly. The heavy burden of bloody desire, the rusted anchor-hooks of regret try to block time from flowing as it should. And so time cannot fly straight like an arrow; the rain falls and stops through the night, the clock hands waver again and again in bewilderment, the birds still linger in their sleep, the faceless postal clerk silently sorts the postcards, his wife's beautiful breasts heave violently up and down, and someone knocks stubbornly, ceaselessly, on the windowpane. The knocking stays perfectly rhythmic, as if luring him into a deep, dim maze of suggestion. Knock, knock. Then knock, knock again. Don't turn your eyes away. Look straight at me. Someone whispers at his ear.

At the end of "Kino," Kino cautiously begins to look himself straight in the eye. Then he weeps, and the story stops short.

This collection is far from Murakami's finest work. But if you know and love his style, this book is enough to give you a warm, enjoyable reading experience — like meeting an old friend: no surprises, perhaps, but no disappointment either.

### **Book Review**

***Hard Choices***

Author: Hillary Rodham Clinton

Publisher:

Simon & Schuster

Publication date: June 2014

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Hard-Choices-Thorndike-Narrative-Nonfiction/dp/1410471004/ref=sr_1_2?ie=UTF8&qid=1475253281&sr=8-2&keywords=Hard+Choices)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161001004019yfRkcgXqBdYzF53Z.jpg-w600)

**Reading Notes: *Hard Choices***

**By Mu Yao**

I.

Just after Hillary stepped down as Secretary of State, I read a comment in the media that went roughly like this: yes, she was a successful Secretary of State, winning broad public support and international acclaim. But was she a great Secretary of State — comparable to the best in modern American history: Marshall, Acheson, Kissinger?

Kissinger's memoirs happen to be among my favorite books, so a comparison between Hillary and Kissinger makes for an interesting exercise: both are widely regarded as among the finest diplomats of their era; both accumulated enormous political prestige during their tenure as Secretary of State, to the point that their approval ratings far exceeded those of the presidents they served. Both faced international circumstances not particularly favorable to the United States, with a powerful rival whose ideology stood in direct opposition to America's rising swiftly and posing a strategic threat to the U.S. in every corner of the globe. Both inherited unpopular wars, yet had to weigh the overall situation and painstakingly sustain those wars in order to end them with dignity. Both tended to see global strategy as a set of mutually linked chessboards (hardly the mainstream mode of thinking in American politics — Kissinger complained countless times about how ill-suited and hostile the American bureaucracy was to his way of thinking), carefully extending America's global reach so as to rebalance the situation and preserve the initiative in international affairs when the odds were against them. And both were viciously attacked by domestic opponents — attacks that ran through their entire careers — but both endured.

But there is one fundamental difference between them: Kissinger did not want (and was not in a position) to run for president, while Hillary did.

That makes their memoirs two entirely different flavors to read. Kissinger's memoirs are the meditations of a philosopher in retirement: vivid vignettes and sharp critiques, painful personal reflection, and the detached musings of one who is both an insider and an observer — reading them feels like a heart-to-heart with a wise old man. That is exactly why I love them so much.

Hillary's memoir, by contrast, is a piece of campaign literature six hundred-plus pages thick. That is not to say it is shallow — there is no shortage of profound discussion in it about the state of today's world. It is that every line of it has been carefully calculated to ensure no possible negative political effect. The result is so bland it reads like a middle-school textbook and could put you to sleep. There are exceptions, of course — the chapters on Lawyer Chen, or on the Benghazi terror attack — but only because those events are so dramatic that any telling of them carries its own tension. Beyond that, the book is truly hard to take any reading pleasure in. That it received nearly unanimous negative reviews on Amazon is no accident.

II.

Hillary herself gave a series of interviews on her new-book tour. In my view, listening to these interviews is usually more interesting than reading the book itself. Not because interviewers tend to go for the gossip — in fact, even listening to the serious foreign-policy discussion in these interviews works better than the book.

This is determined by Hillary's own traits. She is an outstanding conversationalist: broadly knowledgeable, clear in her positions, rigorous in logic, precise in expression, and at ease in manner. Even if you disagree with her views, listening to her talk about international affairs (or any other serious matter) makes it hard not to feel respect for her welling up. Her high standing on the world diplomatic stage is surely due in no small part to this.

But on the other hand, unlike Kissinger, she is not a profound thinker, and she has not contributed any revolutionary idea or framework to the world. You can see this in her book and her speeches, and the feeling comes through especially clearly when you read. The book is organized by region: each chapter discusses the challenges facing that region and the corresponding U.S. policy, but not one chapter leaves you feeling that she left a mark of her own there. The only thing in it that can be called a diplomatic victory is Myanmar's opening — yet even that chapter makes her seem less the driver of the revolution than a bystander. It was Than Shwe's voluntary retirement and Thein Sein's bold reforms that fundamentally changed Myanmar; Hillary (and Aung San Suu Kyi, for that matter) merely seized the opportunity and helped it come to pass. As for the remaining chapters — the Middle East, Central Asia, Europe, Latin America, the Asia-Pacific — every one begins with difficulty and ends with difficulty. She executed American foreign policy superbly, keenly seized everything that worked in America's favor, and managed to hold the line; but it is hard to say what people will remember her for.

This is not a flaw of hers — she was always a doer, not a strategist. She is seasoned in maneuvering, experienced, broad in vision, and her understanding of the role of new technology at the social and political levels far exceeds that of her peers. It is hard to find any weak link in this book, yet after closing it you still feel a sense of regret. She passed; and to go down in history, passing is far from enough.

III.

Hillary has never concealed her hardline stance on China, or the ideological factors behind it. Compared with Kissinger, on this point she is rather more like a Republican. Kissinger did not like China's ideology, but his love of geopolitical statecraft (and the key role China plays in it) far outweighed his aversion to that ideology. Hillary is entirely different. In the book she speaks frankly about several leaders in China's foreign-policy establishment, in a tone much like the one Kissinger uses for the Kremlin in his pages. In Kissinger's view, Brezhnev and his colleagues overestimated the cards they held — their ambitions exceeded their capabilities. Hillary never says so outright, but the view between her lines is much the same.

Of course she is not simply "anti-China." More than once she mentions her fondness for Dai Bingguo. In a private conversation, Dai showed her a photo of his granddaughter and said to her: "This is what we're in it for." — which moved her deeply. (By contrast, Lawyer Chen comes off in her account very much like a clown.) But her overall attitude is cool-headed:

> "China-US relations remain full of challenges. We are two big, complex countries with very different histories, politics, and outlooks, yet our economies and futures are deeply entangled. Our relationship cannot be simply categorized as enemy or friend, and I fear it will always be so. We are sailing in waters without navigational markers; keeping our course while avoiding shoals and whirlpools takes both principle and flexibility, with frequent adjustments of heading — sometimes at painful cost. If we push ahead too hard, we may endanger each other. If we compromise too much, the other side will press for more. While weighing all these factors, we may also overlook that the other side has its own pressures and responsibilities. The further both of us travel, following the brave example of our diplomatic forebears who bridged misunderstandings and chasms of interest, the better our chance of making progress."

But there is no doubt that, before the historical trend of China's rise (and the accompanying surge of nationalist sentiment), the choices actually available to her were quite limited. What she faced was not merely one or two politicians she may not have admired, but more than a billion ambitious people and the complex gaze they cast toward the United States. The Cold War was not begun by Marshall and Acheson, and the Kremlin's aging was no credit to Kissinger — they were the workings of history's own deep rhythms. The same holds for the competition and entanglement between China and the US when it comes to Hillary.

This Monday Hillary came to our company for a sit-down and to promote the book, answering quite a few questions from colleagues. Had I been on the scene, I would probably have asked her the following:

"As a Chinese person educated in both China and the United States who is now working in America, every level of my life depends on the peaceful coexistence and common prosperity of our two countries. In your view, how long can this state of affairs be maintained? Will we see a new world war in the twenty-first century?"

I think I can guess the boilerplate answer she would give — but what I am truly curious about is what her honest answer would be, deep in her heart.
