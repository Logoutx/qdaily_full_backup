This is a far cry from what Silicon Valley had in mind.

At 12:30 a.m., a female streamer on the livestreaming app Live.me — her hair worn loose, dressed in an oversized gray T-shirt and denim shorts — sits cross-legged on her bed, rambling about her day and occasionally greeting viewers who have just joined. In the small hours, the app's front page is full of women streamers chatting with people like this.

A moment later, two streaks of purple light flash by in quick succession, and a purple sports car covers half the screen. Then several more small gifts fly into the livestream, including a "bride's veil," which automatically settles on her head once it takes effect.

The streamer, Felicia Sanders, is amused, and thanks the gifting users by name in her room. The sports car she received is worth 4,999 coins (which cost 415 yuan to buy, or US$60). The platform also has a pricier gift — a 19,999-coin castle that requires topping up more than 1,600 yuan to send.

If you've ever watched livestreams on Inke or Huajiao back in China, none of what just happened will be unfamiliar. In fact, even the app interface won't feel unfamiliar:

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170419131741RuCgQahJUAtO6ycX.png-w600)

The streamer's name sits in the top-left corner; across the top are the viewers who paid to pin their avatars there. Lower on the screen, comments from users who haven't paid flicker past, while gifts and heart-shaped likes keep popping up.

As with Chinese livestreaming apps, Live.me streamers earn their income from gifts. Viewers pay to top up coins and exchange them for gifts. The gifts a streamer receives are then converted into diamonds (the streamer's take is roughly half of what viewers paid), and once they add up to 4,000 diamonds, they can be cashed out.

Sanders, who turned 24 last month, lives in Los Angeles and has tried all kinds of livestreaming apps. The only time she had ever been covered by the media before was while doing a video livestream on Instagram. NBA star Chandler Parsons — the Memphis Grizzlies small forward worth US$20 million — told her in the livestream comments to pull up her top and show a bit more.

[Tabloids like the *Daily Mirror*](http://brobible.com/sports/article/chandler-parsons-asks-instagram-model-felicia-sanders-boobs/) described Sanders as an "Instagram model" — a term close to "Weibo influencer." She has well-defined features; she's photogenic, but not especially memorable — just like the influencer streamers back in China.

Sanders has 180,000 followers on Instagram, where her square photos document a life of trips to the beach, the sauna, nightclubs, the gym, and staying in at home. Among her Instagram photos, only one doesn't feature her as the main subject, and most of the time she's wearing a bikini to show off her figure.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170419132016LchSprdtzvnDwMG5.png-w600)

Most of Sanders's followers are men who praise her as "sexy, with a great body." But she doesn't shy away from her love life over it — she often posts photos of herself with her DJ boyfriend on social media. Beyond that, what she reposts most are pizza and naps — the former her favorite food, the latter what she loves doing in her downtime: dozing off.

Perhaps because not many people like watching livestreams on Instagram, Sanders's Instagram livestream didn't last long. She then spent a while posting videos on Snapchat, and later moved to Twitch, America's biggest game-streaming platform, to stream games. But she had no real gift for livestream interaction or gaming, so even with her Twitter account — followed by over ten thousand people — reposting and plugging her streams, her view counts on Twitch remained tiny.

American livestreaming platforms kept arriving one after another, and most of them never really caught on. Streamers like Sanders couldn't find a stage that suited them either — until Live.me.

In under a month, Sanders gained more than 20,000 fans and 790,000 diamonds. Under the platform's 50-50 split, she can now cash out roughly US$2,806. These days she streams one to three times a day, each session running anywhere from 15 to 90 minutes.

Sanders's streaming style doesn't really have any substance to it. She constantly thanks users for the gifts they send, and repeatedly reminds you to tap the "follow" button in the top-left corner, share the livestream to other social platforms, and like the stream.

She represents a whole category of streamers on this platform. Every night in the small hours, the feed on Live.me's front page is most dominated by sexy girls like this.

![The feed page after midnight U.S. time](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170419132500z7Xh4381RjerA6Pc.png-w600)

As long as you're young, good-looking, and have a hot, sexy body, you can earn decent attention and income — just like the top-ranked streamers you often see on Chinese platforms such as Inke and Huajiao.

### The top earners here are male streamers, and few will earnestly sing you a song

Live.me launched in April 2016. It's made by the Chinese company Cheetah Mobile, and is currently the livestreaming platform with the highest downloads and revenue on the U.S. Apple App Store. On April 28 this year, Live.me completed a [US$60 million funding round](http://tech.sina.com.cn/i/2017-05-01/doc-ifyetwsm1549083.shtml).

In its earliest days, Live.me also relied on internet celebrities to pull in traffic. He Yandan, Cheetah Mobile's vice president and the product lead for Live.me, told QDaily that to attract an audience at the start, they signed deals with some YouTube influencers to draw users in.

Roman Atowood, for instance, who has 12 million subscribers on YouTube, was one of Live.me's early signings. On Live.me he often appears in front of the camera together with his wife, and the two of them set up fun challenges in their livestreams — like whether six men can pull a moving Mini car, whether a rifle can split a tree a hundred meters away in half, or letting viewers weigh in together to name his daughter in the livestream room.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170419140220qvusKAI4wF1ngNML.jpg-w600)

This kind of idea, which goes viral so easily on YouTube, did not become the norm among American livestreamers. Those YouTubers are not regulars on Live.me, and most of the content on this livestreaming platform doesn't look like it was carefully designed either.

In the vast majority of cases, the thing American streamers most love doing in their livestream rooms is chatting with you. You won't find any "MC"-style shout-singing masters like MC Tianyou among them; even in the talent category, you rarely see a streamer hanging a microphone headset on and singing nonstop. But they like to tout "follows" and "likes" for themselves, and they love putting on music while chatting away with you.

It's hard to find streamers with similar looks and identical styles on these livestreams' front pages. In the most prominent spots, streamers of different races, body types, occupations, and dress styles can all attract attention.

The top-earning streamers on Live.me aren't sexy, pretty girls either. Of the top ten on the platform's revenue ranking, seven are men. The biggest earner among them has already racked up more than 40 million diamonds — the equivalent of over US$100,000.

But if you watch how they interact, it's not hard to understand why these male streamers — with neither looks nor talent — are so popular.

Brandon is one of many male streamers. His avatar is the kind of middle-aged American army sergeant you'd see in a movie, wearing the standard cold-weather camouflage of the U.S. Army, "U.S. ARMY" on the left chest and the surname "Brandon" printed on the right.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170420094530YMuC4B2JWK3dTRPa.png-w600)

Brandon streams at least three times a week, but he rarely wears his uniform — usually just a T-shirt or a hoodie in his downtime. He also likes to hold a cigar and slowly blow smoke rings at the camera. Brandon is a father of two daughters, and occasionally he picks up his 4- or 5-year-old little daughter so she can say hi to the audience... but more often, his state during a livestream is like this:

> "If you're not following me, you must be crazy!"
> "Follow me, guys, follow me now!"
> "Hurry up and f***ing share my livestream!"
> "One more time! Do it again!"

In less than thirty seconds, Brandon repeated all of this three times. He can keep it up without a pause for a good ten-plus minutes — exactly like the drill sergeants barking at new recruits in movies such as *Hacksaw Ridge*. There are quite a few American soldiers streaming on Live.me; flip through the list and you can easily find several streamers with military-uniform avatars.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170420095130cMv59qInWmftZkA1.png-w600)

Beyond the trappings of his profession, Brandon mainly uses raffles and mutual follows to get people to send him gifts.

"Send me 30 coins and that counts as one raffle entry — you can draw for a fireworks gift. Send 100 coins and you can draw for a Ferrari."

If a fan sends him a gift, he rallies all the viewers to follow that fan together. "Everyone, please follow xxx." "If you're not following xxx, you're crazy." Within half a minute, Brandon loudly repeated the gifting fan's ID name five times to help them gain followers.

"Male streamers really are more popular on our platform." He Yandan sees this as the biggest difference from some similar livestreaming platforms in China. Another difference, he believes, is that streamers like to "make friends" here. Mutual following is a culture among American video streamers — back in the YouTube era, streamers often followed each other and made guest appearances on each other's shows. After switching to livestreaming, streamers also frequently follow viewers who send them gifts.

Brandon is one of them. During his streams he writes down the accounts of gifting users in a notebook, and promises to follow them back after the stream ends. Although Brandon hasn't been streaming long and his fans have only just reached 5,301, with this "sincere" style of streaming he has already earned over 400,000 diamonds — roughly US$1,400.

This style of streaming is hardly rare on Live.me.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503013617tmrjCifJYUbSneZ5.jpg-w600)

But most foreign streamers rarely put on a headset and earnestly sing you song after song. Many of them, like Brandon, choose to stream by "making friends." More often, streamers just chat aimlessly with the users leaving comments in the livestream room, and some even drag their family and friends into the late-night room to shoot the breeze together.

Based on two weeks of data tracking major livestreaming platforms at home and abroad during two time windows — noon and late night — platforms represented by Live.me are dominated by chat-style livestreams, while China's Inke and YY have differentiated into various categories of livestreaming formats, with YY being the more diverse of the two — though Chinese streamers' personas are also more stereotyped.

### The two hottest livestreaming platforms in the U.S. right now both come from China

The platform Brandon and Felicia chose, Live.me, launched in the U.S. last April. Two months later, its download ranking broke into the top 50 of the overall U.S. app chart, and it currently hovers around 180. On the App Store revenue chart, Live.me also often sits in the top 50 — it has now even surpassed *Boom Beach*, a game from the well-known gaming company Supercell.

The other major livestreaming platform is Live.ly, which launched last May, with download and revenue rankings each a bit lower than Live.me's. Live.ly also comes from China — its office sits in an old building on Jumen Road in Shanghai's Huangpu district.

![Live.me's download ranking on iOS](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503013019ef7kUILADHq3ENCy.png-w600)

![Live.me's revenue ranking on iOS](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503013057TvO94gaHKewBLVQM.png-w600)

They brought with them a similar livestreaming platform interface and virtual gifts. Live.ly, however, has more talent- and interaction-based livestreaming formats.

On Wednesday night last week, Eric Artell tagged his Live.ly livestream with #StarWars. At 12:05 a.m. U.S. time, more than 10,000 viewers were still watching online. Eric had set a theme for the night: a *Star Wars* trivia quiz — he's a die-hard *Star Wars* fan.

Using Live.ly's remote-connect feature, a viewer named Venasa was patched into his livestream room. Eric greeted her in an excited voice and gave the first challenge question: "Who wrote the Star Wars theme music that's playing right now?"

Venasa was clearly stumped, and her tone turned uncertain. Viewers in the comment section had already rattled off the correct answer, and the screen was flooded with John Williams. Eric still gave Venasa the BB-10 toy he had prepared in advance, even though she hadn't managed to come up with the right answer.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170419221123IU5dvmrPA0CQJehg.png-w600)

Eric Artell is an American actor who now lives in Los Angeles. He once voiced the character Merry in a not-very-popular *Lord of the Rings* game series, and also hosted an American children's science education show, *DragonflyTV*. He originally debuted as a comedy star — even the ads he shot for Coca-Cola and Burger King were all positioned along a wacky, comedic line.

Having never made it big as an actor, Eric Artell tried his hand at every social media platform. On YouTube he and a friend made a show, *Rolf Review*, reviewing movies, actors, and even electronics — but after several years it still had only a few thousand subscribers. When Vine officially shut down, Eric posted a photo marked RIP, mourning the short-video platform that had helped him make so many friends and colleagues over the years.

![Eric Artell](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170420095425RjHXtmzD9nk6Ec5I.jpg-w600)

Compared with his old acting career, [Eric Artell](http://www.imdb.com/name/nm1364903/?ref%5C_=nmbio%5C_bio%5C_nm) is now, arguably, famous. Even a late-night interactive game show on Live.ly draws over ten thousand viewers. When the first debate between Hillary Clinton and Donald Trump was livestreamed on Facebook Live at the end of last year, peak concurrent viewership was only 180,000.

On Live.ly, a platform whose users are concentrated between 13 and 18 years old, the already 36-year-old Eric Artell counts as an "old master." But he knows the rules of streaming on this platform well — for example, using the Emoji Game to boost interaction: whoever sends the emoji matching the noun Eric calls out fastest wins Eric's BFF ("best friends forever" — simplified here to mutual account follows).

Live.ly has livestreaming features and virtual gifts similar to Live.me's, but everything on Live.ly is designed to be younger and more fun. The virtual gifts are no longer luxury cars and roses — they're pandas, two hugging chibi dinosaurs, and heart-launching gifts with a cute vibe. The most expensive gift costs 328 yuan, considerably cheaper than Live.me's castle, which goes for over 1,000 yuan.

All these differences point to the teen short-video platform behind Live.ly: Musical.ly. This is a video app even more popular in the U.S. — by July 2015 it already had more than 70 million users, with monthly actives around 55 million. Today, most Live.ly streamers are Musical.ly users.

Eric Artell was a Musical.ly user from early on — he recorded 463 short videos on the platform, mostly role-playing or funny skits. Many of Live.ly's top streamers, like Eric Artell, come mostly from Musical.ly. They recorded anywhere from several hundred to over a thousand 15-second short videos, building up sizable fan bases on Musical.ly.

### Many livestream influencers got their start in short video

Unlike Live.me, Live.ly's traffic comes mainly from the short-video app behind it, Musical.ly — nearly all of Live.ly's active users also make short videos on Musical.ly.

Musical.ly launched simultaneously in China and the U.S. in July 2014. It's a tool that lets users pick music from a library and record a 15-second short video — lip-syncing, dancing, whatever tests your creativity, though the recording process itself is simple. Biz Carson, a writer at the American tech blog [Business Insider](http://www.businessinsider.com/what-is-musically-2016-5), described it this way: unless you go deep into the teen crowd, you might never know this app exists; but if you have a high-schooler at home, you've probably appeared as human background scenery in one of their 15-second lip-sync videos.

![Musical.ly's interface](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170420095847WYNihMZEP5tv1n0d.png-w600)

From the early days of YouTube in the U.S. to today's Musical.ly, users with a personal flair and rallying power still have a chance to become grassroots influencers. Singer Justin Bieber originally got his break covering R&B songs on YouTube, caught Usher's eye, and debuted after signing with Island Records.

But nowadays it's much harder for talented ordinary people to get famous on YouTube. Many big institutional accounts have moved onto YouTube and are dividing up the traffic that used to go to these grassroots creators. Among [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLirAqAtl%5C_h2pATA79dIKsTKgfs6KAwWRs)'s top ten most-watched videos of 2015 and 2016, every single one was an official celebrity MV. Measured against such polished content, it's not easy for grassroots creators to squeeze into the front-page video recommendations.

Musical.ly has now taken over that role. Some of its users were once popular on Vine and YouTube, but what it attracts more are ordinary young people.

Here, it became a platform that can make grassroots influencers famous and gather teenagers together.

Ariel Martin, an ordinary 15-year-old middle-schooler, is a superstar born on Musical.ly. She has 18.5 million fans on Musical.ly, where she named her ID @BabyAriel.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170420095923WJo1p97qtG2Kmvxn.jpg-w600)

"Going viral was a complete accident," Martin says. During the summer vacation of 2015, Martin's home in Florida was hit by a major flood, and the whole family had to move in with her grandparents. In that bored, idle summer, Martin made her first lip-sync video on Musical.ly. She chose a passage from American female rapper Nicki Minaj — probably to fit the line repeated in the lyrics:

> I - I - I'm the shit (not wearing any makeup)
> With no make up (that's how bold I am)
> Don't have to curl my hair up (no need to curl my hair)
> All this booty here mine (all these booty girls are mine now)

In the video, Martin wore no makeup, let her curly golden hair down, and wore a white turtleneck T-shirt. After she posted several videos in a row on Musical.ly, her follower count started growing fast — within three months she had 2 million fans.

Of the more than 1,000 short videos she recorded, the most common backdrop is the purple floral bedsheets in Martin's bedroom. Every day, Martin says, preparing to record a single 15-second video takes her two to three hours — choosing the music, learning the lyrics, designing the right expressions and moves, and doing makeup. "I record it over and over, until I get a version I think is good."

Golden long hair, big eyes, extra-long painted nails, a tooth-baring smile, and exaggerated facial expressions — those are pretty much the signature of her videos. Most of the time, 15-year-old Martin looks far more mature than her age. In her videos, her hairstyle, lipstick, hair color, and earrings can all become hot topics of discussion among fans in the comments.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704200959433MF1LTXnkOhVPjef.jpeg-w600)

After going viral, Baby Ariel became part of Musical.ly's star-making program. Musical.ly held outdoor tours in 20 American cities, bringing the platform's influencers offline to perform and sell tickets. Riding on her influence on Musical.ly, Martin's YouTube channel also attracted 2 million followers, and she appeared on *Good Morning America* — gradually going from an influencer on a single platform to a more mainstream star.

Even before Musical.ly took off, there were similar lip-sync apps — Xiaokaxiu in China, and Dubsmash, the "German Xiaokaxiu" that caught on among celebrities abroad. Beyond porting the fun features over, Musical.ly put more effort into community operations. One of its themes, "Don't Judge Me," became a hot internet topic for a while: users would first make themselves look ugly in a video, then with a wave of the hand reveal their real appearance, to express the attitude of "don't judge me by my looks."

The download curves of Musical.ly and Live.ly swing dramatically with the weekly cycle — downloads surge on the two weekend days and immediately fall back on workdays. Behind this is a team that puts a heavy emphasis on operations.

![Musical.ly's download ranking on iOS](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503012557S5RaycXnx7kQipdW.png-w600)

![Live.ly's download ranking on iOS](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705030126538NYBg21rZVzfWSMb.png-w600)

Musical.ly's active users like to call themselves "Musers," and most of them are 15- or 16-year-old teenagers. Every weekend, everyone puts on T-shirts printed with the Musical.ly logo and gathers for irregular offline meetups. Influencers like Martin join in too, interacting with fans during the officially organized tours. Offline events are simultaneously livestreamed on Live.ly, placed in the most prominent spot.

### What Chinese companies brought over includes, of course, inflated data

When we first livestreamed on Live.me, we gained 80 fans within five minutes — and even though the livestream showed nothing at all (a black screen), those viewers stayed loyally with us.

Live.ly doesn't have that many users "interested" in a black screen, but it never lacks for fans. From the moment you register, followers come naturally even if you never post a video. All these fans' profile pages are remarkably uniform: no self-introduction, no posted works whatsoever.

A feeling all too familiar from livestreaming platforms back in China.

When it comes to monetization on livestreaming platforms, the virtual gift tipping of Chinese "showroom" livestreaming offers one path. Although most Chinese showroom livestreams are highly homogenized, some companies have still made money this way — YY, the earliest to do it, went public.

In its financial reports, Momo also called its livestream and short-video business "the biggest engine of continued revenue and profit growth."

Now they've gone to America.

### The livestreaming revolution Silicon Valley envisioned never came — what came was a time-killing showroom

As in China, livestreaming is nothing new in America.

Twitch, which built its business on game streaming, is already six years old and was sold to Amazon for US$1 billion in 2014. But Twitch lives mainly on PC.

America's mobile livestreaming war began in 2015. Meerkat went viral at the beginning of 2015 at the South by Southwest conference, but within a few months, Twitter-incubated Periscope stole its thunder.

All these livestreaming apps aimed to get everyone involved, to share what they were experiencing with others. Naturally, media outlets loved that sense of immediacy. Jimmy Fallon used it to livestream rehearsals of his late-night show. The host of America's morning news and talk show *Today* used it to take viewers behind the scenes.

But things didn't develop the way Silicon Valley entrepreneurs and the media had imagined. Meerkat has already been declared a failure, and Periscope is eerily deserted.

Owen Benjamin is an independent musician who has been holding piano recitals around the U.S. for years and has 635,000 followers on Twitter, but his piano-and-singing livestreams on Periscope attract at most one to two thousand viewers at a time. Ordinary users' livestreams on Periscope mostly draw around 50 viewers — it hasn't learned to inflate its numbers yet.

The biggest difference between Live.me, Live.ly, and these products is that they're not trying to get everyone involved — they attract professional streamers by giving them a chance to make money.

Among the companies that went all-in on livestreaming and failed, the biggest is undoubtedly Facebook. Mark Zuckerberg's social network now has 1.9 billion users worldwide. At Facebook's developer conference a year ago, Zuckerberg launched Facebook Live, and he gave an example of watching a livestream himself:

"I saw a mom on the livestream platform skiing down a snowy mountain with her child, and the scene was mesmerizing. I stared at the screen for several minutes, because I was curious whether that child would fall the next second." When Facebook had just launched livestreaming, Zuckerberg [envisioned it becoming](https://developers.facebook.com/videos/f8-2016/keynote/) a place where family members share their lives and record what is real.

In June 2016, Facebook reached partnerships with over a hundred partners — including mainstream media companies such as CNN, the New York Times, Vox, and Tastemade, plus more than a dozen celebrities — to produce livestreamed programming. Facebook's livestreaming was also packaged as a key tool for declaring war: Hillary Clinton's team once used Facebook for a livestream program four hours long — reading aloud the 5,500 lawsuits involving Trump.

In July 2016, a Black man in Minnesota was shot and killed by police during a traffic stop, and his fiancée, Diamond Reynolds, who was sitting beside him, livestreamed on Facebook Live the process from the shooting to his death.

Livestreaming pushed the conflict between police and Black people directly in front of the camera, with no media outlet filtering whether such content could be broadcast. For a time, the discussion began over whether Facebook Live would change news dissemination.

But livestreaming for everyone never caught on. What made Facebook Live famous was all big news.

In mid-April this year, a middle-aged man uploaded a video of himself shooting and killing an elderly man in his eighties during a livestream to Facebook Live. The bloody, violent video wasn't removed until two hours later.

Every time a bloody image appeared, Facebook Live got a huge amount of attention. In the year since its birth, people's eagerness to search for it has all been tied to violent incidents:

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503065240E8d1HvzCYSkeb5wP.png-w600)

But after each jolt, that attention vanished. None of these shocks turned it into an everyday part of people's lives.

At this April's Facebook developer conference, Zuckerberg was already notably cold on livestreaming, saying only that he would pay attention to filtering out inappropriate content.

From small startups to Twitter and Facebook, Silicon Valley's effort to use livestreaming video to change news and the way people transmit information did not succeed. What ended up catching fire is the same as in China: using boredom to defeat boredom.
