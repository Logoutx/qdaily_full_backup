My motivation to learn has always come from the awareness that I am a person in this universe with no ground under my feet. Not knowing life, not knowing death — it forces you to dig deeper into things, to observe more closely.

On October 23, *The Actors' Lab* 《演员实验教室》, directed by Jin Shijie, was performed at the Wuzhen Theatre Festival. The play was first staged in Taiwan 34 years ago, with actors from the Lanling Theatre Workshop telling their own life stories on stage. This production is a revival marking the workshop's 40th anniversary, again performed by the original cast, with the content of their stories updated.

Barefoot and dressed in everyday clothes, the actors told their most private memories and feelings on stage. After the performances, *The Actors' Lab* received 14 reviews on Douban, many of them peppered with exclamation marks: "What have I done to deserve this luck!" "The best show at the Wuzhen Theatre Festival!" "Wonderful, wonderful!" Every single review gave it five stars.

![After the two Wuzhen performances of *The Actors' Lab*, its Douban rating reached 9.9](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026223853roZc1wltM8haSeV3.png-w600)

In early October, not long before, the "drama-opera musical" *Yang Yuelou* 《杨月楼》, in which Jin Shijie performed, premiered. *Yang Yuelou* was written and directed by Peking opera actor Li Baochun. It tells a bizarre romance set in the late Qing dynasty, mixing Peking opera, spoken drama and musical theater — and even Rap.

*Yang Yuelou* played three shows in Beijing to poor reviews. As one audience member commented: ["The fusion of so many art forms feels forced, the script is drawn out, and the final product has the feel of a gala evening show. The only two saving graces are probably the opera singing and stage movement, and Jinbao's performance."](https://www.douban.com/location/drama/30321251/)

"Jinbao" is the nickname some friends and audiences use for Jin Shijie. It comes from the novel *Gimpel the Fool* by the American Jewish writer Isaac Bashevis Singer. Its protagonist is a man who, out of a kind-hearted belief in others, willingly goes along with what people tell him. Jin Shijie likes his attitude toward life.

As with these two plays, from the 1980s to the present, Jin Shijie has performed in just about every genre. His stage work includes *Secret Love in Peach Blossom Land*, *Tonight, Who Does Crosstalk?* 《这一夜，谁来说相声》, *Me and I, Him and Him* 《我和我和他和他》, *The Red Sky* 《红色的天空》, *The Last Fourteen Tuesday Sessions* 《最后十四堂星期二的课》... His films include *The Terrorizers*, *A Brighter Summer Day*, *The Grandmaster*, *Brotherhood of Blades*, *Detective Chinatown*, *The Final Master*, *See You Tomorrow*, *The Last Women Standing*... His TV work includes *Princess Agents*, *Surgeons*, *Midnight Diner*, *In Time with You*... He has also done voice work for documentaries and animations — for example *The Rocking Sky*, a documentary about the air force of the Republic of China during the War of Resistance, of which Jin Shijie's father was a member. And the "Kung Fu Master" in *Kung Fu Panda*, "Lord Jian" in *The Guardian*, and the "Spirit Granny" in *Big Fish & Begonia*.

![Jin Shijie in Edward Yang's film *The Terrorizers*, playing Shen Weibin, the heroine's old lover](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026224832aU8DwVO6HbvWrCLu.jpg-w600)

Among these films, shows and plays there are absolute classics and plenty of lousy productions. Jin Shijie mostly turned in creditable performances in them; in the bad productions, his acting could even seem jarring because it was too concrete, too solid. He has never won a major acting award, but audiences widely recognize his craft.

Asked about appearing in bad films, Jin Shijie's response was: "On a single boat it's hard to sail to a good place so that everyone benefits. You can do your own part well, but the boat doesn't sail anywhere better." "Someone who comes out of the theater is used to working as a group. If they're bad, everyone is bad together. So that somewhat answers what you just asked — I don't think about whether I'm good, but about how hard this boat is to steer."

![Jin Shijie in the film *Detective Chinatown*, playing "Big Shot" Boss Yan](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026225132LVCzI3tqKkWv9jFb.jpg-w600)

He puts the emphasis on "whether my own performance was done well."

Jin Shijie was born in 1951 in Pingtung, Taiwan, and grew up in a military dependents' village. After high school, he skipped the joint college entrance exam and chose to study veterinary science at an agricultural college. "Exam, university, a high-paying job in a big city" — that was not what he pursued; it was the social model he wanted to rebel against. Society demands that people do certain things, he said, but people don't always submit to such demands. He hated putting the cart before the horse, and he held his own fixed sense of which was root and which was branch. He wanted to study "freely and happily."

After graduating from the veterinary program at Pingtung Agricultural College, he spent a year and a half raising pigs, then decided to go to Taipei to do something related to telling stories. In Taipei he worked as a mover and a warehouse keeper, and loved it — because he could support himself and had plenty of free time to read and write. He also liked the picture of making money through physical toil and sweat.

In 1980 Jin Shijie co-founded the Lanling Theatre Workshop, regarded as Taiwan's first experimental theater. Its members included Lee Li-chun, Gu Boming, Liu Ruoyu, Lee Kuo-hsiu, Wu Jingji and Li Tianzhu — figures who would later matter greatly in Taiwan's theater world.

Jin Shijie wrote and directed *Hezhu's New Match* 《荷珠新配》. The bar girl Hezhu gets, from a rich merchant's chauffeur who leaks the secret out of vanity, a chance to pose as the merchant's long-lost daughter. She schemes and plots with all her might; but once she is finally reunited with the merchant, he goes bankrupt and everything comes to nothing. The play's dialogue is full of wit, but the stage design was very crude. When the wealthy merchant Qi Zixiao enters with his wife, the two "arrive in a Mercedes-Benz" — signified by a flag painted with the Mercedes emblem, carried around the stage. Jin Shijie himself said the audience laughed so hard they nearly took the roof off.

For years afterward, answering other questions, Jin Shijie would still say this: he wanted "a plain stage — I have nothing, I just come out and perform, and I can hold you rapt; when you go home just thinking about the play makes you die of happiness."

*Hezhu's New Match* was staged in 1980 and took part in Taiwan's first experimental theater festival. It became a sensation and was praised as "setting a wildfire in Taiwan's barren theater scene," while the Lanling Theatre Workshop came to be seen as the enlightenment of Taiwan's modern theater.

![A group photo from the days of *Hezhu's New Match*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026232359B0Kp6Xo4I9wTf3bh.jpeg-w600)

The workshop then operated for about 10 years. In Taiwan, the Lanling Theatre Workshop directly or indirectly gave birth to many theater troupes, including the Ping-Fong Acting Troupe, U-Theatre, the Performance Workshop, the Paper Windmill Theatre, the If Kids Theatre and others. Among them, the Performance Workshop, founded by Stan Lai in 1984, had Jin Shijie performing in many of its plays.

One of the plays Jin Shijie still performs in, *The Last Fourteen Tuesday Sessions*, is adapted from the autobiographical novel by the American writer Mitch Albom, and he has been playing it since 2011. It is a bestselling novel that is pure chicken soup for the soul. Jin Shijie plays a professor stricken with ALS, who keeps meeting and chatting with his student throughout the play. As the story goes on, his illness grows worse and worse, until he is on the verge of death.

Jin Shijie has talked about why he took this play. At first he didn't want to. But after reading the adapted script he decided to do it, because what he read in it "was two people. People appeared. A teacher, a student. These two seem to be on a journey through the entire play — each goes through his own stretch of the journey, and when they collide, sparks fly. As the story turns page after page, their outlines grow clearer. I feel that between the two of them they even form something close to a wrestling match."

Although *Hezhu's New Match* back then satirized the habit of everyone wanting to become an overnight tycoon, it was more Jin Shijie speaking from feeling than a deliberate satire of reality. He is more interested in the relationships around people.

Because of the material deprivation of his military-village childhood, Jin Shijie likes "being poor." He wears old clothes, uses old things, eats leftovers, lives in a small house. Taiwanese TV host Chang Hsiao-yen saw Jin Shijie's plays back in the Lanling days. She says that when she first knew him, he carried a schoolbag and rode a bicycle, creak-creak, and ate one steamed bun a day.

Friends also often tell the story of him going to the writer Li Ang's home to eat leftovers. Having discovered that Li Ang's household had leftovers from every meal, Jin Shijie made a point of going to her house to eat them. He would first phone to confirm: could he come at this time? Were there leftovers? When he came, no one was allowed to keep him company at the table, and no extra dishes could be added because of him. So every time Jin Shijie visited, Li Ang would hide far away, not daring to speak, or the rules would be broken.

![Taiwanese photographer Hsieh Chun-te published the photo collection *Faces of Our Time* 《时代的脸》 in 1986; the cover was this portrait he took of Jin Shijie](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026232853CoBhWn4QZVzkKbuO.jpeg-w600)

On the variety show *Kang Xi Lai Le*, he also told another story: back then he liked packing up everyone's unfinished boxed lunches. Once, after packing them up and getting home, he found one was missing; when he went back and found it by the roadside, a dog had nearly gotten to it first. He shooed the dog away and carried the lunch box home again.

For a time Jin Shijie insisted on no marriage, no children, no car, no telephone. Once, director Chen Kuo-fu happened to see Jin Shijie riding his bicycle down the street and said to him: "I think that speed of yours is too proud." Chang Hsiao-yen, who had acted in plays with him, feared he was too lonely; she would urge friends to leave more messages on Jin Shijie's answering machine.

The 1988 stage play *Tomorrow We'll Meet Again in the Air* 《明天我们空中再见》, written by Jin Shijie and staged in Taiwan, tells the love story of a female radio announcer, and was beloved by film director Edward Yang. The play documented "his speed."

Zhong Mingde, professor in the theater department of Taipei National University of the Arts, described the play this way: "*Tomorrow We'll Meet Again in the Air* is a mood of 1980s Taiwan... Taiwan was really buzzing back then: street movements, street racing, postmodernism, neo-Marxism, the Dajiale lottery craze, the real estate surge, and 'Taiwan's money flooding up to your ankles' (rather like Shanghai today?) — amid such a swaggering Taiwanese economic and democratic miracle, *Tomorrow We'll Meet Again in the Air* stood out as lonely, young, taut, fragile — yet innocent and earnest to the bone."

It wasn't until 2009. Only after marrying did he buy a bigger house for his wife and child, get a driver's license, and start using a feature phone.

Looking back on his changes, there was a turning point when he was nearly 40. Jin Shijie was riding a roller coaster in the United States; at the moment of the steep plunge, people's screams couldn't be heard — or rather, the sound fell behind. He was shocked to realize the difference between the outside world's speed and his own. He wondered: living all along at his own speed, had he been a bit of a fool?

In 2015 Jin Shijie took part in the documentary *Where My Family Came From* 《客从何处来》, in which he went searching for his family's origins.

Talking about how it felt to film the documentary, he said: it's as if I boarded a time machine, letting time flow backward, and stood once more beside my young parents. Watching every movement they made back then, every figure, the sweat they shed, the air they smelled, the wind they felt, the sun they sat in. He said this struck deep into his heart.

When *Where My Family Came From* released its trailer, it put out a photo: Jin Shijie standing beside a few trees, wearing a white shirt, dark blue trousers, with a bag slung across his chest.

At the *Yang Yuelou* press conference, he wore a dark, sporty, very old-fashioned outfit — also with a cloth bag slung across his chest.

The two outfits were not quite the same, but the feeling they gave was very similar.

In fact, both of those moments already fell within the period when he had begun to change his former pace of life and took on many film and TV roles to support his family. But comparing Jin Shijie at these moments with photos of him from the Lanling days, the feeling is still very much alike.

As host Chang Hsiao-yen exclaimed when she saw him on a show: "Jin Shijie hasn't changed from the Lanling days until now — except that he secretly got married somewhere in between."

When Jin Shijie is interviewed and answering questions, he will temporarily put aside his concern about being interrupted and wants to finish a passage completely. In this final interview the session was extended again and again; the day's schedule was tight and Jin Shijie was anxious to get to rehearsal, already showing a slight fidgetiness — but he still sat there answering questions as requested, looking both very willing to hold to himself and very willing to accommodate others.

**Q: QDaily****Jin Shijie: theater director, playwright, actor**

**Q: Why did you want to take on a show like this (*Yang Yuelou*)? You yourself have called this show "a strange boat." I see you acting in all kinds of TV dramas, films and stage plays, collaborating with all kinds of people. I'm quite curious: in your case, how does this possibility of collaboration come about?**

Jin Shijie: I started out in the theater — how to innovate? How to subvert the system? How to fight tradition? Those were the things we especially pursued when we were young. But theaters come in all types: there are commercial theaters, popular theaters. We all studied a great deal of coursework and rehearsed many plays. So I feel that at the beginning my range of contact was very wide, and later I became quite OK with, quite familiar with every type. That has always been true of me. The very vulgar, the very refined, the very highbrow, the very popular — all OK.

**Q: Back then (at 23) when you went to Taipei, your original intention was to tell stories. Acting and directing feel different. How do you look at that original intention now?**

Jin Shijie: That intention shows I wasn't very conscious of having to do one particular thing. I never told myself I had to become an actor, a playwright or a director — I wasn't even sure about theater.

I could write novels, I could write songs, I could sell beef noodle soup — all possible. I wanted to be a storyteller of some kind — run a teahouse, beat a gong, beat a drum, clap the clappers, tell stories, or whatever — or it could be very idle chatting. Maybe I open a bar, or a coffee shop, or maybe I open a children's playground.

I never thought about the form. Meaning the person now sitting in front of you is a famous actor, a famous director. Even accepting that label, I feel hollow inside. It's just a title — what's director or not director, actor or not actor.

In fact I've always been standing at that starting point, and that point is storyteller. Like the stories I experienced in my childhood, or the ones I heard from my older brothers and sisters, or Zhu Ziqing's essays — it's that. As for which form it takes, I don't care.

**Q: Is it a worldview?**

Jin Shijie: It is the most essential self-awareness. I am always in service of that thing. So suddenly serving the acting profession is not changing careers. It's more that one's practical life gets disrupted. Professionally, I just strive to do that thing — Dreammaker, Storyteller, either works.

**Q: If I asked you where your motivation to learn comes from now, would it overlap with what you just said?**

Jin Shijie: My motivation to learn has always come from the awareness that I am a person in this universe with no ground under my feet. Not knowing life, not knowing death — it forces you to dig deeper into things, to observe more closely.

Now let me confess that life has gained one more helper: my children. I sometimes look at the world through their eyes, and because of them I find the motivation to live the next second. So some of the joy of learning also comes from their joy.

Because they occupy such an important place in my life: when I watch the news on TV, the smallest trivial thing makes me tense. Someone shouting loudly on the street — I immediately think, what if my children saw a scene like that? There are many things in this world — fun ones, frightening ones, topics, events — that set off nerves in me.

**Q: A friend of mine wants to know how your acting system was built. It's an interesting question — even for very good actors, questions about an acting system don't always apply, but I feel it's all right to ask you.**

Jin Shijie: Why do you think I'm more suited to this topic?

**Q: Your acting feels like a very concrete thing — I don't know how to describe it. If the notion of an "acting system" holds up, are you aware of your "acting system"? How was it built?**

Jin Shijie: Hmm. My answer is: I make no comparisons. Actually, having come all this way, I feel acting is... different from some people's. For instance, some people hold up Stanislavsky... Stanislavsky's existence and structure influenced many people in many places. And by extension, those things spread out like ripples; once they bloom on their own, the questions and opportunities they bring you connect to other questions.

So many teachers at drama academies fly the old master's banner, but what they teach are different branches and twigs — all possible. Many people in many countries on this earth have different acting pursuits, more or less influenced by the old master. They each build their own, fused with the local culture, or abruptly dream up some other system of their own. It isn't entirely unrelated to Mr. Stanislavsky, but it is also their own system.

What is my own system? Well, before I formally became an actor, I was already certain that this person's job was actor — and the actor I mean is not the actor you all know now. I call human behavior, human language, all of a person's outward phenomena, everything you see — performance.

People are all plagiarists; almost nothing is original — everything. Including your face, your expressions. Some things come from the refraction of genes; some come from the nurture of the environment; some come from imitation, unknowingly infected by others, this and that and that. From a very young age I felt that this thing called "actor" was a matter of enormous interest that made my appetite wide open, something I very much loved to think about.

I often experience whether people's behavior is real or fake. Including behavior and mannerisms — I often feel that a person is an actor, rather than an actor being an actor. Of these two propositions, I belong to the former. I just happen to also do the acting work that makes money, that puts food on the table. My greater ambition is: without this job, when I go home and face the sky and face the earth, in my heart I feel I am an actor.

If we're talking about a system, I should answer you this way. In all of this, I develop a pursuit of my own, an attitude of my own.

Now, beyond your question, we can add a topic: before Lanling, we had invited an actor named Wu Jingji — the Lanling Theatre Workshop's instructor.

He had just returned to Taiwan from a visit to the La MaMa theater in New York. He himself had a PhD in psychology, and he fused psychology with the La MaMa performance style in the activities he taught us. For a year and a half we didn't come out of a room — we just kept training, and never performed.

We were very young then, and the theme of all our training was Relax. Relaxation. The first step of our relaxation course was called Massage, and we did Massage for a long time. Saying it this way is a bit deliberately playful, but truly — the physical relationships between people, including how to roll, how to compose, how to run — he fused all of that with psychology.

He pursued Relaxation all along. Relaxation is, to this day for me, a course that has never ended; often after the course I feel as if the whole thing was just playing games. Does it have anything to do with acting? No.

For a long, long time we wondered: what on earth is this course doing? We're not patients at a psychiatric sanatorium. (Q: What you're saying...) So these training directions had a big influence on me, and I myself loved this pursuit very much. That is: enabling a person to let go of everything that can be let go — all the martial law, all the weaponry. Being unable to help oneself, shyness, the sense of freedom — let go, let go, let go — and what comes in is something like a playful child, wide-eyed with curiosity.

This is what I myself pursue more. I feel that every time I read a script, or watch a performance, those curious-filled eyes seem to be on me — what are they doing? How can it be like this?

Like a child's first visit to the circus — ooooh — eh?! — ah?! Toward the theater, toward acting, toward those incredible, fascinating performances, my mood is often just like that. Facing a role — I want to play this role — oooh. Some mischief wants to come out; some reflection; some being faithful to everything time prescribes; and some villainous voices chiming in.

I haven't said it clearly enough, but I've tried my best to say it clearly.

**Q: You said acting is like play for you — you should be the one paying, yet instead others pay you. In that process, has this sense of satisfaction ever been lost?**

Jin Shijie: When you run into a lousy script, a lousy troupe, a lousy show, you just muddle through with one eye open and one eye shut. In a more interesting performing state, I would explain it with that sentence.

**Q: You like the attitude toward life of "Gimpel" in Isaac Bashevis Singer's novel *Gimpel the Fool*: "dealing with suffering all your life, even treating self-abuse as a kind of pride to play with." Many interviews mention that you take this as your own attitude toward life. Why is that?**

Jin Shijie: Why?

**Q: I don't quite understand what that sentence means in your case.**

Jin Shijie: *Gimpel the Fool*, in short: a man who wears the green hat — his wife cheats on him constantly, sleeps around on him constantly, lies to him constantly — and he believes her. Oh, oh, oh, he thinks it's nothing. Later he dies and goes to hell, where he meets his wife. His wife is still sleeping around. And he believes her again. Oh, oh, oh.

Reading this, I thought: what kind of person would write such a story? What kind of people would write such a story? The answer: the Jews. Truly, only an old people would write such a story. Too deep. That sense of suffering is too strong. That depth, and the relationship with suffering, and the jesting found under suffering and repression, the capacity to laugh at oneself — that's what I like, what I admire.

Because why is he still laughing then? Tell him a lie and he believes it — what he saw just now was mistaken; what just jumped out the window wasn't a man, it was a dog. Any excuse works. It's a bit boring, not a very funny joke. He writes it so meanly, so low. I find a laugh-or-cry quality in it, and something behind that attracts me. What kind of person writes such a story.

**Q: You said something behind it attracts you. You also often mention pessimism. Where does this pessimism of yours come from?**

**Because you are also nostalgic — is nostalgia a sign of feeling safe about the past, or of having reached a very happy state before, that makes one nostalgic? Then I don't quite know how your pessimism grows out of this?**

Jin Shijie: Where the pessimism comes from, I can't quite recall. I've been pessimistic since childhood. Maybe timid, cowardly, easily frightened. Things like planning for a rainy day, being full of anxious sentiment... my body just can't quite take in the sunlight — a coward, a weak character. Fear — easily afraid. I guess it's related to that.

Plus, from childhood, going with my parents, with my mother, to the Christian church and reading those stories — life and death, talk of heaven and hell — it may make you prone to brooding. A child already can't quite figure these things out; thinking and thinking, the gloom sets in. Hee — yo?! Heaven, hell, death... ahem ahem ahem.

**Q: Regarding the relationship between nostalgia and security you just mentioned — do you agree with it?**

Jin Shijie: Yes, I have a nostalgic bent. But the crux is not the old; it's time.

I liked wearing old clothes at New Year that my father gave me. I never liked new clothes since I was small; I like old clothes. I feel old clothes carry the smell of a person, new clothes... There's a "long live the poor" feeling. I think it's the influence of my mother's thrifty habits. Things must be used to their fullest; nothing may be wasted; the tap must be turned off tight; the lights must be switched off when going out. (laughs)

**Q: Many of your film and TV performances left a deep impression on people; people even clip out passages of your acting to pass around.**

**As an audience member, I sometimes have this feeling: seeing certain clips of yours can instead make me feel a bit disillusioned.**

**Because the other characters, or the whole web of relationships in that film or show, is not something I can believe in — then suddenly a very real performance of yours comes out, and I instead feel you're lying to the audience.****As an actor, do you also have this sense of imbalance?**

Jin Shijie: You mean it acts too fake, right?

Q: No — it's because you act too real, but everything around you is too fake. More bluntly put: the film just isn't very good.

Jin Shijie: That one I can't answer. I can only be responsible for my own performance; I can't be responsible for the whole. This... this... I'm rather without a position to answer this.

Q: But does it bring you a sense of loss?

Jin Shijie: On a single boat it's hard to sail to a good place so that everyone benefits. You can do your own part well, but the boat doesn't sail anywhere better. The captain of this boat doesn't have the ability...

On a film set I'm often a tiny, tiny, tiny bit more talkative than some people. I often find I have to be very careful not to overstep my authority. Not to exceed, not to disrespect others. But often the moment I perform — huh? How come I can't hide it?

Someone who comes out of the theater is used to working as a group. If they're bad, everyone is bad together. So that somewhat answers what you just asked — I don't think about whether I'm good, but about how hard this boat is to steer.

**Q: Have you been pondering any questions lately? Or questions you've thought about for a long time.**

Jin Shijie: The question I've been thinking about lately is: how do I talk with my child when he's ten years older. My child is 7 now, and I'm a fairly old father, so of course I worry about the finitude of life. I think: wow, I'll be even older then — how will we talk? We'll chat about things past and present, Chinese and foreign, about love, about careers, about whatever — what meaning can I create?

**Q: As a storyteller, some people want to present the problems in society and solve them. Their creative drive comes from that. Do you belong to this category?**

Jin Shijie: Not really. In my relation to reality there's always something of a detachment. When I wrote stories in my youth, I so much liked to avoid mentioning that we're on Chaoyang Avenue, that I'm in Beijing, that I'm on such-and-such a street in Taipei. Anything related to reality — even political issues, even economic issues, current affairs, politics — I tend to blur. I'm used to that.

On this point I'm different from Stan Lai with his crosstalk material. That crosstalk genre by nature carries a satirical literature; he is bound to touch the current state of things — how Taiwan's elections go, how Taiwan's historical baggage goes, how Taipei was in such-and-such a decade. He would create in that direction. In my choice of subject matter I usually steer away from those topics. Yes — my answer to this question is that I keep a certain distance.

**Q: In real life, in terms of your own thinking, do you have issues you worry about?**

Jin Shijie: The issues I worry about aren't particularly absolute; they mostly belong to relationships — relationships between people, your relationship with yourself, with your family, with society, or the story of some twisted personality. I'm more interested in those things.
