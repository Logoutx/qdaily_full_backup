Wang Xuan signed the lease renewal without much hesitation.

The monthly rent was 1,760 yuan, plus a service fee of 1,183 yuan, payable quarterly. Wang Xuan paid 6,463 yuan in one go through the Ziroom app — roughly one month's take-home pay at the time.

She deliberated for a week before tapping pay. The Spring Festival had just passed, and Beijing rents were climbing fast in early March. She checked prices nearby — also in Tongzhou: "Ten square meters, far from the subway, and they dare to ask 2,500, wishing for 3,000."

"(Renewing) was purely about the price — it actually didn't go up." By renewing a month early, she also got the service fee cut to 56% of its usual rate, saving Wang Xuan 1,000 yuan all told.

Wang Xuan is 26, fluent in Spanish and mad about *League of Legends*. She has changed jobs three times in Beijing and currently works in marketing and public relations at a Japanese-owned company. Like most of the [7.943 million Beijing drifters](http://www.bjstats.gov.cn/bwtt/201802/t20180227_393406.html), her basic unit of solo living is a bedroom of under 20 square meters — or, in her words, "the biggest variable in my cost of living."

The anxiety brought by that variable peaked at the end of 2017. Her job at a public relations agency wore her down — "a terrible temper" — and she had also just broken up with the boyfriend she had been with for five years, a breakup on the eve of marriage.

The apartment made everything about the future more uncertain, because this was a partition room.

A partition room is a common rental form in first-tier cities: a landlord or agent carves an extra single room out of an entire apartment to squeeze in more tenants. It is also called "N+1"; Ziroom markets it as an "optimized room."

According to Centaline Property's 2015 tally, [over 60%](http://cd.house.ifeng.com/detail/2015_11_20/50627263_0.shtml) of commercial housing on sale in Beijing was three-bedroom units or larger. Beijing's property market is oriented toward sales, which is unfriendly to shared renting: living rooms are large and underutilized, yet every tenant has to share the cost.

N+1 has never been legal in Beijing. In November 2017, as part of a campaign to clear out and rectify non-compliant housing, Beijing's districts and counties [cleaned up and rectified](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/47749.html) rooms rented out as partitions on platforms like Ziroom, and some rooms were forcibly demolished. Ziroom CEO Xiong Lin came forward to respond, offering compensation to tenants forced to move out.

The apartment Wang Xuan lives in has four bedrooms, and hers is the only "illegal" one, converted from the living room — a textbook partition. As the storm broke, her Ziroom house manager told her not to open the door for anyone of unknown provenance coming to inspect the room, which meant she could be thrown out at any moment.

Wang Xuan pulled out her suitcase and packed some of her belongings, ready to leave at any time. Fortunately, no one had swung a hammer through her walls by the time she renewed in March.

The partition storm seems to have passed, but still no one can tell Wang Xuan whether her little room might be demolished. So she keeps living there on borrowed hope: "The two words 'partition' mean you can never fully set your mind at ease."

By now, what worries Wang Xuan more is not the partition but a rent hike. In the eight months or so since she renewed, everything in Beijing has gotten more expensive.

A UBS report shows that, counting rent, Beijing is the [fourth most expensive city in Asia to live in](http://cd.house.ifeng.com/detail/2015_11_20/50627263_0.shtml), with Shanghai close behind. The two cities look about the same, but when UBS released the report three years ago, monthly rent for a furnished two-bedroom in Beijing and Shanghai was about US$1,150 and US$1,120 — both roughly a quarter of what the most expensive city for rent at the time, New York, charged. By 2018, however, Beijing's average rent had pulled well clear of Shanghai's, reaching US$1,523 a month — about 1.7 times Shanghai's.

A supply shortfall drove rents up. [Data from the China Real Estate Association](http://www.creprice.cn/city/bj.html?type=lease) shows that after the Daxing fire at the end of last year, the number of rental units available across Beijing fell 56% from November 2017 to February 2018. As of November, renting in Beijing cost 93.14 yuan per square meter per month, 20 yuan more than in November 2017.

After the interview, Wang Xuan half-joked on WeChat: "Ziroom isn't going to pinpoint me for a rent hike, right?" — followed by a "hahaha" and a wry-faced Pikachu sticker.

By the end of 2017, Beijing's officially published resident population stood at [21.707 million](http://www.chinanews.com/gn/2018/01-19/8428594.shtml), down 22,000 from the end of the previous year — the first negative growth in Beijing's resident population since 2000.

Wang Xuan is not one of them yet, but leaving Beijing seems to have become a conviction: "It's an absolute certainty. Beijing is not a place for me — I won't sink my entire net worth into an apartment here."

We recorded our conversation with her as an oral account — about work, about love, about young people's Beijing:

### From Chengdu to Beijing: I Live in the "Illegal" Room

I graduated in 2015 and worked as a management trainee at a hotel in Chengdu, earning very little. A senior colleague told me it was rather pathetic for me to stay in hotels. It's called "management trainee," but really it's a glorified waiter.

A classic example: you're carrying a tray of drinks toward a guest and the drinks spill. Your first reaction is supposed to be to tip the tray onto yourself and ask the other person, "Are you all right?" — while you're the one drenched head to toe. Truly pathetic.

That senior colleague had studied overseas, and he said, "Why don't you try the PR industry? I can recommend you, but the job is in Beijing." My boyfriend was already in Beijing, so he was one factor in my move, but the main reason was my career switch.

I worked in Chengdu for eight months and came to Beijing during the May Day holiday in 2016. The first place I lived in was a two-bedroom shared with my ex-boyfriend and one of his classmates, so I basically only had to pay about 1,000 yuan.

Our original lease was up on April 20, but I had to travel to the Shanghai auto show on April 19. On April 16 a Ziroom house manager took me to see the apartment. I said there was nothing to nitpick or haggle over — I'd sign on the spot. The manager also showed me a backup option, a "legitimate" bedroom with a small balcony.

I grabbed the lease on the 17th, and on the 19th I headed to the Shanghai auto show with my suitcase. After the trip I had a moving company haul my things over. It was a tight squeeze.

We had a three-bedroom-plus-living-room apartment, and I was "that living room" — the place had been turned into four bedrooms. Once you came through the front door there was basically no space left, just a corridor. The master bedroom had its own bathroom and was occupied by a woman, plus two secondary bedrooms.

Their three were legal rooms; mine was the "illegal" one. What made me happy was that the subway was right outside the complex — extremely, extremely convenient.

![Beijing CBD. Image from Pixabay](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181111173055OmlpsAbEHW4jdqTI.jpg-w600)

My roommates and I got along wonderfully. The girl in the master bedroom worked near Dongsi Shitiao and I worked near Liangmaqiao, so we'd leave at the same time every morning — 8:30 a.m. — and ride the Batong Line together, egging each other on to get up early. The guy in the other room was very warm-hearted too, often rallying everyone to eat together.

I was pretty satisfied. Living along the Batong Line is quite remote, but it's still better than a ten-minute walk after getting off the subway — it's like riding two extra stops.

My roommates made me feel that at least when I got home there was someone to talk to, and I didn't have to trudge through wind and rain outside for long. That's what the apartment gave me, even though it was a partition.

### The Two Words "Partition" Mean You Can Never Fully Set Your Mind at Ease

When the partitions were being demolished, I remember it coincided with the RYB scandal. All I knew about the Daxing fire was that a lot of people had died; I never thought it had anything to do with me.

One noon, while I was at work, a friend sent me the article that had first gone viral on Moments. Its title was something like "Earning however much a year and still treated as low-end population," about someone paying several thousand yuan a month for a Ziroom apartment whose walls were smashed straight through with sledgehammers. I looked up and asked my colleagues who else lived in a partition. It turned out that at our table of six, apart from one person who owned her own place, the other five were all entangled with partitions.

We were all deflated that afternoon. In the evening I called a close friend and said I might end up with nowhere to live — losing my job, losing my love, losing my home, that kind of thing. At worst, I'd just leave Beijing.

After that, every day I searched Weibo at least 20 times for "Ziroom partition" and "Beijing partition," hoping to spot a glimmer of a reprieve. But most of what I found was panic.

I didn't join those chat groups. I didn't want the panic spreading to me. I trust reliable information channels, and I can search on my own. Those groups are full of brainless people setting the rhythm, and plenty of others mindlessly following along. I detest those groups.

But I think one sad thing about young people is that they gradually lose the desire to speak up for themselves. At least I rarely pile onto any comment threads on public social media. Don't dwell on what you can't change.

I have a dedicated cabinet for my figurines — more than twenty *League of Legends* ones of all sizes, plus Pikachu, Lego and so on. I keep them somewhere I can see them, and they're like guardians.

![The League of Legends World Championship finals, live.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181111173702IyFDhslBO4vfi7AG.png-w600)

I took the occasion to do a big cleanup and packed all the figurines away. I was afraid of cutting too pathetic a figure if I had to leave in a hurry — and of having my figurines actually smashed. So many limited editions.

They guard me, so I figured I had to guard them well.

My roommates were all loyal. The guy in the secondary bedroom said, "If they really demolish yours, I'll hang a curtain and sleep in your room, and you take mine."

Later word came that the centralized crackdown would last until December 31, and I waited on tenterhooks for that day. My partition wasn't demolished, and I breathed a small sigh of relief.

Actually, to this day no one has confirmed that my partition won't be demolished. It's no exaggeration to say that from the first news of the partition demolitions until now, not a single message has said whether N+1 is legal.

Once — I think in May or June — my house manager messaged me privately: don't open the door for anyone who knocks today. "If they knock and no one opens, the whole thing just passes. But if you open it, tough luck for you."

I've never thrown out my packing boxes, and the anxiety has never eased. Same as always — I never felt this apartment would let me live on, safe and settled. I still hold the attitude of "if they make me leave, I leave; if they don't, every extra day I stay is a day I've won." This apartment has never given me a sense of security.

I had actually planned to quit at year-end — I was even tempted to forgo my year-end bonus entirely. Then this happened, and I felt I really couldn't quit, with not a penny in hand. So I decided to tough it out.

Those two words — partition — mean you can never fully set your mind at ease. Except that the rent didn't go up, so your cost of living stayed the same for now.

### I Learned to Slow My Pace, and to Accept Being a Cog

Renewing the lease happened to intersect with a change in my career.

I left my previous company in March. I hadn't found my next job yet, and I figured I'd settle the work question first before deciding whether I needed to move.

The small startup I joined then was in Chaowai SOHO, so I thought the distance was okay, and weighing the cost, I decided to stay.

I had been at the startup for about a month when my current company happened to find my résumé online and called me in for an interview. I didn't have an offer in hand yet, but I decided to quit the startup first and prepare properly for this interview.

That startup didn't pay our social insurance and housing provident fund for us, and the new company would only start paying them the month after next, so I ended up covering two months myself — April and May — over 2,000 yuan a month. The financial pressure was considerable. I didn't tell my parents until the offer was confirmed.

The new company is the client side. The first thing you realize on the client side is that you're no longer in the agency's nonstop sprint. The client side runs on relationships — what you produce in the company is meant to be seen by your leaders, your colleagues, other departments. So I put more energy into those things.

That's the difference between the client side and the agency side. On the client side you have to compromise. You adjust your mindset, reset yourself to zero, and recognize that you're a pure cog — you won't play any critical role.

So my whole pace slowed down. I leave work punctually at 5:30 and almost never work overtime. Once, because of an issue on my end, an email system broke down, and by the time IT fixed it, it was already 6 p.m. My manager said, "Forget these past two years — I haven't stayed at work until 6 in years."

As for promotion, the company's rule is clear: an evaluation comes only once every two years at minimum, unless there's an explicit business need.

I've quietly accepted all this. In return I get a slow pace of life and not much work pressure — and correspondingly more free time.

So I exercise, and I want to learn things. I had let my Spanish lapse for a long time and want to pick it back up. I want to learn another language, Japanese — it helps at a Japanese company. These are very practical considerations.

My former colleagues at the agency got promoted at least once, even twice a year, and thinking about that can make me pessimistic. But on the client side, the growth in ability is also big — you see things you could never see at an agency.

I chase a sense of inner ease more — feeling that you can do what you want to do, that's security. When I worked at the agency, after a busy day I didn't even feel like looking at Spanish. I had no mood for anything — not even the mood to take off my makeup.

But that doesn't mean I mind breaking my current calm. Suppose someday a project and a brand I really love comes along — even if the work is hectic, I'd go for it without hesitation.

![Tiantongyuan, one of Beijing's largest rental communities. Image from [NetEase Real Estate](http://bj.house.163.com/photonew/1OQR0007/144739.html#p=9OE0IBLT1OQR0007).](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201811111733401OtEgavWYmyozGVM.jpg-w600)

Take housing again: I'm living very comfortably for 1,000-something yuan now, but if one day I have to move and need to rent a 2,500-yuan place, I'll have to rent it. Nothing can break my current state, except a truly devastating blow — a serious illness, say, or some other great misfortune.

Anything else — a stumble in your career or your finances — I don't consider real problems.

If one day I lost the meaning of life, that would be insecurity. Right now I'm making progress, and I think that's the meaning of life.

### "Beijing Is Not a Place for Me"

My ex-boyfriend and I got together in my freshman year; he was a year ahead of me, and by the time we broke up we'd been together for over five years.

We had reached the point where marriage was on the table. Both of us were in Beijing, and our families felt we should sit down and talk.

He came to Beijing to work first. I came over at the end of April 2016, around the May Day holiday, and we were together in Beijing for about ten months, parting at Chinese New Year in 2017.

We later diverged on life. He worked in media, running WeChat and Weibo accounts — basically on duty 24 hours a day. My personality is pretty strong to begin with, and he'd sit with his back to me, posting to Weibo at his computer.

Later I said maybe we should spend some time apart — this state of things was bad. And at that very moment he was still posting to Weibo. Looking at his back, I thought: there's no point anymore.

My years at the agency left me with a terrible temper. I had a boss with a terrible temper, and I would dump all my negative energy back onto him. I was out of line too, and he lost the patience to absorb it — he wanted to hit back hard. That's how you get a scene like that one: me earnestly talking about reconsidering our relationship while he earnestly typed out his Weibo posts.

Before that I never dared think about breaking up. Everyone says you break up after graduation; we graduated and didn't, and a year passed and we still hadn't, and people told us, "I believe in love again." Because of that I felt I owed everyone an explanation — and that was a burden.

After deciding to break up, I told my mother, "Would you rather hear news that I've broken up, or news that I'm getting divorced?" She understood. I didn't want to marry under the weight of my parents' expectations or my friends' expectations — marriage should be something you enter into wholeheartedly.

I also don't want to say "by 30 I should be this or that" — it boxes you in. I just turned 26, one year past 25, and calmly considered, I still have a lot of potential.

Society is much more tolerant of women now. My former boss was an unmarried woman in her thirties who believed career always comes first. I haven't yet met a man who rivals my career drive, so I can't say whether I'd end up the same.

I've let myself go now, and my anxiety is minimal. I've been reading psychology lately, and one idea I strongly agree with: you have to build self-knowledge in your deep subconscious, so your mental system doesn't fall into disorder.

I used to want recognition from the outside world. Now I feel that making myself happy matters more than pleasing others and winning their approval.

This shift probably only came after I moved to Beijing.

But I will definitely leave Beijing — it's an absolute certainty. I can't buy a place here; I don't have the patience to pay individual income tax for five years and then hand over a down payment of more than a million yuan.

There's no reason for my parents to smash all their savings to make my down payment. I'm not that kind of person.

I also haven't thought at all about which city to settle in. My parents keep pressing me about where I'll go, and I honestly don't know.

Wherever I end up, I'd be renting anyway — I can just grab my suitcase and go.

I stay in Beijing now because my network is here, though there aren't that many friends really. You're used to life here, and work is going smoothly for now.

As for special circumstances — if something goes wrong with the apartment, or work dries up and I can't find anything at all, or some reason forces me to stop working for two months... I think those would be the triggers for me to leave Beijing, but I don't see any of them right now.

If I moved to another city for a future boyfriend, I think the precondition is that my work focus can't change. Love can take up 50% of your life's focus — at most 60%, no more.

My sense of security comes from self-improvement. If I moved somewhere purely to marry you, that's not what I want — I can't lose myself. The precondition of "chasing love to the ends of the earth" is that self-awareness comes along.

But still, same as always: Beijing is not a place for me.

At the interviewee's request, Wang Xuan is a pseudonym.
