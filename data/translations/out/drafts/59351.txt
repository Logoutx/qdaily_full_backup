The author Tung Chuin once said: “Foreigners actually believe that Chinese gardens were handed down from Japan.” “I want to write something about this. We have too few books on the subject, while Japan has so many, so Westerners mistakenly believe that Chinese gardens were influenced by Japan — putting the cart before the horse.”

“What meets the eye is only one part of the whole. As the visitor presses forward to explore, the scene unfolds step by step — inscriptions of poetry and prose stir the imagination; boundless views invite endless reverie.”

This is how 《东南园墅》 (Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China), republished in November, describes the experience of wandering a Chinese garden. Its author, Tung Chuin, held that compared with the “monotony” of gardens built under a France that “prizes reason and insists on logic,” Chinese gardens “spare no effort to avoid rigid geometry and stereotyped order,” and their air of “spontaneity and abandon” is in perfect harmony with the philosophy of the Chinese literati.

![Provided by Pura Culture](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181217003304Uh4nqlxM2G3diNtQ.png-w600)

Tung Chuin was one of China’s first generation of architects. He graduated from the senior division of Tsinghua School in 1925, and that same year went to the United States on a government scholarship to study architecture at the University of Pennsylvania, where he was a schoolmate of Yang Tingbao, Liang Sicheng, and Chen Zhi. In the autumn of 1930 he returned to China and became a professor in the architecture department of Northeastern University in Shenyang.

After the September 18 Incident of 1931, Tung Chuin left Shenyang in haste. Two months later, after a circuitous journey, he reached Shanghai, where he embarked on a brand-new career in a completely unfamiliar cultural territory. During his years in Shanghai, he co-founded the Huagai architecture firm with Chen Zhi and Zhao Shen, and within a few years completed such buildings as the Nanjing Ministry of Foreign Affairs building, the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall, and the Grand Shanghai Theatre. When the Battle of Shanghai broke out in 1937, Tung Chuin followed the Nationalist government’s National Resources Commission to Chongqing.

In that short span of six years in Shanghai, Tung Chuin completed the most important scholarly work of his life, 《江南园林志》 (A Record of the Gardens of Jiangnan) — the first modern Chinese monograph to treat the theory of Chinese garden-making with scientific methods. It was also during this period that he began publishing in English the material that would become Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China.

![This photo was taken on Yuquan Hill in Hangzhou in 1932; the leftmost and rightmost figures are Tung Chuin and Chen Zhi, both newly returned from studying at the University of Pennsylvania — a rare moment of leisure in Hangzhou after they fled south following the September 18 Incident. Photo provided by Tong Ming](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018121622503627pnNh5s3RiIACFZ.jpg-w600)

Originally, this was a work of popular science aimed at the Western world. Since the world’s understanding of Oriental gardens at the time centered on Japan, Tung Chuin began publishing a series of garden-related essays in order to correct this view. The book 《长夜的独行者》 (The Lone Walker of the Long Night) records Tung Chuin as saying: “Foreigners actually believe that Chinese gardens were handed down from Japan.” “I want to write something about this. We have too few books on the subject, while Japan has so many, so Westerners mistakenly believe that Chinese gardens were influenced by Japan — putting the cart before the horse.”

Between 1936 and 1938, he wrote three English essays a year for 《天下月刊》 (T’ien Hsia Monthly), introducing to the world China’s nascent modern architecture as well as Chinese culture; one of these was *Chinese Garden*, published in the October 1936 issue. In 1944, Tung Chuin wrote two sections, *Chinese Painting* and *Chinese Garden Design*, for an English-language volume, *Chinese Culture Series*, that a group of Chongqing scholars were putting together; that book, however, was never published.

![The October 1936 issue of T’ien Hsia Monthly, provided by Tong Ming](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181216225629l7AbwTM5UFK8tzS9.png-w600)

It was not until 1981 that Tung Chuin, drawing on the English essays he had written piecemeal over 50 years, produced 《东南园墅略》 (Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China: An Outline). In March 1983, on his sickbed, he dictated the concluding sections, and the manuscript was finally complete. Two weeks later, Tung Chuin died. And it was not until 1997 that Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China finally found publication, on the occasion of the anniversary celebration of the architecture department at Southeast University.

In the 1930s, Tung Chuin spent nearly every weekend traveling among the gardens of the Jiangsu–Zhejiang–Shanghai region. But the gardens of that time were nothing like what we see today; they lay in a state of “collapsed houses, desolate rockeries, and overgrown weeds.” “This sense of urgency was probably the fundamental reason why he (Tung Chuin) persisted unremittingly in garden research for the following 50-plus years,” writes Tong Ming, translator of the new edition of Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China, in the preface to another of Tung Chuin’s works, 《论园》 (On Gardens).

Tung Chuin regarded the garden as an architectural form possessing a cultural life of its own, and held that a garden’s layout is shaped first of all by rhythm and harmony. Take the plants alone: beyond those valued for their flowers, fruit, and foliage, there are also plants meant purely to delight the ear — the rustle of leaves in the wind, the patter of raindrops on water-lily leaves, the chirping of birds and insects. In the end, “Western gardens are made to please the eye; Chinese gardens are made to please the mind.”

Unlike most architectural histories, which approach gardens through documentation, surveying, historical research, and the interpretation of images, Tung Chuin — with the eye of an outstanding architect — pointed to their charm. “Here, delight matters far more than technique and method,” he wrote. This delight embodies the “leisurely life of a country gentleman” that Chinese literati pursued. And the garden’s winding paths leading to secluded spots fully express the Chinese garden’s theme of concealment and discovery — “this deferred pleasure is in fact the joy of a double enjoyment.” Wandering at random through a garden built like a kaleidoscope is a kind of spiritual relaxation: “visions multiply, the maze never ends.” For the general reader, Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China is a book about how to appreciate and experience the beauty of gardens.

From a professional standpoint, the book’s translator Tong Ming considers it also a work of theory of great depth, in which Tung Chuin analyzes how gardens are actually made. Wang Shu — dean of the School of Architecture at the China Academy of Art, who was deeply influenced by Tung Chuin — read Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China through six times. Winner of architecture’s highest honor, the Pritzker Prize, Wang has been praised by overseas scholars as having “invented a building language closely tied to the movement of the gaze.” Wang believes this is connected to Chinese landscape painting, and that his own understanding of landscape painting “was first provoked by the questions raised in Mr. Tung Chuin’s Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China.” In Wang’s view, Mr. Tung Chuin’s reading of garden delight “has essentially become a possible foundation for architectural theory.” He came to realize that “garden-making does not begin with theory, is not dictated by method, and has nothing to do with whether something is important — the most essential thing is these two words: delight.”

![The 1997 edition of Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China, from Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181216225859eQ2cWjPBx58matXI.png-w600)

In 2018, Tung Chuin's grandson, Tongji University professor Tong Ming, retranslated the book, and Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China was republished. Unlike the 1997 first edition, the new translation adopts the form of pianwen, or parallel prose, but its vocabulary is not the ornate kind typical of that genre — it is plain and concise. Tong Ming felt this was a natural decision; he told QDaily, “When you discuss an object like the garden, you naturally discuss it in the language that the object itself possesses.” The cadence of classical Chinese carries a poetic quality that can carry the reader directly into the garden’s own realm.

Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China also preserves the original English version written by Mr. Tung Chuin himself. As a book on gardens originally written for Western readers, it contains extensive comparisons with Western and Japanese garden theory and garden-making practice. Tong Ming says: “It transcends the categories of various civilizations, nations, and regions, and from a holistic vantage point compares people’s purposes, actions, techniques, habits, and differences of taste in making gardens, thereby presenting the distinctive character of Jiangnan gardens.”

But what Tung Chuin wanted to express was not which is superior, the Chinese garden or the European garden; rather, he saw the garden as a deep dialogue between humanity and nature that exists everywhere, the highest expression of a regional civilization. Only through these extensive comparisons can the character of Jiangnan gardens be brought out more fully.

The new edition’s translator, Tong Ming, is the grandson of the author Tung Chuin and a professor at Tongji University’s College of Architecture and Urban Planning. He studied architecture as an undergraduate and master’s student at Southeast University, and received his PhD in urban planning theory and design from Tongji University in 1999. He is also the principal architect of the firm TM STUDIO, whose works include the Hehe Courtyard, the Zhou Chunya Art Studio, Suquan Garden, and the renovation of the small old-town park in Luqiao, among others.

We also spoke with Tong Ming about Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China, gardens, modern architecture, and more. The following is some of what we discussed.

**Q: QDaily**

**Tong: Tong Ming**

**Q: Wang Shu writes in the preface that you are not an interesting person, and (at the time) did not even like architecture. Yet you later devoted enormous energy to organizing nearly all of Mr. Tung Chuin’s writings. How did your passion for architecture build up bit by bit? And how did you first come into contact with gardens?**

**Tong:** These are two things. First, the interest in architecture — I don’t find it strange at all; it is quite natural. Because when you begin studying architecture in school, you study it in an abstract state. You do schemes, designs, drawings — but these have little to do with actual buildings. While in school, there is no way you can have the conditions to actually build a house in any substantive sense. So you only truly get into architecture, obviously, after you actually start building. As an architect, you conceive, you give form, then you build, you implement. Then you watch people move about within the product of your conception. I think that is a completely different kind of experience — your thinking condensed into reality. I think it is basically in this kind of context that an interest in architecture can naturally arise.

As for the other question — the interest in gardens — I actually think that even now it is still hard to explain very clearly. Because treating gardens as an object of research, and treating them as a genuine passion that wells up from within, are still two different things.

![The Lingering Garden, provided by Tong Ming](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181216230209YWtBNK6qMIL2wgZb.jpg-w600)

**Q: So for you, is it an object of research or a passion?**

**Tong:** Both at once. It also involves a certain state: if gardens exist for you only as something processed through text, then they are an abstract, cold object. But if they are something substantive for you — an environment you can enter and inhabit — then they have warmth for you, and that is a different kind of situation. Back during the publication of the first edition of Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China — in 1996 — in order to edit the book we had to add a lot of plan drawings, which meant visiting many gardens. The Suzhou gardens go without saying; it also involved gardens in Jiaxing, Haiyan, and Pinghu, and even the Shuihui Garden in Rugao, northern Jiangsu. Some of them were very hard to reach then — you had to take long-distance buses. There were no private cars, no highways. In 1996, even a trip to see the Qiuxia Garden in Jiading was quite difficult; it took a whole day.

**Q: Could you get the plans once you got there, or did you have to draw them yourselves?**

**Tong:** We drew them ourselves. Some gardens took two or three trips. So in order to produce the first edition of Glimpses of Gardens in Eastern China, we basically retraced Tung Chuin’s own route through the gardens of the region.

**Q: But the new edition has no plan drawings — were they dropped?**

**Tong:** Yes, they were dropped in this edition. Because nowadays plan drawings are no longer anything unusual — they appear in all kinds of books, and many gardens are now open to the public. Take the Mo Family Garden in Pinghu: when I went, it was still closed and hardly let anyone in. Now it has become a famous scenic spot, and access is quite easy. So there was no need to spend more ink at the introductory level. And for the new edition, more of the energy went into the text. Because I think that beyond this kind of introductory material, the book’s greater value lies at the theoretical level.

**Q: By the theoretical level, do you mean what Mr. Tung Chuin wrote about understanding Jiangnan gardens from an aesthetic perspective?**

**Tong:** Yes — how to understand Jiangnan gardens.

**Q: Is this also what you think most distinguishes it from other books on gardens?**

**Tong:** Yes, you could put it that way. Jiangnan gardens are a very distinctive product. Unlike gardens in many other regions, they are intimately bound up with daily life. They sit within the hustle and bustle of the city — conjuring a patch of mountain forest within a space of mere feet. This involves a certain mood and artistic conception: the kind of rustic woodland charm you can find anywhere once you leave Suzhou for the countryside, say. But to create or evoke that within an urban, market-street environment is extremely difficult.

So it raises a correspondingly more basic question: how was it built? This is what interests us about the matter — and what puzzles us: how did they make it? Even a garden as large as the Humble Administrator’s Garden is tiny compared with a natural setting. Yet it presents you with a boundless world; in this you can see a great deal of thought and of ways of making.

![Provided by Pura Culture](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181216232004Y8jwQiWELGTxqkpK.jpg-w600)

**Q: Among other architectural forms in the world, are there others that also exist in such urban, market-street settings?**

**Tong:** Yes, but not obviously so — there aren’t all that many to compare it with. In medieval Europe, for instance, there were the cloister gardens attached to the monasteries of the church in Western Europe — gardens for meditation, completely cut off from the outside world, enclosed within inner walls, suited to meditation. But (the Chinese garden) is not quite like that. The Chinese garden is also surrounded by a ring of high walls, but it is not so somber; it contains a great deal of delight, so it is a place of joy.

**Q: In this new translation, the stylistic form adopts pianwen (parallel prose), but with plain, concise vocabulary (not the ornate diction usually associated with that genre). Wang Shu called it “a new stylistic experiment in the understanding of gardens.” What was your thinking behind it, and how do you view the new translation’s style?**

**Tong:** I think it was entirely natural. When you discuss an object like the garden, it is more fitting to discuss it in the language that the object itself possesses. It is very concise, but its cadence runs deep. Take the “With Whom Shall I Sit” pavilion in the Humble Administrator’s Garden, named after Su Dongpo’s lines: “Leisurely I lean on the folding couch; beyond Lord Yu’s tower, a thousand peaks. With whom shall I sit? The bright moon, the clear breeze, and I.” A certain resonance is born from this; all of one’s thoughts may be poured into the situational creation within a garden. This is a perfectly synthesized construct — not merely material, but more a product of the mind.

I think this is the richness that gardens offer at such a high level. And classical Chinese belongs precisely to this mode of utterance. Classical Chinese is also simple; much of it is just the stacking of nouns. But more importantly, it is the lingering flavor produced by the collision between word and object — and this aftertaste is inexhaustible. This is different from our language today, which seems too verbose, too excessive, leaving you no poetic corner in which to develop or express anything. It is too stiff and flat, lacking efficiency, and lacking this kind of space. So I think that using a style leaning toward classical prose to carry forward the meaning of this treatise is probably the most fitting way.

**Q: The book’s typeface is rather large, and different from that of ordinary books — it feels like the type used in elementary and middle school textbooks, like when we studied classical Chinese as children. Was this by design?**

**Tong:** Because we wanted to slow down the speed of reading. At a pace of taking in ten lines at a glance, you miss a great deal. Of course, nowadays when we read we wade through vast amounts of useless information, so you cannot help reading that way. But within a classical Chinese system, the word count is small. Like many passages in the Records of the Grand Historian, they are extremely forceful — every character may be worth savoring for a long time. I think this is the power of language. And naturally, this kind of linguistic power is equally suited to the subject of gardens; gardens are like that too.

**Q: One chapter of the book explores the “comparison of East and West.” In your reader’s guide to On Gardens, you also mention that Mr. Tung Chuin was a northerner with an overseas education, which means he began his research from an almost completely unfamiliar state. Can I read this as viewing Oriental gardens from a Western vantage point? How do you view such a perspective?**

**Tong:** I have a slightly different view. Tung Chuin — or his whole generation, really — did not entirely use a Western vantage point to look back at the East. By his own account, he regarded his native culture, or the cultural qualities he himself possessed, with a universal eye. He called it a *cosmic basement* — a cosmology, or a worldview. It transcends the categories of various civilizations, nations, or regions, and looks at the matter of garden-making from a holistic vantage point, asking what sort of thing it really is.

That is why he made so many comparisons: the differences in purpose, action, technique, habit, and taste with which people in various lands and civilizations make gardens, and through these, the character of Jiangnan gardens is presented. The character of Jiangnan gardens can only emerge through contrast and comparison — not through a muddled, soliloquizing kind of expression.

**Q: Are the gardens mentioned in the book still what we call gardens today? How has the meaning of the word changed? Has the concept of the garden today transformed into the concept of urban public green space?**

**Tong:** Yes, this is a big problem — the dilution of the concept of “garden.” Looking at the earliest origins of gardens, and at the written character itself: the character yuan (“garden”) has an enclosure frame around it, and the frame represents a ring of walls. As for the character’s origin, it is related to the ancient character you, which referred to plots for growing vegetables or keeping animals. And the garden itself is a man-made, delimited space filled with natural things — trees, rocks, water — all bounded by human artifice.

So it has a specific meaning, in both East and West. But now the situation we face is that people apply the term far too loosely: the little garden behind someone’s house is called a yuan, and a city park is called a yuan too. These are entirely different from the kind of garden we are talking about — one with spiritual power, a claim on one’s life, and the enjoyment of viewing. This conceptual confusion and abuse causes many of our problems today.

![Tong Ming. Photo: Liu Zhen. Cropped](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181217103354UyEIK6zgZulocNM8.jpeg-w600)

**Q: If we don’t muddy the concept, are gardens still developing today?**

**Tong:** Of course. Gardens come from your life, your real world. As long as the real world does not perish, this will always exist. That is to say, many of us still keep the habit of growing flowers and tending plants — and of course this doesn’t mean I grow a couple of scallions to eat or to use; it is more a spiritual pleasure, or something in which to invest one’s feelings. It is like keeping a pet — you keep cats or dogs not for any task, but for the joy obtained from interacting with them.

As for gardens, we can understand them as artificial pets, a naturalized pet created by human hands. They reach a state of communion with your temperament and vitality. This kind of human wish and aspiration can hardly be extinguished in any era — otherwise you would become a cold-blooded animal.

**Q: So can I understand it this way: only with this kind of spiritual claim can something be called a garden in our time?**

**Tong:** Well, the first question is how we define the garden.

In A Record of the Gardens of Jiangnan, Tung Chuin gives a very incisive definition: the character yuan (“garden”), written in its traditional form, is an enclosure frame combined with a phonetic element. The frame stands for a ring of courtyard walls. The inner element can be broken into parts: at the top is the component meaning “earth,” in the middle is a square “mouth,” and at the bottom is the component meaning “clothing.” He interpreted it this way: the “earth” on top is a mountain, the “mouth” in the middle is a pond, and the “clothing” below represents branches and twigs. This essentially makes the character a pictograph — its correspondence to an image is very clear.

That is the kind of thing it is, corresponding to a scene such as we find in the Lion Grove Garden or the Lingering Garden. If you are very conservative and very strict about it, then the word “garden” cannot be used carelessly.

**Q: I read in an article that “landscape gardening is a welfare phenomenon reflecting real life, providing people with places for recreation and rest.” Do you agree with this view? What role does the garden play in contemporary life?**

**Tong:** If we put it this way: as an object of making, the garden is very hard to create again under contemporary conditions. After all, it is a space — a narrow space within a bustling urban environment — and it is the slowly evolved result of a long entanglement with a master, or with a family. Take the Jichang Garden: its construction began roughly in the Jingtai era of the Ming dynasty, and for more than 400 years that family remained essentially unchanged. Many gardens are bound up with the fate of a family or a master. The Sui Garden in Nanjing, for example: after Yuan Mei died, the garden essentially fell into ruin. Its fate was tied to that of an individual. So from this angle, a garden accordingly takes on a person’s character and his life — the two become one.

Under today’s conditions, we can hardly have this anymore. In an urban environment, life changes so much, and the various regulatory conditions are so complex, that it is very hard to obtain again such an exclusive, or delimited, form. But this does not rule out its continued necessity as a form of reflection on the world we live in. We are actually moving toward another extreme: the whole world is constructed as a purely materialized domain. The relationship between your housing and the concept of your family is growing ever weaker. Your living environment has less and less to do with you as an individual. A person living in a city is like a vagrant — nothing around him seems to fit him; he drifts about like a rootless boat. This is a transformation of the era, a great change of our times.

And the question follows: do we need roots, or a cultural foundation? Do we still need an aspiration like the garden? It need not be expressed in the form of a garden, but as a person — as a person with cultural roots — I think this is still worth pondering.

![A photo taken by Mr. Tung Chuin in the 1930s while surveying gardens in Suzhou. Provided by Tong Ming](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181216230550UKSE8mdq0PL1OB5u.png-w600)

**Q: On Gardens mentions that “the Chinese garden is not a place of mass amusement… it is the product of a refined art,” and “this is not a place of diversion, but a place of retreat and quiet contemplation.” Beyond leisure and vacation, what do you think is the meaning of viewing and appreciating gardens for us today?**

**Tong:** I think this is a matter on which different people see different things. As for the current way gardens are presented, I find it hard to offer much more criticism. If they were not open to society and served only as a setting reserved for a cultured few, that would not be entirely appropriate either.

**Q: Has academia come up with better ideas for how to present gardens? Or is it still searching?**

**Tong:** Still searching. But the Kyoto system in Japan, I think, is worth learning from. That is, unlike the Suzhou gardens, which have become a park system — buy a ticket, and on holidays they are packed with people and the garden’s mood evaporates entirely — Kyoto uses a reservation system; for some popular gardens you have to book one or two months ahead to get in. Tickets are not necessarily expensive. This limits the number of visitors on the one hand, preserving the ambience of the garden-viewing experience, and on the other hand it gives the gardens some breathing room for maintenance. More importantly, this way of presenting them happens in a state that is more intimate and more consonant with the garden’s own temperament.

Could the Suzhou gardens be run this way? I think it is worth considering. The first big problem is that gardens must be distinguished from the broader park system and landscape system. They are national treasures, different from the consumption venues of people’s daily activities. They are a cultural distillation and cannot simply be conflated with those.

![Provided by Tong Ming](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181216234539vAYRrOPgDlEVy5MC.png-w600)

**Q: In the book, Mr. Tung Chuin already wrote in the 1980s: “Western landscape architecture, now rapidly becoming a fashionable course at academies across China, is eroding the foundation of the classical Chinese garden, passed down through the generations but already teetering on the verge of collapse. If we slacken and let things drift, leaving them to fend for themselves, the classical Chinese garden will, like traditional painting and other traditional arts, gradually sink into an archaeological relic.” What is the state of research in this field today, 40 years later?**

**Tong:** (That situation) still exists. He recognized this very precisely 40 years ago. Landscape studies is more of a subject-like education: we think about making a piece of landscape — a residential district is built, and we fit it out with landscaping: what trees to plant, what ground to pave, what scenery to create. It is more like a dish that needs a garnish of color on the side. But the garden is not such a thing. The garden has a cultural life of its own as a subject; it grew from a unique source, and it carries a dense history. To treat it merely as something decorative, or a form of visual entertainment — for such a precious heritage, that is obviously inappropriate. I think Mr. Tung saw this very accurately as early as the 1980s. And I think his words remain very sharp for people today.

In the way we set up academic disciplines, we have already lumped the two together — the landscape architecture major and so on. You can place them side by side as disciplines, but the things themselves are completely poles apart.

**Q: Beyond his love of gardens, Mr. Tung Chuin also loved Western modern architecture. The reader’s guide to 《新建筑与流派》 (New Architecture and Its Schools) notes that from his “journey to Europe” in the 1930s onward, Mr. Tung Chuin conducted sustained research on modern architecture spanning several decades. In that era he was already paying attention to the modernist architectural wave that had only just begun to sprout. This also shaped his creative attitude during his Huagai architecture firm period. Forty-five years later, he was still following the modernist wave then surging in Europe and America after World War II. In the book, he was already pondering how to cross the divide and find China’s own path to modernist architecture.**

**What influence did his love of Western modern architecture have on Mr. Tung Chuin? And what influence did it have on his study of Chinese gardens?**

**Tong:** Generally speaking, when we talk about modern architecture, we usually mean the result formed since the Industrial Revolution through the development of modern technology, modern materials, and new ways of thinking — a new architecture different from anything before. It is an embodiment of the progress of the times. In a broader sense, modern architecture has an even wider meaning: the contemporary expression of a traditional civilization, we would also call modern. Therefore the road to the modern is not a single one — or rather, the modern is not only the sense of transformation brought about by the technological development of the Industrial Revolution; there is also cultural renaissance, which is likewise a kind of modernity.

At this level, I think his thinking was a cut above ordinary people’s. That is, he did not confine what we should do today within a narrow conception, but thought from a more fundamental angle — what is the modern?

And whether the things of our past can become modern: for him the answer was affirmative. So his two lines of research, which seem entirely unrelated — one on the century-long evolution of Western modern architecture, the other on the classical Chinese garden, or cultural research with the garden as its emblem —

actually, once you put them together, you can see his very clear intent: how can a cultural renaissance be transformed into a modernity? This transcends insights at the ordinary level of material technology and reaches a deeper plane.

![The former site of the Nationalist government’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs (the Nanjing Ministry of Foreign Affairs), first built in 1934 (the 23rd year of the Republic), designed by Zhao Shen, Tung Chuin, and Chen Zhi. From Baidu Baike](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181216233000AWZzYoci4PQ3UeK6.jpg-w600)

**Q: This year’s Shanghai PSA exhibition 《觉醒的现代性——毕业于宾夕法尼亚大学的中国第一代建筑师》 (Awakening Modernity — China’s First Generation of Architects, Trained at the University of Pennsylvania), which you curated. In the exhibition video, I saw a passage of yours about “modernity.” You said: “Even today we may not yet have thought through or reflected on this question thoroughly and fully. In such a state, the discussion of modernity is extremely important.” What modernity are you referring to?**

**Tong:** Modernity is an eternal question. What is *modernity*? What does the concept mean? The impressions we usually associate with the word “modern” are things that are fashionable, new, never seen before. But that is only one aspect — or rather, a shallow understanding.

What produces these completely new, unprecedented, fashionable things? They are produced by “contemporaneity” — by the quality of the present moment. The here and now is necessarily different from everything that existed earlier along the axis of time. In the here and now, what can you express? This is a question about modernity — about being-there, about human existence, about the present. So this is a core question, a huge, deep one. And so: what should our present be?

Translated into an architect’s question: what should architects of our moment do? The answer certainly cannot be to haul over and copy what came before. Whether from the past or from elsewhere, that is not the meaning of the modern. The so-called modern refers to something that sprouts, that grows. And the causes that bring about the sprouting we usually attribute to a technological revolution, or a transformation of social structure, or a cultural modulation — any of these is possible. The question has to be pursued further down.

From this angle, the process of Western modernization is the product of modernity’s manifestation. The earliest expression of this consciousness was the Renaissance. The Renaissance was actually, in human history, an originating moment in the awakening of modernity. *Renaissance* means a sudden rebirth — that consciousness suddenly appeared. Faced with the magnificent legacy left by Roman times, what should people of the present do? Although it took the splendid art of the Roman period as its reference, it was absolutely a brand-new cultural system formed on the basis of reflection.

This includes painting, sculpture, poetry, and architecture — all of these burst forth at once in that posture of sudden expansion during the Italian Renaissance.

By the 20th century, likewise: with all kinds of new energy sources, new technological materials — steel structures, concrete — and the emergence of various functions, what should our architecture of the moment be? This, too, was the question posed in that era. When that question was posed in the 1930s — when this group of people returned to China and, facing the reality before them, had to take up the question “What should modern Chinese architecture be?” — they naturally began to think about these problems. How did these schools and styles, the various technologies, the transformations happening in every field, touch architecture? They strongly opposed continuing to use old traditional forms; instead, they asked how development should proceed from within, from the inner source. Likewise, from the standpoint of traditional culture, the point was not to copy a form that already existed, but how to draw out the thinking within it and apply it to the present. So this is a question of form and meaning — a discourse at a different level.

**Q: Does the discussion of that time continue today?**

**Tong:** It is still discussed today, but I think today’s discussion should be deeper than it was then, because times have advanced after all, and there has been much cross-fertilization of new ideas. I think that in our discussion of this question today, the level should be much higher than it was back then.

**Q: I have seen some works from your studio, TM STUDIO, and they seem to carry shadows of the Chinese garden. What influence has traditional Chinese architecture had on your design practice?**

**Tong:** I think this is mostly a subtle, imperceptible influence; there is no need to distill it into tangible methods and then put them into practice. Anything touching this field is never that simple. We speak of giving form to express meaning — the process of going from likeness in form to likeness in spirit — which is forever a deep-rooted problem in artistic creation. If you merely imitate on the level of form, you can never reach that artistic realm; rather, you must draw strength from the spirit, from that which can be sensed. Only then can you create tangible works that are more full of life.

This is a hard, big topic. For me, I don’t think I have deliberately used garden methods to do anything; rather, through my apprehension of gardens, I bring into my design thinking something about materials, about light and shadow, about the connecting relationships between object and meaning. At the very least it forms a judgment within you — what is good and what is not — and I think that is very important.

As with many things: when you are designing a building, you face many choices. A very good architect can always make the most fitting and most poetic choice, rather than a simple, pallid development. I think this judgment is very important.

**Q: Does university education today offer any education in this kind of judgment you speak of?**

**Tong:** No, very little. Of course this is also very hard. It is actually a cultivation of taste, or of character.

**Q: So do you think this can be brought about through university education?**

**Tong:** Impossible. This is always personal.

University education is only a broad, universal education — a mass education; that is why it is called a university. *University* means mixing together all kinds of specialized disciplines; it is just a general, comprehensive instruction, not education proper. Its cultivation of a person’s mode of thinking, intellectual character, and tastes is forever an individual matter. So I think the question of education cannot simply lay all the responsibility on schools; it should be seen as a whole. The biggest problem of our era is that we have produced too many test-oriented talents, while people who think with an independent spirit are too scarce.

**Q: When it comes to gardens being built now, what comes to mind for me are many guesthouses; the atmosphere they create is quite similar to gardens, and they have rockeries and the like. Does this count as a form of design practice for the garden in our time?**

**Tong Ming:** The premise first has to be what object we are discussing. In reality there is a great deal of construction — apartment buildings, guesthouses, houses. But the context of our current discussion is the architectural work; like a garden work, it is a consciously, aesthetically aware act of building. That is somewhat different. The two cannot be lumped together for commentary.

Within this context there is a distinction between *architecture* and *building*. *Building* is a product of engineering technology, or of socioeconomics. Architecture with a capital A is associated with *art*. But what is art? This is a complex question; it is more a re-presentation of the human spirit. So it is hard to draw the relationship between the two apart at once, because they are both different and obviously closely connected — it is not that simple. So I am not willing to explain this question in a very simple way.
