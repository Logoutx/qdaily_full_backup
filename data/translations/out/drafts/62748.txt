Is "the contemporary era" the biggest meme of the contemporary era?

If we're talking about buzzwords in life today that manage to be "rich in meaning," "as versatile as a cure-all that works anywhere," and "something no one can quite explain," the word "contemporary" would probably rank in the top three.

At first, "contemporary" simply meant "the present age"—the sort of word that seems to have always existed in everyday life, not requiring any effort to understand, and hardly a fresh formulation. You've probably seen it many times in QDaily Research Lab. But if you search your memory a little, you'll find that for a long time it appeared in rather serious guises like "contemporary art" and "contemporary writers," only entering our everyday vocabulary in recent years, and its meaning has become somewhat hard to pin down: today's "contemporary" is less a summary of a period of time and more like "an era so complicated and fast-moving it can't be explained in a sentence or two." In other words, "contemporary," like "hardcore" or "traffic," has become a kind of [spectacle](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/59766.html), a meme with a hint of [mockery and deconstruction](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/59539.html). What it really wants to say is: "Look, this is just how the world is."

### What Are We Talking About When We Say "Contemporary"?

At the end of 2018, QDaily Research Lab asked everyone, "[What small things this year made you feel an era was beginning or ending?](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2619.html)" The most upvoted answers included "realizing popular stars are younger than me," "never needing to carry cash anymore," "the passing of Mr. Jin Yong," and "getting used to paying for video streaming memberships." Together they neatly summarize the typical impressions people have of the concept of "contemporary":

- A way of life represented by keywords like globalization, [fragmentation](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/23355.html), and [urbanization](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/54099.html) (a typical example is [the "cloud space" of cafés and airports, which looks more or less the same everywhere in the world](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/58362.html));
- Ordinary people who love technology and embrace the internet yet are awed by its dark side and trapped by their phones and social media;
- Trendy emotions characterized by vivid interpretations of all kinds of tiny [first-world problems](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/37103.html) (such as [social anxiety](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/54106.html), [FOMO](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/54474.html), and [bad-news fatigue](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/59830.html));
- And the "Age of XX" symbolized by celebrities, internet influencers, products, and big companies (the sharing age, the idol age, the instant-messaging age, and so on).

These dimensions overlap to form the brave new world before your eyes, along with an intangible yet thought-provoking "sense of the times."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190411221411kJVXvwxMAGKLlbea.jpg-w600)

But putting that aside, what exactly is so special about this contemporary era branded with shopaholism, internet trolls everywhere, the influencer economy, information anxiety, and so on? Those of us living in it, bearing the honorary titles of [millennials, Gen Z, digital nomads, and the drifting generation](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62492.html)—have we really become so temperamentally different that we deserve to be categorized so finely?

No, we raise this question not to deny the uniqueness of "the contemporary." From hippies to rappers, from television to smartphones, from newspapers to Instagram, every generation has its own fads, symbols, and appeal, and change has certainly happened. We only want to remind you that when every generation gains a voice, it habitually focuses on and emphasizes its own individuality, habitually thinking "the best times are already past," "what's with those young people," and "our generation has it so hard."

From this perspective, the so-called "new era" is also a bit like a repeater; the gap between it and the last era may not be as big as we think: when you look at those surface phenomena ([rebellion against tradition](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/58139.html), [constantly reminiscing about the past](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/58944.html), [fatigue with the taken-for-granted](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52610.html)) and consider the human reason and emotion behind them, you realize that in most cases they aren't all that fashionable or novel; sometimes they're even recurring, [universally present eternal experiences](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/29390.html).

The British science-fiction writer Douglas Adams once proposed a "Three Laws of Technology": "Any technology that existed when I was born is ordinary and part of the natural order of the world. Any technology invented between the ages of 15 and 35 will be a revolutionary product that changes the world. Any technology invented after I turn 35 is an abomination against the natural order." This passage can partly explain why talk of "the Age of XX" has become denser and cheaper: in rare cases, time happens to be cleanly divided by things called "milestones/catalysts/anniversaries," but more often, "an era" is just a shortcut for "I want to find a word to sum up everything within my sight," a web woven for us by information that makes us feel "honored to be part of it."

Since "the contemporary" has already been given so many personas and labels (even the word "contemporary" itself has become a label), we might as well try giving it some new personas; they might even sound more interesting than "the traffic era":

### The Avocado Age

Cherries haven't been around long enough, and kale's name is too obscure. Besides the avocado, what other fruit could win so much affection, be called "[a symbol of global trade](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/51688.html)," and sweep from North America across Asia? [The USDA says Americans consume 7.5 pounds of avocados per year](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62640.html), more than peaches, pears, plums, apricots, and nectarines combined. At this rate, the middle class has no business calling itself "middle class"; it should really be called the "avocado class."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190411221344jgQAwvZDKphyiR4P.jpg-w600)

### The Sparkling Sweet-Drink Age

["Either don't drink, or drink too much" is no longer the rule](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62650.html). For young people who like low-alcohol drinks, "a little drink at home after work" is the new trend. You can read this as contemporary people being more casual, more self-loving, lonelier, more mindful of interpersonal boundaries... but whatever the reason, the sweet little drink has won.

### The "Pink Must Be the Limited Edition" Age

Whatever the product, people buy the black or white version for reasons like specs, experience, performance... But there's only one reason to buy the pink version: "Because it's pink." Pink hasn't been a women-only color for a long time; it's the unattainable ideal in everyone's heart. Otherwise, why do you think so many people would bust through doors and stand in long lines to grab a pink [cat-paw cup](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61673.html) and pink limited-edition sneakers?

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190411221316ZXk8sWjhE0NFHBt6.jpg-w600)

### The "There's No Problem a Cat Can't Solve" Age

[Humans are said to have kept cats since ancient Egypt](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/58681.html), but cats have only truly become the spiritual totem of the contemporary era in recent years. Cats are [a sense of belonging](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62492.html) in empty-nest life, unofficial emotional therapists for office workers, and the source of joy for millions of cloud cat slaves. Even Netflix's latest release, [*Love, Death & Robots*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62119.html), casts the cat as the mastermind behind it all... Everything is illusory; only the cat is real.

### The "Why Are There More and More Disposable Things?" Age

A joke isn't funny after you've heard it once; friends can unfollow each other at the drop of a hat; idols are abandoned the moment scandal breaks; influencer cafés are never revisited after you've checked in... Collins Dictionary chose "[single-use](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/58105.html)" as its 2018 Word of the Year. Look around at all those [fast-food aphorisms, disposable truths, and one-off TV dramas](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2875.html), and you have to admit that, as they say, this is a full-fledged "throw-away society."

### The "I Chat So Much Online That Offline Feels a Bit Awkward" Age

Face-to-face conversation used to be part of daily life, but the art of in-person chat is no longer common knowledge—after all, no matter how thick your inner bullet comments are, they can't be synced to your face. [Using slang, stickers, and punctuation correctly, plus just the right number of "hahas,"](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/21431.html) is the language class everyone must take these days. Some say [real offline contact is about to become a luxury of this era](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62528.html). But so what? Isn't that exactly what we socially anxious people want?

### The "I Can Give You WeChat But Not Douban" Age

Yes, social media platforms come with their own territorial segregation. These days, [we can divide even the word "friend" into 17 types](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/54519.html), and even a friendship with the same person progresses at different speeds on different platforms. Who doesn't have a few friends these days who are "life-and-death buddies online but fall into an awkward silence after three sentences of small talk offline," or whom you "thought you might need but couldn't stand shortly after adding, so you muted them"? This isn't hypocrisy; it's sticking to your principles.

### The "Headphones Are Humanity's Built-In Force Field" Age

If you too suffer from ["feeling uncomfortable everywhere without headphones when going out"](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2618.html), or have the "no one can defeat me in my own BGM" complex, then congratulations—you've caught the rare sweet moment when "coldness replaces smiling as the default look for going out." This era welcomes you.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190411221326IGo59rqE1WuidNhS.jpg-w600)

### The "Lifestyle ≈ Decompression Philosophy" Age

The [good-life trio from Northern Europe and Japan](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/48729.html) has been touted for years; [minimalism](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/60333.html), whose basic principle is "don't add entities unless necessary," hasn't been fully figured out yet; and [danshari](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/60577.html), whose secret is "let it go," is catching up. Ready-to-eat life philosophies iterate into new versions with strange logic every so often, from [pyt](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/61701.html), which claims to reboot your life, to [ASMR](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/59265.html), dedicated to five minutes of relaxation—the more anxiety buzzes through contemporary daily life, the more popular the placebos that claim to [solve it](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/60394.html) become.

### The Secondhand Life Age

The glorious achievement (or byproduct) of information saturating life: [whatever problem you encounter in life, you can find an operation template just by searching online](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/58601.html). How to dress understatedly and elegantly, where to eat cheap and tasty food, which headphones are better value, whether to work or take the grad-school entrance exam, whether to buy Bitcoin—you just take the ready-made answers. In short, what the world is like, what it feels like to experience life, and how to live most wisely—someone has already served up all the answers. You no longer need to explore life's answers for yourself; a search engine can provide them.

### The "Every Emotion Needs a Name" Age

[Researchers at Queen Mary University of London](http://nymag.com/scienceofus/2016/06/10-extremely-precise-words-for-emotions-you-didnt-even-know-you-had.html) say that when an emotion has a name, you feel it more often and feel its effects are more controllable. The lovable people of the contemporary era have put this into practice quite thoroughly: the cookie-cutter little joys and little annoyances are no longer in vogue. Those trivial little feelings now have more exquisite names: "[fear of better options syndrome](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/56436.html)," "[blank-time anxiety](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2200.html)," "[multitasking OCD](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2819.html)," "[answer-dependency disorder](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/2633.html)," and the pièce de résistance of triviality, "[Whisker fatigue](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52610.html)" (meaning: when a cat presses its face into a bowl, the sensation of its whiskers rubbing against the rim makes it tense, causing it to paw food onto the floor, fight with other cats, or become anxious while eating)... Really, there's no creature more self-absorbed than contemporary humans.

### The "Social Media Needs to Be Reined In" Age

Being addicted to your phone is sinful; controlling your endless craving for information is the ultimate political correctness. Everyone is shouting "scrolling Weibo is so exhausting" and "put down WeChat and do something else," but no one can really leave them: hot topics appear every moment, the information feed never ends, and the happy game mechanics are genuinely addictive. "Everyone's using them, what can I do?" we sigh, while skillfully tapping a friendly like on a nine-grid Moments post.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190412112018LRUci9VusJS6A5nK.jpg-w600)

### The "The Internet Age Is Already Passé" Age

Twenty years ago it was still called the "prehistoric internet age"; now if anyone mentions "the internet age," it sounds like they're about to reminisce about BBS forums, floppy disks, and MSN; ["Year One"](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2507.html) and ["the wind tunnel"](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/2705.html) are not only misnomers but also disappearing at a rate now measured in days: "the contemporary" may be an illusion, but "the sense of the times is iterating faster and faster" is absolutely true.

### The "The Best Era Is Already Past" Age

TV dramas used to be better, childhood cartoons were better looking, women of that era were more beautiful... "When literary youths want to return to the age of Hemingway and Picasso, the Hemingways want to return to the age of Byron and Hugo, and the Byrons always want to return to the Renaissance—the golden age is always in the past." This line can explain not only childhood, nostalgia, and music, but also why we are always reminiscing about the past. After all, scientists have noted that [the essence of nostalgia is actually a kind of unhappiness](https://www.bustle.com/p/why-do-we-feel-nostalgia-a-new-study-says-it-has-more-to-do-with-sadness-than-you-think-17015765).

### The "You Wouldn't Dare Live Through It Again" Age

This phrase comes from [the New York Times](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/59224.html), but it describes nothing unfamiliar. Heraclitus said no one can step into the same river twice, and the same is true of the contemporary: the moment you say it, a small part of it has already become past tense. On one hand, life seems to be getting flatter; on the other, when you experience certain things, the feeling of "being struck right in the heart by contemporary life" suddenly wells up inside. In that moment, you may finally come close to the essence of "contemporary life": some things have changed, but some things never will.
