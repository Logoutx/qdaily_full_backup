## This Spring Festival, we unpack 14 of Paul Graham's intriguing observations about good design from *Hackers & Painters*.

## Good design is timeless

As time passes, many designs are forgotten by us, but the ones that survive the test of time, still deliver the best solution, and stay etched in our memory — their appeal must come from the power of design itself, not from the whims of passing fashion. Fashion is a race you can never finish, but if you strip away the ever-shifting factors of trends and vogue and aim simply for the best solution, the result will never be bad.

Aircraft designer Kelly Johnson once put it this way: "If the solution is ugly, then there must be a better solution out there — it just hasn't been found yet."

So if a design from 50 or even 100 years ago is still held in high regard today, it's safe to imagine that 100 years from now it will still stand as a model of good design.

**The ET66 calculator**

Braun's most iconic calculator, the ET66, was born in 1987. Its simple rounded-rectangle form and gently protruding round buttons came from the hand of German designer Dieter Rams. After joining the German company in 1955, he gradually wove his "less, but better" philosophy into its products.

Looking at the ET66, it's hard not to think of the calculator interface in Apple's iOS — and indeed, the iOS calculator took its design inspiration directly from the ET66.

**The 1970 Volkswagen Beetle**

This is the single car model produced for the longest time in history, and the best-selling single model of all time.

Japanese designer Mitsuhiro Shoji tells us this was the first car he bought as a student. "The driving experience of giving old things new life has the most charm. The simple beauty of older products' construction can, with slight modification, keep being used indefinitely — a distinctive elegance that modern design lacks. Isn't this exactly what modern product design must take into account?"

In the summer of 2003, the last Beetle rolled off the production line at Volkswagen's plant in Mexico, closing the book after more than sixty years on the market. But judged purely as a car, the Beetle was never an innovative, high-caliber model even when it officially debuted in the late 1930s. What it did have — sturdiness, reliability, simplicity, and a friendly shape, the qualities visible from a design standpoint — gave this little car something many of today's complex, powerful models lack.

**The Uten.Silo wall organizer**

This wall organizer, Uten.Silo, was designed in 1969 by German designer Dorothee Becker for Swiss furniture giant Vitra, and it remains a best-seller in Vitra's lineup today, radiating a strong retro charm.

Uten.Silo was widely acclaimed from the moment it appeared, and a smaller version was later released. With storage boxes and hooks of various sizes and shapes, Uten.Silo keeps all kinds of everyday items tucked away yet easy to find.

The small odds and ends that would otherwise scatter across the room for lack of proper storage all have a home in it. Keys and other little objects can hang from the hooks, while sticky notes and business cards can find their place in a metal clip.

**The Alessi 9093 kettle**

The 9093 kettle, designed by Michael Graves, has been one of the best-selling products in the history of Italian kitchenware brand Alessi since its birth in 1985, and it is also a landmark of postmodern design.

Its conical stainless-steel body and round handle are its signature design, and the arched padding on the handle protects the user from being burned by the metal.

But the most special part of the 9093 is the little bird perched on the spout. Its inspiration came from Michael Graves' experience of hearing a train whistle on a country morning, and many people bought the kettle precisely to relive that feeling of being woken by birdsong or a steam whistle. It is not just decoration — it is also a safeguard against boiling-water burns. When the water boils, a rubber whistle hidden in the bird's wings sounds, as if the bird were singing, adding a touch of vivid charm.

At first the manufacturer thought Michael Graves' fee of US$75,000 was too steep, but when sales reached 1.5 million units, it proved that putting the kettle into production was a wise decision. Last year, to mark the kettle's 30th anniversary, Alessi once again invited Michael Graves to design a 30th-anniversary edition.

**Leica cameras**

Leica is a legend among camera brands. First introduced in 1925, the Leica camera pioneered the miniaturization of cameras, created the 35mm film format, and gave rise to the vast camera market built on that format.

Its loyal users have included war photojournalist Robert Capa and Henri Cartier-Bresson, father of the "decisive moment" theory, among others. The Leica M, launched in 1954 in particular, was the ultimate product of the 20th-century camera industry: its arrival ended the golden age of twin-lens reflex cameras led by Rolleiflex, and forced German maker Zeiss to abandon head-on competition and turn instead to single-lens reflex cameras.

In his biography *The Mind's Eye*, Cartier-Bresson wrote that when he discovered the Leica at the start of his career, "it became an extension of my eye, and I have never been parted from it since."

Today, even the cheapest model in the Leica M system — a bare M-E body — costs US$5,450, and the finest Leica setup runs to nearly US$20,000. Yet the professional photographers who own Leicas all trust its craftsmanship and lens quality, and relish the absence of flashy extras. **The Coca-Cola contour bottle**

On November 16, 1915, the Coca-Cola contour bottle — with its one-of-a-kind curves — officially received its patent, its "birth certificate," and it has now been around for 100 years. It long ago ceased to be merely a container for liquid and became a cultural icon, a legend of packaging design.

Remember the brief Coca-Cola first sent to glassmakers across America when it solicited design proposals: the packaging's shape had to instantly evoke the drink inside, and even if the bottle shattered into pieces on the ground, people had to be able to tell it was a Coca-Cola bottle. In the end, two designers at the Root Glass Company in Indiana found inspiration in the Encyclopaedia Britannica, modeling the curvaceous contour bottle on the shape of the cocoa bean.

From a design perspective, its aesthetic itself carries a metaphor of the female body, offering pure aesthetic pleasure. Second, the contour bottle's grip is far better than an ordinary beer bottle — simply hold the raised curve and it sits easily in your hand. And the contour bottle also creates a visual illusion: the liquid inside seems to be more than it actually is.

Years later, these three traits were distilled into the three great laws of beverage-bottle packaging design, passed down among designers ever since. **The Keppel watch**

This is designer Henning Koppel's 1977 work, from the top Danish design house Georg Jensen. An exquisite white enamel dial, black hands, a silver bezel, paired with a dark-green alligator leather strap. The striking, pure simplicity of its aesthetics combined with the rich detail of its mechanical movement — these are roughly the most standard requirements for a fine watch, and The Keppel delivers them all. When Georg Jensen brought it back into production in late 2015, there were two reasons: one, it now houses a modern mechanical movement; two, it is a classic.

## Good design is redesign

Very few people get something right on the first try; doing it a second time, you usually understand it more deeply — think of da Vinci's egg sketches. Strictly speaking, unless it's a brand-new invention, much of what designers do can be called "redesign": designers keep optimizing the things that exist in the world, guided by their own sensibilities.

**The Porsche 911**

The Porsche 911 was born in 1963. At its birth, it looked nothing like the car we see today. At the time, the 911's prototype was jointly designed by chief engineer Labi and chief stylist Komenda. They leaned strongly toward a four-seat design, lengthening the wheelbase by 10.5 cm and widening the body by 7 cm, making the whole car look bulky and crudely styled. Ferdinand Alexander Porsche, the eldest son of the Porsche family, firmly vetoed this bloated design. He sent the 2+2 blueprints straight to the Lude coachworks and had workers there build the body for him, and this insistence on aesthetics paid off handsomely: the 911 displayed its supremely classic curves.

**The Guggenheim Museum designed by Wright (Frank Lloyd Wright)**

Design work on the Guggenheim Museum began in 1947, and it was completed in 1959; since then it has been regarded as a masterpiece of modern architecture. Its exterior is simple and white, a spiral concrete structure quite unlike the traditional architectural style of museums. But it wasn't always this way: in Wright's earliest sketches, the right half of the museum looked something like an ancient ziggurat. Later, in a flash of inspiration, Wright flipped it upside down, and it became the now-classic form.

**Bang & Olufsen speakers**

The Bang & Olufsen speaker brand was founded in 1925 in Struer, Denmark. From its birth, the brand never took market share as its benchmark, and for many years it has maintained an attitude of questioning convention, launching many products with avant-garde forms. In 1974, they launched the Beogram 6000; twelve years ago, they launched the BeoLab 5; and last year they launched their latest product, the BeoLab 90.

"We want ordinary people sitting in their living rooms to enjoy the sound quality of a recording studio," says B&O technical expert Geoff Martin. In truth, the living room and the recording studio are two very different spaces, and B&O has come up with a cutting-edge solution.

In form, the BeoLab 90 breaks from the traditional boxy shape of speakers, adopting a triangular-pyramid design paired with curved wood, black knitted fabric, and aluminum, full of abstract artistic flair.

Technically, B&O introduced the concept of 360-degree sound, using multi-channel, multi-driver, and multi-angle reflection design to achieve the best sound quality.

In hardware, the BeoLab 90 is covered with Scan-Speak drivers large and small: 7 tweeters of 30mm, 7 midrange drivers of 86mm, 3 woofers of 212mm, and 1 front-facing low-frequency driver with a diameter of up to 260mm — 18 in total. The placement and orientation of each driver are the results of countless experiments.

The BeoLab 90 is a product that perfectly fuses art and acoustics, and its price is correspondingly steep: on the official website it costs upwards of 260,000 yuan.

**Tom Dixon's metal lampshades and candlesticks**

Tom Dixon is a product design company mainly engaged in interior design. [We previously published an article related to them](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/21205.html), in which Tom Dixon delved into the influence of materials and scent.

"The deeper you go into an object's color, form, and comfort, the more you become aware of the other details that stimulate you: the sound or smell of a place. Compared with the visual part, these may have a greater impact on your experience." Tom Dixon believes that the more products a designer makes, the more insight he gains — and then he can simplify the choices and weave a story around them.
