Him, them, us.

“All that is solid melts into air.”

People wary of modernity, artists, cynics, defenders of the poetic and the pastoral, believers in the gun and in violence... and, of course, communists themselves — everyone loves this line.

This mesmerizing line appears in the famous *Communist Manifesto*.

It has a touch of literary flair and a touch of revolutionary swagger, like that famous portrait of Che Guevara — the political icon, resolute, gazing into the distance — who wouldn't like that vibe? People look to this line for inspiration, existential or artistic, and for the strength and reasons to act.

Cool.

Today, Karl Marx would be 200 years old — if he were still alive.

### **1818: “All That Is Solid Melts Into Air”**

Perhaps rehearsing Marx's【1】 intellectual achievements and political legacy has become a cliché by now. No one is better suited to sum up his life than Engels【2】, and nothing is better known to Chinese readers than the eulogy Engels delivered at his graveside.

“The law of development of human history” — historical materialism, yes; “the specific law of motion” of “the modern capitalist mode of production” and bourgeois society, yes; original discoveries in numerous fields — reportedly including mathematics — yes.

But is that all there is?

As Marx's contemporary, Engels could not have foreseen how later generations would come to understand Marx — how, like blind men groping an elephant, they would seize on one facet of him or another, then sincerely apply it, stubbornly misapply it, or cunningly appropriate it. These scattered, overlapping projections would become the main source of the general public's understanding of Marx.

Some read in *The Communist Manifesto* the inevitable death of capitalism, and demanded it be carried out politically; some read in the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* the “alienation” of man, and developed from it a humanist aesthetics and social theory; some read in *Capital* a kind of economic determinism, after which culture no longer mattered, and neither did the spirit...

In 2017, the Marx scholar Jonathan Wolff, giving a lecture in London, reminded his audience to pay attention to the date *The Communist Manifesto* was published. Everyone knows it was 1848; some know about the armed revolutions that broke out across Europe that year. But what Wolff focused on was this: Paddington station opened in 1838; Waterloo station opened in 1848; King's Cross station opened in 1852...

![London's King's Cross station, 1852. Image source: [Honbicot](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kings_Cross_ILN_1852.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505033159IQZtc1bHKmlsvuqC.jpg-w600)

This meant that large-scale population movement became possible, and drastic social change became possible. The poet Baudelaire【3】, born three years after Marx, captured this modern condition in literature: flux, the fleeting, the contingent. This was the horizon Marx saw.

We can still see that horizon today.

This makes it easier to understand the line at the top of this article. In one official translation, the fuller passage reads:

“Constant revolutionizing of production, uninterrupted disturbance of all social conditions, everlasting uncertainty and agitation distinguish the bourgeois epoch from all earlier ones. All fixed, fast-frozen relations, with their train of ancient and venerable prejudices and opinions, are swept away, all new-formed ones become antiquated before they can ossify. All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned, and man is at last compelled to face with sober senses his real conditions of life, and his relations with his kind.”

Among all the critiques we know of modern society, this passage still holds an important place — this modern society persists to this day, and the critique of a society driven and shaped by industrial capitalism remains meaningful. At the very least, it seems more relevant today than “a specter is haunting Europe.”

If you wander Europe today, you won't find the legendary footprints at the British Library, but you can see Marx's former residence on Dean Street — in London's Soho. Marx moved here in his day to save on rent; now it's one of London's most vibrant neighborhoods: bars, strip clubs, upscale restaurants...

The phrase “class struggle” has dissipated from these streets. Left-wing youth passing through today might sigh over “alienation” — which, of course, also comes from Marx...

All right, our curtain has risen.

What follows: you'll see many familiar faces — oh, them, all of them branded, it turns out, by the mark of the world's most famous bearded, curly-haired man. Some call themselves Marxists, yet are deeply doubted; some never acknowledged any connection to Marx, yet kept running into the very questions Marx had set; some are little known, quietly guarding and developing Marx's spiritual legacy.

We believe that showing this “constellation” — to borrow Benjamin's【4】 term — irradiated by Marx and, in turn, irradiating us, can offer a dimension for observing Marx different from the mainstream narrative. Following these various “avatars” of Marx, we will bear witness to the modern history of the past 200 years, to how people have submitted, resisted, and reflected within the modern world.

Sometimes, even (of course), we'll see our own shadow too.

“The best and most ideal thing that could happen would be for the landlady to throw me out of the house. Then at least I would be spared paying the twenty-two pounds. But I can hardly count on such a great favor from her. Then there's the baker, the milkman, the tea merchant, the greengrocer, and the outstanding debt to the butcher. How am I ever to pay off all these damned debts?” Marx wrote to Engels in a letter of complaint in September 1852.

On receiving the letter, Engels sent money by return post.

People often see Engels as a revolutionary who betrayed his own class. In popular culture, his main job seems to have been, as above, “sending Marx money.” But some important works of Marxism were written by Engels alone: *Anti-Dühring*, *Dialectics of Nature*, *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*...

No wonder Marx wrote in another letter to him:

> “Frankly speaking, my conscience is often weighed down as though by a nightmare, at the thought that it is mainly on my account you have let your fine energies rust and become stunted in commerce.”

In fact, if you taught a course on the intellectual origins of feminism, *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* would sit at the top of the reading list. In this book, Engels supplies anthropological evidence for the birth and evolution of patriarchy. His perspective centers on the division of labor, and gender is precisely the most basic form that division of labor takes.

This is “the world-historic defeat of the female sex. The man took command in the home also; the woman was degraded and reduced to servitude; she became the slave of his lust and a mere instrument for the production of children,” Engels wrote.

Marx's second daughter, Laura, married Paul Lafargue【5】, a left-wing activist born in Cuba. But Marx wasn't entirely pleased with some of his son-in-law's words and deeds. Not long before his death, he said something sharp about Lafargue and another French labor-movement leader, Jules Guesde【6】: “One thing is certain — if they are Marxists, then I am not a Marxist.”

What made Marx so angry was a subtle disagreement: Guesde did not believe reform could be won from the bourgeoisie, and he thought [part of the program](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1880/05/parti-ouvrier.htm#n5) previously agreed with Marx did nothing for the class struggle and would instead become bait the bourgeoisie could use to win over workers. It seemed that he and Lafargue were more radical than Marx.

In [an article from 1883](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lafargue/1883/lazy/preface.htm), Lafargue mocked how the authorities used the clergy to pacify the suffering masses. Then he turned around and proposed a workers' “right to be lazy.” It sounds exaggerated, but it points to an important insight: socialists must attack not only the bourgeoisie's institutions but the ethics behind those institutions.

Today, people seem to have grown ever more accustomed to all sorts of “corporate culture.”

In hindsight, the small dispute between Marx and Lafargue foreshadowed the left's fate of splintering. From Lafargue to Bernstein to Kautsky, from radical change to reform and reformism, people argued endlessly over who truly represented “orthodox” Marxism. The argument would continue into the 20th century, even between nations.

In 1883, Marx died at the age of 65.

That same year, two economists were born: Keynes【7】 and Schumpeter. Half a century later, they would become theoretical rivals, but the questions they wrestled with never escaped the context Marx had set. This was the horizon they saw.

A year later, a group called the Fabian Society was founded in London. It would go on to attract several important socialists, including H.G. Wells【8】, author of *The Time Machine*, and India's Prime Minister Nehru【9】. At the society's founding, a young man with little formal education got involved. Back then, hardly anyone had heard the name “George Bernard Shaw.”

For Shaw【10】, joining the Fabian Society marked a turning point in his thinking. Two years earlier, he had read *Capital* for the first time; within the Fabian Society, his enthusiasm for communism gradually shifted toward a more English brand of reformism. At an international conference in 1896, Shaw prepared a “Report on Fabian Policy,” declaring that outdated elements of Marxism should be criticized, and that social progress should be achieved through compromise within a democratic society.

This line of thought extended even to George Orwell【11】. Labeled an “anti-totalitarian,” Orwell was in fact a left-wing writer. His hatred of social injustice and authority came from his impoverished background and his experience of colonial life, while the infighting within the left that he witnessed in the Spanish Civil War left him deeply wary of the Soviet model.

In the customer reviews for *Homage to Catalonia* on Amazon UK, one reader wrote: “I have been a socialist all my life and this book perfectly sums up how the left is more effective at destroying itself than any enemy ever could. I have to quote it every time the left turns on itself (which is always). 'My cause is more important than yours!' That's always how it goes on the left. Seventy years on, and it's still the same.”

Let's keep this passage in mind.

### **1917: “The World Clock Changed Its Ways”**

[“From this moment — 3:10 — the world clock changed its ways.”](https://read.douban.com/ebook/12591767/?dcs=subject-buylink&dcm=douban&dct=3757732)

Ten years later, in 1927, the writer Stefan Zweig would record the departure of a train this way. It set out from Zurich and arrived in Petrograd. According to Zweig's account, Lenin【12】 stood on a tank there and delivered his first speech to the people.

Many people were not prepared for events like the October Revolution and the founding of the Soviet Union. Just look at Lenin's own lieutenants — before long, many of them would fall into life-and-death opposition, most typically Stalin【13】 and Trotsky【14】.

Inside and outside the Soviet Union, Lenin could not convince everyone.

Lenin's core idea was that a “vanguard party” made up of intellectuals should train the proletariat into a disciplined revolutionary army. He agreed with Karl Kautsky【15】 that the proletariat could not build a “modern socialist consciousness” by its own strength alone, because it lacked “profound scientific knowledge.”

To put it in a metaphor: the working class, the masses, were the body, and the vanguard party was the “brain” that commanded the body. Another metaphor Lenin used was that the relationship between the vanguard party and the revolutionary masses was like that between the engineer who designs a factory and the bricklayers who build it.

It seems Lenin didn't much believe the masses were capable of spontaneously launching and organizing a revolution. Other Marxists thought precisely the opposite.

Lenin's German contemporary Rosa Luxemburg【16】 believed that the creativity of the masses was abundant and unpredictable. By using the “dictatorial force of a foreman” to direct the masses' revolution, Lenin could only end up strangling that creativity:

> “Without general elections, without unrestricted freedom of press and assembly, without a free struggle of opinion, life dies out in every public institution... Public life gradually falls asleep...”

So wrote Luxemburg.

Another critic of “vanguard party” theory was also a woman. Alexandra Kollontai【17】, head of the Women's Department of the Secretariat of the Soviet Communist Party's Central Committee, was the spokesperson for the “Workers' Opposition” faction within the party in 1920. This left-wing faction opposed experts and officials who weren't working on the front lines dictating production, and demanded the creation of an All-Russian Congress of Producers, elected by trade unions, to guide production.

“Communism cannot be implemented by decree. It can only be created through a process of practical research, through mistakes — perhaps only through the creative power of the working class itself. While experts and officials play an important, cooperative role, only those directly tied to industry can give it lively innovation.”

By the way, Kollontai was the world's first officially accredited female ambassador.

For intellectuals and artists, this was an age woven together from chaos and hope.

“-ism” became the most productive word, yet almost no two people could reach full agreement on what any particular -ism meant — and even within a single person, one could witness contradiction or self-betrayal. Prokofiev【18】, who created modernist music, and Eisenstein【19】, who revolutionized montage theory in film, both felt dizzy in the presence of power.

Between art, politics, and thought, they sometimes actively sought their own place, and sometimes accepted the place assigned to them.

Hannes Meyer【20】, the second director of the Bauhaus school of architecture. This architect, radically left-leaning in his politics, had barely taken office before he added courses in advertising and sociology to the school; he also held a radical view of architecture's function: it should meet social needs, and nothing more — no aesthetic standard required.

As the political atmosphere in Weimar Germany grew tenser, Meyer stepped down as director in 1930 and left for the Soviet Union — where he did not receive the trust he had expected.

Consider Meyer's [1928 summary of his architectural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannes_Meyer#cite_note-2):

> “1. sex life; 2. sleeping habits; 3. pets; 4. gardening; 5. personal hygiene; 6. protection against bad weather; 7. hygiene in the home; 8. car maintenance; 9. cooking; 10. heating; 11. exposure to light; 12. service — these are the only factors we should consider when building a house.”

But the place that most attracted artists remained Paris.

If, like in a Woody Allen film, you traveled back to the Paris of the 1920s and '30s, you'd have the chance to see avant-garde artists like Picasso and Buñuel. Many of them became Communist Party members.

Comrade André Breton【21】, joined the party in 1927. Writer, founder of Surrealism. In the [“Manifesto for an Independent Revolutionary Art”](https://www.marxists.org/subject/art/lit_crit/works/rivera/manifesto.htm), co-published with Diego Rivera (though many believe it was actually written with Trotsky), he quoted words said to be Marx's: that the writer should treat his work as an end, not a means, and should, if necessary, sacrifice his own existence in exchange for the existence of the work.

Comrade Pablo Picasso【22】, joined the party in 1945. Painter, founder of Cubism. In 1945, [he published an article in a newspaper](https://blogs.cul.columbia.edu/schapiro/2010/02/24/picasso-and-communism/) explaining his “motive for joining the party”:

> “Joining the Communist Party is the logical outcome of my whole life, my whole body of work, and the condition that gives them meaning. Through design and color, I have tried to probe deeper into knowledge of the world and of man, seeking liberation within that knowledge... But oppression and unrest have made me feel this is not enough; now I must fight not only with painting, but with my whole body and soul.”

Henri Cartier-Bresson【23】 — not yet a “comrade.” Photographer, [he had read Marx in his youth](https://www.moma.org/documents/moma_catalogue_2165_300296079.pdf). Before the 1936 French elections, he and some friends made a propaganda film for the French Communist Party, *La Vie est à nous* (Life Belongs to Us). One segment indicted wealth and class privilege by presenting 200 families — Cartier-Bresson's own family among them.

In 1948 and 1949, as China moved toward Communist Party rule, [Cartier-Bresson “happened” to be in China](https://pro.magnumphotos.com/C.aspx?VP3=SearchResult&ALID=2TYRYDDIBX94). To this day, curators are still reading the political elements in his work, hoping to reconstruct the image of [“Comrade Cartier-Bresson.”](https://www.theguardian.com/artanddesign/2014/feb/20/henri-cartier-bresson-exhibition-pompidou-centre-communist)

Comrade Luis Buñuel【24】, joined the party in 1931. Director. He didn't think much of Dalí, his partner on *Un Chien Andalou*, claiming Dalí [“had gone off to the world of money.”](http://k.sina.com.cn/article_5573262723_14c313d83034001hgf.html) His own work constantly sought to expose the absurdities of capitalist society — sometimes, reality itself was surreal enough.

He was the kind of director who would visit slums for the sake of a film. In the documentary *Land Without Bread*, he gave a close-up to a child in the slums, chalk in hand, writing on a blackboard: “Respect private property.” The image said everything.

Few could satirize “bourgeois” as precisely as Buñuel. Below are the titles of the first ten scenes in the screenplay of *The Discreet Charm of the Bourgeoisie* (per Fu Yuchen's translation). The film runs 102 minutes and tells the story of how a group of people keep failing to eat a meal.

> 1. A misunderstanding over timing, and a wake held at the restaurant, leave the dinner unfinished
> 2. Drug dealing and unease
> 3. A couple's lovemaking makes them late for lunch
> 4. The film's protagonists walk aimlessly along the highway for the first time
> 5. Tea and coffee run out; the afternoon snack never happens
> 6. The ambassador's failed tryst with Madame Thevenot
> 7. A military exercise breaks up the dinner party
> 8. Dinner at the colonel's falls through, followed by a stage performance
> 9. The film's main characters walk aimlessly along the highway a second time
> 10. At the Sénéchals', dinner falls through due to police interference, and other things happen too

![A still from *The Discreet Charm of the Bourgeoisie*. Image source: [Douban](https://movie.douban.com/subject/1303364/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505034114BmDMwHGgz2PV1UjR.jpg-w600)

Turn the clock back to just before the founding of the Soviet Union. In 1921, Antonio Gramsci【25】 founded the Italian Communist Party, and was later imprisoned by Mussolini. It was in prison that Gramsci put forward his far-reaching theory of cultural hegemony.

Put simply, cultural hegemony refers to using religion and education to instill one's own ideology across society as a whole, thereby maintaining one's rule. Fascist society under Mussolini became Gramsci's best example.

Fascist society is hardly the only example. Any government pushing large-scale social reform relies on a powerful social apparatus. In the 1980s, as neoliberal movements flourished worldwide, laid-off workers were propagandized as an unavoidable cost of social development. When family-planning policy was first rolled out in China, the walls of every village plastered with slogans like “To get rich, build roads first; have fewer children, plant more trees” were also a contemporary expression of cultural hegemony.

Like Gramsci, Lukács was both scholar and statesman. He opened up a new school of thought within Western Marxism.

The year the First World War ended, the 33-year-old Lukács【26】 joined the Hungarian Communist Party. His friends found the decision baffling: before this, he had seemed more like a literary young man and critic who liked publishing theater reviews in the papers.

Five years later, Lukács collected his essays on Marxism into a book. *History and Class Consciousness* became his most famous work, and is regarded as the founding text of Western Marxism.

He believed Marxism was not set in stone: “Orthodox Marxism refers exclusively to method. It is the scientific conviction that dialectical materialism is the road to truth, and that this method can only be developed, expanded, and deepened along the direction laid down by its founders.”

For Lukács, this deepening of Marxism showed up in his development of “commodity fetishism.” He used the term “reification” to describe the universal condition of the individual in a market economy: “Man's activity becomes wholly estranged from himself. All human activity turns into a commodity, subject to the rules of the market economy, becoming, like any other consumer good, independent of the existence of man.”

Even today, this view hasn't gone stale. Imagine someone putting on a streetwear brand like Supreme and believing that this makes them cool. Lukács would say that's just an illusion — you've merely paid money for a piece of clothing, and nothing about you has actually changed.

Aside from commodity value, human worth seemed to lose any other yardstick — a worry that would recur again and again in 20th-century social critique.

The same year *History and Class Consciousness* was published, the University of Frankfurt in Germany founded an Institute for Social Research. In 1930, the sociologist Max Horkheimer【27】 became its second director, and under his leadership the Institute grew into the famous Frankfurt School. They combined Marxism with critical theory to examine every facet of society.

Theodor Adorno【28】, philosopher, sociologist — and, in a lesser-known role, composer. The avant-garde music he wrote didn't suit most people's listening habits, but that may have been exactly the effect he wanted: to challenge the culture industry's domestication of the human ear.

The “culture industry” describes a kind of disciplining of culture by capitalist society. To traditional Marxists, culture was where human creativity — that is, humanity itself — was best able to flourish. But under the culture industry, cultural products are subjected to the same processes as industrial production: standardized, commodified, and mass-produced. Its function is to provide capitalist society with cultural legitimacy, and to fold human life into the operating track of capitalism.

In *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, Adorno had his own critique of the film industry. He believed genre films deprived people of the possibility of thought, that cartoons endlessly repeated the triumph of technical reason over reality, and that detective films deprived people of their desire to solve problems.

Think of the mindset you bring to a Marvel superhero movie — no matter what, you already know justice will triumph over evil in the end. This may be exactly what Adorno least wanted to see: you have lost the capacity to exercise reason independently.

After all, not everyone is named Žižek【29】, thinking of “revolution and love” while watching *La La Land*.

On the surface, Benjamin's attitude toward popular culture looked entirely different from Adorno's. He believed that with the arrival of the age of mechanical reproduction — epitomized by photography — appreciating and creating art was no longer the privilege of the few. Film gave the proletariat a tool for showing itself, advancing the democratization of society — an optimism people would keep invoking all the way into the YouTube era.

But alongside this optimism, Benjamin also knew full well that entertainment factories epitomized by Hollywood were manufacturing new idols, strangling film's democratic potential. More important was his pessimistic judgment about history: the angel representing hope seems always to be blown backward into the past by the storm, leaving behind a heap of wreckage. In *Theses on the Philosophy of History*, he wrote a famous line: “There is no document of civilization which is not at the same time a document of barbarism.”

Reading this, people may think of art once kept by power, of today's dream factories, of war and racism — barbaric acts dressed in the clothing of civilization.

This is exactly where Benjamin's appeal lies. His optimism about popular culture was not a vulgar technological determinism, but sprang from a belief in breaking out of endlessly repeating history and arriving at a messianic moment — as a Jew, he fused “redemption” with “liberation,” and believed this moment might already be latent in the here and now.

Unfortunately, that moment was not his.

On September 26, 1940, Benjamin, fleeing for his life, was held up at the France-Spain border over a small procedural problem, and took his own life.

### **1945: “Are You Still a Marxist?”**

When the Third Reich's ambitions were extinguished in 1945, world history arrived at a moment of direct confrontation between two ideologies. This turn was especially visible in France. In 1946, the French Communist Party became the largest party in parliament after the general election; a year later, a cascade of domestic and foreign troubles split the coalition government of the Communists and the Socialists. In May 1947, the Socialist prime minister Paul Ramadier expelled all the Communists from the cabinet. That December, the United States agreed to extend $40 million in aid to France, Austria, China, and Italy.

After the 1970s, the French Communist Party dropped the phrase “Marxism-Leninism” from its charter. The radical left never again won the more than 20% of the vote it once had — not until Mélenchon in 2016.

As a Marxist, Jean-Paul Sartre【30】 never joined the French Communist Party. He hoped Europe could become an independent culture apart from the US and the Soviet Union. He managed to do something that looks nearly impossible: reconcile existentialism, which emphasizes the individual, with Marxism, which emphasizes class.

In *Critique of Dialectical Reason*, Sartre argued that society could be reduced to the class, family, nationality, ethnicity, even the body that the individual inhabits. The individual lives within it and participates in it, developing hand in hand with society.

Sartre thus offered a historical narrative distinct from Marx's. In Marx's history there is no individual; history is depicted as advancing, twisting and turning, through class struggle — first slaves and slaveholders, later workers and capitalists. Sartre folded individual agency back into the picture, thereby escaping the charge of “historical determinism” leveled at Marx.

Sartre's partner, Simone de Beauvoir【31】, likewise combined existentialism and Marxism, developing from it a theory for understanding women's predicament in contemporary society.

Beauvoir observed that the oppression women suffer comes from structures across society, economics, and culture. Under this oppression, women fall into an unhappiness born of self-deception, because they cannot make major decisions, because they cannot take responsibility for their own lives.

In the concluding section of *The Second Sex*, Beauvoir wrote: “Both sexes are the victims of one another and also of themselves.” In other words, gender equality is not a process in which one side gives and the other benefits.

![Sartre and Beauvoir. Beijing, 1955. Photo: [Liu Dong'ao](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Simone_de_Beauvoir_%26_Jean-Paul_Sartre_in_Beijing_1955.jpg) / Xinhua](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505034455Rw2mJkIF4g7AbsY1.jpg-w600)

1949 saw two things happen. The significance of the first needs no elaboration — to the considerable surprise of both the US and the Soviet Union, a second great power with Marxism-Leninism as its official ideology appeared in Asia. The second thing is little known. Paul Sweezy【32】, an economist, founded a Marxist journal in Manhattan, New York: *Monthly Review*.

Born into a banking family, Sweezy encountered almost no obstacles on his way to becoming part of the elite. He earned both his undergraduate and doctoral degrees at Harvard. From the late 1930s into the early 1940s, he did economics research at Harvard, collaborating with Schumpeter, an advocate of free-market capitalism.

But as early as his time as a visiting scholar at the London School of Economics right after his undergraduate studies, Sweezy had already begun immersing himself in Marxist economic theory. He later recalled: [“I became convinced that the mainstream economics Harvard taught me was of no help in understanding the major events and trends of the twentieth century.”](https://www.nytimes.com/2004/03/02/business/paul-sweezy-93-marxist-publisher-and-economist-dies.html) He believed an unregulated market economy would lead to economic stagnation and encourage oligarchic monopolies across industries.

In the inaugural issue of *Monthly Review*, an article titled [“Why Socialism?”](https://www.marxists.org/chinese/reference-books/mia-chinese-einstein-194905.htm) stood out. Its concluding section reads:

> “I am convinced there is only one way to eliminate these grave evils, namely through the establishment of a socialist economy, accompanied by an educational system which would be oriented toward social goals... The education of the individual, in addition to promoting his own innate abilities, would attempt to develop in him a sense of responsibility for his fellow men in place of the glorification of power and success in our present society.”

The article was signed Albert Einstein【33】.

As a scientist, Einstein believed that “science cannot create ends” but “can only serve as the means” for achieving certain ends. In his view, an economic science serving the current state of affairs could not overcome or move past a stage of development marked by predation, hostility, and war — which required experts outside any specific field to take part in discussing social issues.

Translated into today's terms, Einstein would be called a “public intellectual.” His thinking represented a shared anxiety among left-wing intellectuals: amid the ruins of the Great Depression and two world wars, could humanity find a path free of future catastrophe? This is where the significance of *Monthly Review* lay: in Einstein's words, breaking through the taboo against discussing these problems “freely and without any hampering conventions.”

That taboo would soon sweep across America with even greater force.

In 1952, the comedian Charlie Chaplin【34】 left the United States under the pressure of McCarthyism. His career onstage and onscreen brilliantly demonstrated the political power of “humor”: in *Modern Times*, the assembly-line worker Charlie repeats mechanical labor for hours on end, even his bathroom breaks watched sternly by the boss to guarantee factory efficiency; after losing his job, he is thrown in prison as a supposed labor-movement leader, and yet he takes to prison life happily, refusing to go back to the “freedom” he'd been promised.

When “Charlie” instinctively pulls out a wrench and starts tightening every nut-shaped thing he sees in daily life, when he works tirelessly among giant gears, any viewer with even a passing knowledge of Marx cannot help but think of the famous critique of “alienated labor.”

Perhaps the most forceful response to the Red Scare of a decade earlier came from something Chaplin said in a 1960 interview: [“I remain just one thing, and one thing only, and that is a clown. It places me on a far higher plane than any politician.”](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/48183-i-remain-just-one-thing-and-one-thing-only-and)

![*Modern Times*. Image source: [Douban](https://movie.douban.com/subject/1294371/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505034625dFcUCeOxqjth6HSr.jpg-w600)

This skepticism toward existing political systems applied equally to the Soviet Union, once romanticized.

In “Why Socialism?” Einstein had already warned: “A planned economy as such may be accompanied by the complete enslavement of the individual. The achievement of socialism requires the solution of some extremely difficult socio-political problems: how is it possible, in view of the far-reaching centralization of political and economic power, to prevent bureaucracy from becoming all-powerful and overweening?”

Besides the Great Purge of the Stalin era, the situation in Eastern Europe after the start of the Cold War also bore out Einstein's fears.

In 1956, the 71-year-old Lukács became a government minister once again. That year, university students in Budapest toppled the statue of Stalin in the square, demanding an end to the Soviet system, the installation of reformists led by Imre Nagy, and a turn toward openness.

To a great extent, Lukács played the role of go-between. He remained a leading party cadre, but argued that the ruling party had to win back the people's trust. The Soviet Union, however, couldn't wait any longer. Facing a satellite state on the verge of spinning out of control, the USSR sent in tanks to invade Hungary, overthrew the Nagy government, and installed the moderate, cautious János Kádár. At the end of the year, Hungary and the Soviet Union met head-on in a water polo match at the Melbourne Olympics; carrying the honor of their entire nation on their shoulders, the Hungarian team won this “blood in the water” match 4-0 and took the gold medal.

In April 1989, Kádár, not long before his own death, publicly apologized for this chapter of history, and especially for his own role in the execution of Nagy. Lukács, who had narrowly escaped, never stopped criticizing the Soviet Union. In an interview shortly before his death, he once again drew a line between himself and Stalin, and between Stalinism and Marxism.

“Unfortunately, Stalin was not a Marxist,” Lukács said.

![A Hungarian flag hung on the remains of the Stalin statue. Photo: [Pesti srác2](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Szt%C3%A1lin_szobor_Budapest_1956.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505034908OD07ZAmKhpIUfkEz.jpg-w600)

Also exiled abroad, like Lukács, was the philosopher Ernst Bloch【35】. When construction of the Berlin Wall began in 1961, he happened to be visiting West Germany, and never returned to East Germany.

Bloch was known as an “unorthodox Marxist.” The “unorthodox” part came from his fascination with the concept of utopia. Revolutionaries who practiced the materialist conception of history often believed that as long as they held the line of class struggle, they would inevitably arrive at communism. But in Bloch's view, every society holds within it a facet of utopia, and the task of Marxism is to uncover these facets and realize them in the present society.

In the view of the Bloch scholar Wayne Hudson, Bloch's most overlooked contribution was his expansion of the concept of ideology. Marx viewed ideology as a tool of bourgeois rule, one that redrew the picture of social reality so that the oppressed proletariat would quietly accept the circumstances it found itself in. Bloch believed ideology existed not only in morality, economics, and education, but even more in everyday life — “advertising, clothing, sports, entertainment” all included.

Think of recent reality shows like *Idol Producer* and *Produce 101* — they break down the process of manufacturing idols and lay it out for you, constantly instilling in the audience what an idol is supposed to be, while nobody stops to think that the idol might be the ultimate product of alienated humanity.

For many “Western Marxists,” a red line was drawn straight through the middle of their lives, splitting them into a before and after, each half narrated selectively. Hardly anyone can imagine that Milan Kundera【36】 — revered today by bourgeois-bohemian youth as a symbol of ethical freedom — had already been a party member for more than twenty years before the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia.

> “Are you still a Marxist?” asks the Catholic Jean-Louis, in a café in Clermont, France.
>
> Vidal, a university professor, replies: “Still am. For the Communist Party, Pascal's Wager still has real relevance today.”
>
> Pascal was a 17th-century French philosopher who sensed the fragility of Christian faith, but managed to find confidence in his theory of the wager.
>
> “Compared with winning, if there's no absolute chance of losing, don't hesitate — bet everything.”
>
> Vidal, a 1960s French Marxist, feels the same way:
>
> “For myself, I ask what meaning history really has. Hypothesis A: society and politics have no meaning. Hypothesis B: history has meaning. Let's say B is only 10% true, and A is 80% true. Even so, I have no choice but to pick B. Because only the hypothesis that 'history has meaning' lets me go on living. I have to choose B to validate my life and my actions. There's an 80% chance I'm wrong, but that's all right.
>
> “Maybe it was Gorky, or Lenin, or Mayakovsky who said this about the Russian Revolution — reality forced them to choose one chance out of a thousand, because if you choose that one chance, hope becomes infinite. Still better than not choosing at all.”
>
> That's how it is: “though the probability is low, the potential gain may be infinite.” The Catholic and the Marxist reach a kind of understanding. Afterward they go to church for Mass, and the priest's words echo Marx's in their own way — he says we are here to welcome a fresh, present-day happiness, that we will be reborn, and the key is that we must hold on to hope.

This scene comes from *My Night at Maud's*, a film by the French New Wave director Éric Rohmer【37】. The year before the film was released, Paris had just been through the events of May '68. In a later interview, Rohmer said this dialogue clearly presented a Rohmer-esque Marxism and Catholicism — not the standard kind of either.

Rohmer really wasn't standard, at least compared with his fellow New Wave director Jean-Luc Godard【38】. In 1963, Rohmer split with the more radical, left-wing film critics and stepped down as editor of *Cahiers du Cinéma*, which they had run together. Rohmer admired American cinema — *My Night at Maud's* even earned Oscar nominations for Best Original Screenplay and Best Foreign Language Film. Godard, by contrast, truly hated Hollywood at the time.

![Godard, 1968. Image source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Luc_Godard)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505035028ZaMVLhIC0z8p9E3Q.jpg-w600)

Godard was more radical. He was unhappy with the bourgeois way of life and criticized French social institutions; turning back to film itself, he accused Hollywood of using cinema to manufacture illusions about life. He believed that documentary and entirely new shooting techniques and narrative styles could recover real history, history that had been overlooked.

He made a number of terrific, fresh works, like *Breathless* and *Band of Outsiders*. And there were other works too, like *La Chinoise* — in which Godard has a group of French youths recite the French edition of Mao's *Quotations*.

European intellectual youth had finally found its next totem.

### **1968: “For Marx”**

In 1959, Fidel Castro【39】 visited the Lincoln Memorial. He had successfully led a revolution, and he and his brother Raúl's grip on power in Cuba would last until 2018. But the man who perhaps better represented the spirit of his age was his central bank governor and minister of industry: Che Guevara【40】.

Che Guevara possessed the most idealistic of revolutionary temperaments. In 1965, he gave up his high position in the Cuban government, entrusted his family to the Cuban state, and set off alone for the Congo and Bolivia, where he was eventually executed. This idealism found subtle expression in his [farewell letter to Castro](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/04/01.htm): “Now everything is less dramatic because we are more mature, but the facts repeat themselves.”

A “post-revolution” Cuba, it seemed, was no longer the best place for him to [create a new man for the 21st century](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm).

This charismatic image quickly became a fixture of popular culture. [“Che's image — simply as an icon, not for what he actually did — represents what we want: revolution, radical change.”](https://www.nytimes.com/1997/04/30/arts/30-years-after-his-death-che-guevara-has-new-charisma.html) So said a snowboard dealer in New York.

![Che Guevara. Photo: [Alberto Korda](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CheHigh.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505035224t4JQVUf9eC1PLw3G.jpg-w600)

And when the well-known left-wing publisher Verso promoted the publication of Che's diary from his youth, they deliberately chose a softer image: a revolutionary licking an ice-cream cone. Printed on the cover of the diary was a line: “*Das Kapital* meets *Easy Rider*” — a nod to the motorcycle trip across the South American continent that Che describes in the diary.

Che's motorcycle journey would not have seemed foreign to Allen Ginsberg【41】. The name of this American poet, a leading figure of the Beat Generation, appears in the first chapter of Jack Kerouac's novel *On the Road*, given the nickname “Carlo Marx.”

Suspected communist leanings put tension into the relationship between Ginsberg and Kerouac. In fact, Ginsberg's own relationship with communists was equally ambivalent. He admired the changes Castro had brought to Cuba, but [once he set foot on Cuban soil](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/opinions/1994/09/04/end-of-the-affair/e195f982-ae73-4c5b-b0b0-feb34d53ebc4/?utm_term=.4a1b0cd1b3ac) — especially after he called Raúl Castro【42】 gay and Che Guevara “cute” — this gay man could no longer keep his seat as honored guest. [In Prague](https://www.nytimes.com/1997/04/06/nyregion/allen-ginsberg-master-poet-of-beat-generation-dies-at-70.html), dissident university students crowned him “King of May,” after which he was once again expelled by a communist government.

The revolutionaries of these countries seemed not yet ready to get along with a poet, but the youth of capitalist countries were already getting ready to welcome the revolutionaries.

> We don't want to take on any responsibility, we don't need anything, we want to seize, we want
>
> to occupy with words, not with hands!
>
> My desire is fact, because I believe in the reality of my desire
>
> Charge, comrade, the old world is behind you
> Move fast!

This passage is drawn from “May '68” as described by James Miller, Foucault's biographer. In May 1968, a group of left-wing students first occupied Paris's Latin Quarter; afterward, more students took to the streets, opposing the government and the “social institutions of the modern world.” When workers joined the protests, things grew even more chaotic. Universities and factories, these ancient social institutions, seemed as if they might explode in the heat of rage.

Among those who took to the streets and stood with the students was Sartre, already a banner-carrier of the left.

Still, viewed from the perspective of later observers, the confusion “May '68” produced was no less than the shock it produced. Many treated Chairman Mao as an idol, but almost no one in the protest columns understood Chinese, or had ever visited the real China. A protester who called himself a “Maoist” later recalled: “If someone had rationally asked me at the time 'what exactly do you people want,' I honestly couldn't have answered. I can't say I didn't even know who these comrades were, and I can't say I was demonstrating just for the sake of demonstrating.”

![The film *The Dreamers*. Image source: Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505035349cfNECXy9DpJG5Rlr.jpg-w600)

More absurd still: when the philosopher Louis Althusser's【43】 many students rushed into the streets, they couldn't find their own teacher.

Althusser was staying in a sanatorium at the time, in the grip of a psychiatric episode. This “mental illness” was very likely no excuse — fourteen years later, Althusser would strangle his own wife, and ultimately escape prosecution on grounds of mental incapacity.

It wasn't unreasonable to expect Althusser to take part in the student movement. In the 1960s, he published two heavyweight books, *For Marx* and *Reading Capital*, and was considered the person in France who understood Marx best. In the first book, Althusser tried to resist a dangerous temptation: dividing the early and late Marx and one-sidedly emphasizing the “humanism” in Marx's early works — as many left-wing intellectuals were doing — a philosophical “discovery” that state propaganda machines then used to attack the Soviet Union.

Althusser used the concept of an “epistemological break” to explain the difference between the early and late Marx's thought. He found evidence suggesting that Marx and Engels themselves gave greater weight to his later historical materialism, while the young Marx's writings were closer to ideology. Emphasizing the late Marx, and backing more radical social change — however you looked at it, this seemed like the natural conclusion.

But Althusser let his students down. He went on to back the French Communist Party's position, dismissing “May '68” as anarchism. Some outstanding young thinkers, including Jacques Rancière【44】, left him over it. Years later, he offered a public self-criticism for his conduct during this period.

Although 1968 is hard to recreate, we can still go looking in books for the ideas that fired up the enthusiasm of youth.

In 1964, Marcuse【45】 published *One-Dimensional Man*. He argued that oppression no longer needed to appear in the form of violence or authority, so long as it could get people used to one-dimensional thinking. When people watch the same television program, they come to believe they belong to the same group. Politically, a phrase like “the American Dream” blurs the vast differences between different people's lives, letting people believe they are living inside that dream.

More one-dimensionality shows up through patterns of consumption. Advanced industrial society creates false needs in people, drawing them further into capitalism's system of production and consumption. Mass media, advertising... the blanket propaganda leads people to believe it's simply that they haven't tried hard enough, and they lose the desire to change the existing system.

This philosopher, who fled to the United States in 1934 and eventually took citizenship there, doesn't seem to have thought much of the country.

Besides Marcuse, Erich Fromm also introduced Marxism to American readers. He drew together the theories of Marx and Freud, exploring at the level of the individual such questions as loving and being loved, and self-realization.

While the streets of Paris were occupied by young people clutching the Little Red Book, a British rock band was busy touring abroad, doing publicity photo shoots, and — most important of all — recording a new album. In May, an album that would later become known as “The White Album” began recording in a studio on Abbey Road in London. The cover held nothing at all except the band's name — The Beatles.

The Beatles' lead singer John Lennon【46】 never claimed to be a Marxist. Even so, search for the question “was Lennon a Marxist” and you'll still find plenty of discussion. Supporters point to “Imagine,” which he released later on. In this song, Lennon depicts a world of great harmony with no hell, no countries, no religion, no possessions — something close to the communist society Marx had imagined. He once admitted the song was [“virtually the Communist Manifesto,”](https://www.rollingstone.com/music/lists/the-500-greatest-songs-of-all-time-20110407/john-lennon-imagine-20110516) though later critics tend to see it more as a simple, humanist song.

People are always squabbling over credit for good things achieved.

In music, homegrown British scholars found something more. They noticed that, in cities including Liverpool, the Beatles' hometown, the children of the working class were embracing all kinds of youth subcultures — offering a new angle from which to observe Marx's classic category of “class”: cultural studies.

![*Resistance Through Rituals*, a landmark work of the “Birmingham School.” Image source: [Douban](https://book.douban.com/subject/4776849/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505040247Rtd6aNJ43qQ01fUh.jpg-w600)

In 1964, an institution called the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS) was founded at the University of Birmingham. Its first director was the sociologist and literary critic Richard Hoggart【47】; its second was Stuart Hall【48】, later a standard-bearer of the “New Left.” They challenged Adorno's relatively static division between “production” and “consumption,” turning their gaze instead to the flesh-and-blood individuals being mobilized by mass culture.

In their landmark work *Resistance Through Rituals*, they treated youth subcultures as resistance to mainstream culture, and as a way for the working class to build community and feel solidarity. They heard anger in rock and in punk, heard an attempt to flee toward a freer, happier world less obsessed with money. But they also admitted this attempt was fragile — these young people were too easily co-opted, their once-revolutionary rituals turned into objects of consumption.

And reality seemed to bear out the latter worry. “Resistance through rituals,” it turns out, was still dancing in shackles.

Some Americans — Black Americans, to be precise — wanted to answer Marx's ideas in a more radical form. One young man named Malcolm【49】 decided to drop his surname — “Little,” a humiliating name “imposed on his ancestors by white slave owners” — and replace it with a single letter: X.

Malcolm X's politics were a peculiar combination. In his early years he practiced nationalism, arguing that Black people were superior to white people, and that the only difference between white liberals and white conservatives was that the former were “more deceptive.” He criticized capitalism; when asked his view of socialism, he asked whether the system was good for Black people, and once told that it seemed to be, he said, “Then I'm for it.”

He was also a follower of Islam — years later, a Chinese writer who had once been a Red Guard and later converted to Islam wrote a romantically inflected [account of the “Black Muslim movement”](http://www.aisixiang.com/data/109127.html): the notion of “Black nobility” “had to be spoken through the mouth of God — and so Islam was chosen.” This writer's name is Zhang Chengzhi【50】.

In 1965, just as he himself had predicted, Malcolm X was assassinated. A year later, a Black self-defense organization modeled on his political program was founded. Rarely in American history has there been a movement quite like the Black Panther Party, waging armed struggle on behalf of ethnic minorities and the working class.

The Black Panthers were considered believers in both Maoism and Marxism. The former inspired them to throw themselves into revolution; the latter inspired them to critique the capitalist economic system and help workers reclaim the means of production. In 1968, they sold Chairman Mao's【51】 “Little Red Book” on college campuses, using the proceeds to buy guns.

Federal security agencies began to crack down hard on this organization, which advocated violence. Beyond carrying out organized assassinations, the FBI also worked to sow division within the group and stir up hostility. The solidarity the Black Panthers called for did not last. In the 1980s, the organization's core figure, Huey P. Newton【52】, became addicted to drugs, and was ultimately killed in a dispute over drugs.

His end made an article [published by a Black Panther in 1970](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/workers/black-panthers/1970/dope.htm) read like a prophecy: “Capitalism + dope = genocide.”

Half a century later, [when the Marxist historian Perry Anderson【53】 looked back on the intellectual and political history since 1945](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1723735), he repeated the judgment he'd made in the mid-1970s: “Europe would see no revolutionary upheaval to compare with the past — not even a failed one... For the first time, most Marxist thinkers began pitching camp inside the university. They produced an astonishing volume of intellectual work, but this work was inevitably constrained by the gap, at the time, between Marxist theory and every kind of mass practice, constrained by the structural separation of intellectual life from political life.”

Whether it was the CCCS being shut down in the early 21st century, or the deaths of the Black Panthers, all of it seemed to bear out Anderson's judgment. The dual identity of “thinker plus revolutionary,” defined by people like Lenin and Luxemburg, was gone for good.

### **“Can the Subaltern Still Speak?”**

Back to the 1960s.

In 1969, the BBC released a color documentary series, *Civilisation* — yes, the original version of today's popular *Civilisations*. For its host, Kenneth Clark【54】, the series represented a stance: as student unrest swept the West, he chose instead to reaffirm the value of these great artists, just as during the Second World War he had insisted on holding concerts under the shadow of the London Blitz.

Three years later, Clark's stance was attacked by a young art historian in another BBC documentary, *Ways of Seeing*. John Berger【55】, a Marxist art critic, whose name has since become bound up with this documentary and this phrase, “ways of seeing.”

Unlike Clark's celebration of great artists, Berger focused on the power behind artworks and the ideology they transmit. Taking the nude women in European oil paintings as an example, Berger pointed out that most of them were not portraits of real individual women, but projections of male desire; and in the contemporary era, aided by color photography, advertising keeps manufacturing myths about desire, pushing viewers from looking toward buying.

After the 1970s, Berger retreated to a village in France, and died in 2017. Contrary to his own hopes — and to Benjamin's, about the revolutionary prospects opened by the masses' grip on photography — the explosive production of images has increasingly followed the logic of capital. At the same time, art's function of intervening in society has grown ever more doubtful. [The graffiti artist Banksy【56】 depicted this absurd situation of art like this](http://melissa-on-the-road.blogspot.jp/2015/12/marx-by-banksy.html): a young man dressed as a street artist paints “Workers of the world, unite!” on a wall, while a cleaner stands by, waiting to wipe the paint off and keep the city tidy.

Artists kept trying to find ways to rebuild their connection with the masses.

Today, visitors to the Hamburger Bahnhof contemporary art museum in Berlin won't miss the large collection of works by Joseph Beuys【57】 held there. The last of these — also the largest — [*Capital Space (1970–1977)*](https://www.smb.museum/en/exhibitions/detail/das-kapital.html) is installed in a huge hall at the far end of the exhibition. Nearly 40 years ago, Beuys used chalk to write, on these mobile blackboards, his vision for the future economic relations of human society, arguing that humanity's creative potential should be placed at the center of economic thought.

![Beuys, 1978. Photo: [Rainer Rappmann](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:BeuysAchberg78.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505035541NRi2yWwea7KXhsl0.jpg-w600)

As a former Nazi Germany air force pilot, Beuys's singular artistic career can be seen as a form of redemption. He considered teaching his greatest work, and fought fiercely against the existing system of art education. In the 1970s, he insisted on admitting students at the Düsseldorf Academy of Art far beyond the official quota, which got him fired, though he was ultimately reinstated after a public outcry in his support. In the classroom, he taught philosophy with great passion — Marx's *Capital*, of course, among it.

These extraordinary acts pointed toward Beuys's ultimate demand: breaking down the boundary between art and society. In *Theory of Social Sculpture*, published in 1979, he wrote:

> [“Social sculpture — that is, the way we shape the world we live in. Sculpture, here, is understood as a revolutionary process; everyone is an artist.”](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Joseph_Beuys#%27Every_Man_an_Artist:_Talks_at_Documenta_5%27,_1972)

The way the British avant-garde musician Cornelius Cardew【58】 bridged art and politics went beyond what most people could imagine.

In 1974, Cardew released an album, several of whose tracks were titled “Sailing the Seas Depends on the Helmsman,” “The East Is Red,” and “Long Live Chairman Mao.” Before that, he had even translated the shapes and sounds of characters from *The Great Learning* into musical notation. According to feedback from a colleague at QDaily, “It's not very easy to listen to.”

Through indeterminate methods of musical composition, Cardew meant to rethink the relationship between musical organization and social and political change — [and this thinking kept evolving](https://www.theguardian.com/music/tomserviceblog/2012/dec/17/cornelius-cardew-music-guide). His turn in the 1970s toward music with more directly political meaning was based on exactly this self-reflection: work in the style of *The Great Learning* did nothing but let capitalist oppression rot further. He even went so far as to found a party: in 1979 he co-founded the Revolutionary Communist Party of Britain (Marxist-Leninist) — and just this past May Day, the party's [website](http://www.rcpbml.org.uk/) was still calling for a fight for a different alternative.

![Cardew's musical score. Image source: [socks-studio.com](http://socks-studio.com/2015/10/05/the-beauty-of-indeterminacy-graphic-scores-from-treatise-by-cornelius-cardew/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505035712OorNlGZIXqEFVLpf.jpg-w600)

In 1981, Cardew died in a hit-and-run car accident. Conspiracy theories about the crash still circulate today. Six years before that accident, another artist and former Communist Party member had died under the wheels of a car — or, to be precise, in a murder.

As in his films, Pasolini's【59】 death carried a shocking “visual effect.” He was beaten with a stick, then run over by a car, and his corpse was mutilated. Police soon arrested a 17-year-old male sex worker, who had accepted the famous director's invitation the night before for a drive. [In 2005, this young man recanted his confession](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2014/aug/24/who-really-killed-pier-paolo-pasolini-venice-film-festival-biennale-abel-ferrara), accusing three other men of murdering Pasolini — and calling him “queer” and a “filthy communist.”

Born in Italy in 1922, Pasolini was shaped by both Catholicism and Marxism. He publicly declared in the newspapers that only communism could offer a new culture, and, as an atheist, brought *The Gospel According to Matthew* to the screen. Shortly before his murder, he left behind a famous line in an interview: [“Consumerism, in my view, is a form of fascism worse than the classical kind.”](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2014/aug/24/who-really-killed-pier-paolo-pasolini-venice-film-festival-biennale-abel-ferrara)

When *The Guardian* paid tribute to the director in 2014, people found that Pasolini's prophecy about the destruction of culture by extreme materialism no longer applied to Italy alone. His fear of media manipulating politics has already become reality in some places. Unlike the generation of Italian '68 youth he dismissed as driven by bourgeois-bohemian complexes, Pasolini's own personal response seems to have been to turn to works like *Salò*, provoking civilization itself.

[“The body is always revolutionary, because it represents what cannot be codified.”](https://book.douban.com/subject/3925866/) This line of Pasolini's may sum up both his aesthetics and his death.

![Pasolini at Gramsci's grave. Image source: [Wikipedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gramsci_Pasolini.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180505035825sMNufI0HkL3WPA4g.jpg-w600)

As for the “bourgeois-bohemian idol” of our times, probably no one fits the role better than Haruki Murakami【60】, but *Dance Dance Dance*, published in 1988, reveals his spiritual pedigree — underneath his lush, romantic prose runs the heavy bass line of social critique already laid down by the “Western Marxists.”

[*Dance Dance Dance*](https://book.douban.com/subject/1020670/) tells the story of a man living off “expense accounts.” In the novel, the phrase “advanced capitalist society” recurs again and again. When the narrator mutters, “We live in a highly advanced capitalist society, where waste is the greatest virtue. Politicians call it stimulating domestic demand; people like me call it squandering — it's just a difference of framing,” readers can clearly hear a feeble resistance to consumerism.

And when the protagonist holds forth — “People worship the vitality of capital, worship its mythic aura, worship the price of Tokyo real estate, worship the gleaming Mercedes-Benz emblem. Besides that, no myth exists left in this world” — readers can't help thinking of Marx's famous critique of capitalism: “commodity fetishism.”

[Murakami has said he began reading the Complete Works of Marx and Engels at age 15.](http://cul.china.com.cn/book/2010-12/21/content_3910835.htm) Having lived through the rise of the Japanese economy and the signing of the Plaza Accord, the petty bourgeoisie in his books can never secure a sense of safety in society, and can only drift through words, alcohol, sex, rock music, and the city.

A postmodern parable.

In 1989, a magazine died.

Four years earlier, the writer Chen Yingzhen【61】, known as “Taiwan's last Marxist,” had founded this magazine. His writing was influenced by Marx, Lenin, Mao, and Lu Xun, and it was precisely this left-wing coloring that got him sentenced by the authorities to ten years in prison in 1968, held at Green Island Prison.

In the inaugural editorial of *Ren Jian* (人间, literally “Among People”), Chen declared his aim to [“make strangers familiar to one another again; make an indifferent society care for each other again.”](http://www.apple.com/) Grounded in a “left-unificationist” stance, *Ren Jian* tried to become [“a medium that breaks through the fractures of its era,”](http://www.ftchinese.com/story/001070332?archive) covering social issues still controversial today, like homosexuality and environmental pollution, and shaped a generation of journalists with its forceful style of literary reportage.

But Chen Yingzhen seemed to be a man out of step with his time. Under his “socialist” style of management, *Ren Jian* eventually folded due to financial problems; when his later political positions angered intellectual circles on the island, he tried to find kindred spirits on the mainland — and [when, at an academic conference on the mainland, he laid out his deep concerns about the future with the utmost sincerity, the mainland scholar who spoke right after him was calling for international investment, treating pollution as a necessary cost of escaping poverty](http://cul.qq.com/a/20161125/004099.htm).

In 2016, Chen Yingzhen died of illness in Beijing. His being “out of step” left behind a sharp question: can the bottom still speak?

This question traces back further to a 1985 essay by the Indian scholar Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak【62】: “Can the Subaltern Speak?” The “subaltern” in her writing refers first of all to lower-class women, long colonized and oppressed by hierarchies of class. When poststructuralists represented by Foucault pinned their hopes for resistance on the individual's biopolitics, what Spivak wanted to ask was whether this individualist solution applied to the “backward” East.

The answer is “no.”

This brings us back to Marx's starting point. In *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte*, Marx had already proposed that the small peasants cannot speak for themselves; they must be represented — and it was precisely they who put Napoleon III on the throne. For Spivak, this is the real political condition of the Third World; struggle here must escape the romanticizing of individual will and take place at a macro level — law, the international division of labor.

The Chinese scholar Wang Hui【63】 has explicitly endorsed Spivak's problem-consciousness, though he believes merely raising this question isn't enough. In an [interview](http://wen.org.cn/modules/article/view.article.php/2059), he nearly repeated Perry Anderson's assessment of the European intelligentsia — that today's intellectuals, in becoming professionalized, have mostly lost their connection to progressive politics, and have even become an obstacle to social progress.

What is the relationship between “us,” as the ones asking the question, and “the subaltern”? Wang Hui presses on.

Marx no longer had to think about any of these questions.

135 more years have passed since Marx's death. And so we arrive at today.

After him, as we have just seen:

Later there was Lenin, holding his banner high, turning the world upside down.

Later there was the Great Depression, capitalism's self-destructive side, perhaps even worse than what Marx had imagined.

Later there was the First World War, the Second World War. Everyone was perhaps thinking a very real question: Marx might not be right, but the world was certainly wrong.

Later there was Sweden's, and the Nordic countries', experiment with a “third way”; influential European organizations like the French Socialist Party and the British Labour Party trace their lineage back to the Second International, once led by people like Bernstein and Kautsky.

Later, private property and capitalism, the very things he had criticized, changed their appearance, and for a long stretch of time seemed to have won an overwhelming victory — so much so that Francis Fukuyama declared history had ended.

And so it goes, all the way to today. The curtain that the modern man Marx lifted has never come down.
