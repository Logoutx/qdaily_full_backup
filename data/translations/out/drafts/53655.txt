The euro's problems are the EU's problems too

Every year, roughly 150 billion banknotes are freshly printed on various papers or plastics, mainly to replace worn-out old notes. Dozens of new designs make their debut each year, generally to stay one step ahead of counterfeiters.

But some also disappear — like the "Bin Laden note," the 500-euro bill, which is being permanently withdrawn from circulation this year.

A single 500-euro banknote is simply too valuable, worth $582 / 3,723 yuan. Ordinary people almost never use it, and legitimate businesses don't settle transactions between themselves in cash. When the European Central Bank decided in 2016 to discontinue it, the reason given was that it facilitates the underground cash transactions of crime and terrorism — which is also where the "Bin Laden note" nickname comes from.

What will cease to circulate is a lot of money — to be precise, 300 billion euros' worth. That is only 2.3% of the total number of notes in circulation, yet it accounts for a third of the total value. Whether high-denomination banknotes should be abolished is still disputed among the world's central banks, but one thing is certain: not many people will miss the euro's design.

There are so many beautiful banknote designs in the world, many of them in Europe: the nature-infused Dutch guilder, the German mark printed with a pianist and a bacteriologist, or the franc, printed on extremely thin paper on both sides.

The euro's design, by contrast, most often draws the verdict "boring." Its obverse shows buildings drawn in simple lines that nobody recognizes, symbolizing the EU's core spirit of "openness and cooperation"; its reverse shows bridges that nobody recognizes, symbolizing "communication between the peoples of Europe and the rest of the world." In addition, the 12 stars on the notes represent "the vitality and harmony of contemporary Europe." It can't be called ugly, but it really leaves little impression.

The paper a banknote is printed on has no value in itself; its value lies in people's trust in the banks and governments behind the currency — the belief that this piece of paper can be exchanged in full for something else in the future. Seen this way, perhaps a banknote's design could simply be a blank sheet marked with a denomination, plus a security strip or hologram, rotated and updated on the international customary cycle of 8 to 10 years.

But in reality, designers employed by governments give banknotes a far sexier appearance. Celebrities, buildings, animals, flags, colors on notes... There is so much meaning behind banknote design; it is the way governments convey values.

From this perspective, the new notes issued worldwide each year are nations' efforts to consolidate that trust. On very rare occasions, people design new banknotes because trust has collapsed — a change of regime (say, from pounds to dollars), or a response to inflation. Among the notes issued by the four major banks of the Republican era in China, the Bank of China's Republican Commemorative One-Yuan Exchange Note used the image of an awakening lion, symbolizing the awakening of democracy and society, while the Bank of Communications' notes mostly featured modern means of transport such as ships, trains, and airplanes. Another example: in the spring of 2009, at the peak of the US recession, designer Richard Smith launched the Dollar ReDe$ign project, inviting anyone to submit their ideas to "redesign the American economy."

As for the euro, it is a singular existence in the global monetary system: never before had one currency circulated as legal tender across 11 sovereign nations (extended to 19 EU member states in 2015). It was also the first common currency Europe had seen since the Roman Empire.

Such a singular currency carries a singular mission — to draw the EU member states closer together and advance European political and economic integration.

It can be said that the euro's very birth was the product of the EU dream made concrete. It asks people to trust the same system across the different cultures, economies, and political backgrounds of Europe's nations, and the process of designing the euro was the process of granting that organization authority.

And so the euro's most important design principle became pleasing everyone. But pleasing everyone is impossible, and in the end it became "offending no one" — a product of compromise upon compromise.

When the European Central Bank commissioned 15 printing houses to produce the euro in 1999, G+D was the one with the largest print volume. On its [official website](https://www.gi-de.com/en/us/), the company writes today: "Studies show that people generally do not trust abstract, unfamiliar shapes."

The images on the euro were not the designer's original intent. Its designer, Robert Kalina, initially proposed showing real architectural designs: the 50-euro note would depict the Rialto Bridge in Venice, the 100-euro note the Neuilly Bridge on the Seine in Paris, and the 500-euro note was modeled on the Pont de Normandie in France.

In fact, the designers did not even confine the euro's imagery to architecture. The 18 candidate themes at the start of the design process in 1995 included Europe's flora and fauna, common legends and myths, Europe's great figures, poetry, maps, myths, and more.

But none of these were what the EU wanted. In the end, the Council of the European Monetary Institute (EMI), the ECB's predecessor, chose architecture as the motif — and during the design process required Kalina to ensure that no building on the notes corresponded to any real-world structure, lest it cause disputes.

![The EMI reviewing design proposals in 1996](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805280912159XnAaJPDYFp03GCR.png-w600)

This also made the euro's design process extraordinarily complex — possibly the most heavily invested design project in history. Research covered hundreds of directions, such as the size of "wallets in each country." According to a survey conducted in 2004, the third year after the euro's launch, by the Dutch central bank, 94% of respondents said the height of the 20-euro note (72 mm) was "exactly right."

The euro's 15 denominations were likewise the result of compromise, arrived at after considering the wealth gaps among EU countries.

Before the EU countries converted to the euro, the banknotes held by citizens of the 6 eurozone countries — Belgium, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and others — were worth between 200 and 500 dollars. In 2000, demand for 1,000-mark notes was 15 times that of 1975, accounting for 34% of the total value of German marks in circulation.

For Germans, the mark symbolized the postwar recovery of the national economy. People displayed an unusual pride in and attachment to the mark. Jens Weidmann, president of the Bundesbank, noted at an event marking the euro's tenth anniversary that in 2012 more than $8 billion worth of marks were still in circulation, a large part of it hoarded as savings.

The other three EU countries — Greece, Italy, and Austria — used, before converting to the euro, banknotes worth less than 2 euros.

To accommodate everyone, the designers set the euro's highest denomination at 500 euros and the lowest at 5 euros, for a total of 7 denominations. The coins ran from a maximum of 2 euros to a minimum of 1 cent, 8 denominations in all. In 2015, average monthly income per capita in Greece was 600 euros, and many people had never even seen a 500-euro note.

The EMI Council decided the euro system's denominations in November 1994 and planned for them to enter official circulation from 2002. Next came the design.

The most common design on the world's banknotes is still the portrait. [Queen Elizabeth II's portrait appears on banknotes on every continent except Antarctica](http://www.pjsymes.com.au/QE2/default.htm); since her accession in 1952, a total of 31 different photographs of her, young and old, have appeared on banknotes around the world.

And excluding political figures, the person who appears on the most banknotes is Columbus: a total of 9 countries have printed his head on their notes, including the one-dollar note issued by the First Bank of the United States in 1874. One commentator remarked that this seemed like an American attempt to claim Columbus for itself, declaring its designs on Central and South America.

The seven portrait slots across the seven denominations could not possibly cover all EU countries, so most of the 18 theme directions proposed by the original euro design working group — composed of historians (art), psychologists, designers, and others — were unworkable: "Europe's collective memory and culture" was too abstract; "Europe's achievements" would inevitably involve people; flora, fauna, and the natural environment also varied from country to country. [The EMI Council narrowed these down to 2](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/other/euro_became_our_moneyen.pdf): "Ages and styles of European architecture" and "Abstract-modern design."

The former theme additionally specified an architectural style for each denomination, as follows:

- 5 euros: Classical
- 10 euros: Romanesque
- 20 euros: Gothic
- 50 euros: Renaissance
- 100 euros: Baroque and Rococo
- 200 euros: 19th-century iron and glass architecture
- 500 euros: 20th-century modern architecture

That is essentially the history of Europe.

The Council also specified the notes' colors, sizes, and the text appearing on them (the abbreviations of Europe's various central banks), and the flags. Only designers chosen by the governors of the EU's national central banks could enter this competition, and they could pick one of the two themes at will. By September 13, 1996, 29 designers or teams had submitted a total of 44 euro design proposals: 27 for "Ages and styles of European architecture" and 17 for "Abstract-modern design."

In public opinion polls, the series drawn by Belgian designer Maryke Degryse won the highest approval (35%). And although only 23% of respondents said they liked Austrian designer Robert Kalina's traditional painting style, the majority of them (76%) felt his banknotes better expressed the idea of "Europe" — which is why the Council ultimately chose his proposal.

By contrast, the euro coins drew far less criticism, because they preserved Europe's pluralistic essence — one side bears a common EU design, the other a design of the member's own country, such as the profile of Queen Beatrix of the Netherlands; the reverse of France's 10-cent euro, with the eternal "Sower" from the franc; or the reverse of Austria's 1-euro coin, with Mozart gazing back at you. A similar design is found on the US state quarters.

But the euro banknotes' denominations were large, and to let more people know what the notes looked like and reduce the risk of counterfeiting, the EMI did not consider letting each country use its own design.

In the end, the euro became the ultimate in political correctness.

Setting aside the iteration of anti-counterfeiting technology, the essence of banknote design lies in summarizing and consolidating the consensus of the people about to use it. On this point, the euro did not do a good job. Michel Prieur, a numismatist in Paris, commented that the euro is full of "sterile architectural blueprints." "If you don't design from identity, you cannot create a currency that enters people's hearts and spirits. The history of the franc is a million times more interesting. They should have chosen some European geniuses to print on the notes, like da Vinci or Mozart."

The euro's design happens to reflect the fatal flaw of European identity. As sociologist [Anthony Smith](https://www.jstor.org/stable/425194) of the London School of Economics put it: "For the inhabitants of the modern European continent, there seem to exist no meaningful and effective common European myths and symbols capable of uniting them."

At the euro's tenth anniversary, [a report in *The New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/01/01/world/europe/euro-is-10-years-old-but-few-are-celebrating.html) noted that ECB officials marked the occasion with a silence and coolness quite different from a decade earlier — in Brussels there was neither ceremony nor press conference. When the euro launched at the beginning of the century, ads reading "the EURO, OUR money" appeared across the EU, teaching citizens how to tell genuine euros from fakes — "touch, look, tilt." Laurent Fabius, then France's finance minister, once praised the euro's use this way: "Thanks to the euro, our wallets will soon be filled with tangible proof of European identity."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180528090720AjNPl1pvODsV0E9b.JPG-w600)

More importantly, the euro has become a microcosm of the EU's own troubled development. Nobel laureate economist Joseph Stiglitz previously wrote that ["the problem with Europe is the euro"](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2016/aug/10/joseph-stiglitz-the-problem-with-europe-is-the-euro).

The euro was a political project from birth; monetary circulation was not the only consideration. It shoulders the enormous tasks of enhancing exchange and cooperation among EU member states, stimulating economies of scale, and strengthening European political and economic stability.

The euro is a central link in making the EU's ideal design a reality; in theory it can advance European political integration and bind together a continent historically riddled with war.

But behind a unified currency and unified exchange rates lies a financial regulatory system that lacks control and is inefficient. Slowing growth, employment problems, terrorism, the migration crisis, the rise of the far right... Compared with when the EU was founded, Europe today is more divided, not less.

The EU's problems won't be solved any time soon, and now Britain is leaving too. But there is still hope of redeeming the euro's boring design.

In April 2017, the EU released the newly designed 50-euro note. Apart from updates to some anti-counterfeiting features, the most important change was the addition of a face to the banknote: Europa, the Phoenician princess of Greek myth.

The Stanford neuroscientist David Eagleman helped push this design forward — though the ECB people who sought his help were motivated not by strengthening European identity but, again, by solving the counterfeit problem.

After the euro's launch, the EU seizes hundreds of thousands of counterfeit notes every year, mostly 20- and 50-euro notes. Although many anti-counterfeiting features have been added to the euro — tilt the note to a slightly different angle and the ink changes color; hold it up to the light and you can see the watermark of a building in the middle; plus more designs invisible to the naked eye, recognizable only by bank machines — the core problem is that nobody cares about them. People in everyday transactions don't check that carefully, and counterfeits usually get discovered only at banks.

Eagleman's advice was that the euro notes' watermarks should use faces rather than buildings. "The human brain is specialized for faces, but there is no dedicated neural space for buildings. Because counterfeit watermarks are usually hand-drawn, people find flaws in faces more easily than in fictitious buildings."

Of course, the argument over who could represent 19 countries and 338 million EU citizens opened again, and the honor went to Zeus's lover Europa — a fictional figure of Greek myth, the first human of Europe. "Europa" also became the name of the ECB's second series of euro notes.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180528090659CO4XcIx6BTo5ZH0M.png-w600)

This conservative choice sidestepped any impact on real-world social values.

A completely opposite example is the new 10-Canadian-dollar note unveiled by the Bank of Canada this March: its obverse bears the portrait of Black Canadian civil rights activist Viola Desmond, and its reverse shows the Canadian Museum for Human Rights, in a clear display of government support.

The euro replaced many of Europe's exquisite banknotes — for instance, a set of three "guilder" notes issued by the Netherlands between 1977 and 1986, designed by Dutch banknote master Oxenaar. Many collectors regard this set as the most beautiful paper money in the world, featuring a sunflower, a snipe, and a lighthouse.

Oxenaar (Robert Deodaat Emile Oxenaar) was one of the few designers of that era invited to design banknotes as an independent designer. Even without the enormous expert team that the euro's birth had, he said in [an interview with Creative Review](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/the-money-maker/) that it was still a long, complex process. Each denomination took 3 years to design; by the time the last denomination entered circulation, exactly 30 years had passed since he first took on the job. "Before I put pen to paper, I received a book as thick as a phone directory, detailing everything about anti-counterfeiting: the paper, the watermarks, every aspect related to security. When you finish reading that book, you feel you could never meet its requirements."

Oxenaar kept in touch with the president of the Dutch central bank by phone and was paid by bank transfer. He even wove many personal elements into the banknote designs, Easter eggs that were only discovered after the guilders had been printed in large numbers. On the 1,000-guilder note he hid his own fingerprint, and on the 250-guilder note bearing the lighthouse he wrote the names of the "three important women" in his life... These designs, which left the Dutch central bank president exasperated, were printed day and night for 30 years, until the euro was born.

News of the 500-euro note's impending withdrawal set off two sharply different debates. First, media reports made the intuitive link between a note's weight and crime. [The BBC wrote](http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/8678979.stm): "A million pounds weighs more than 50 kg, but in the equivalent in 500-euro notes, just 2 kg."

Former US Treasury Secretary Lawrence Summers promptly [published an article](https://www.ft.com/content/c9bfe780-12b4-11e6-91da-096d89bd2173) calling on the United States to follow the EU's lead and abolish the 100-dollar bill to combat crime and terrorism that rely on cash transactions.

On the other hand, [the *Financial Times*](https://www.ft.com/content/1521d216-12d2-11e6-91da-096d89bd2173) pointed out that large amounts of cash serve a basic store-of-value function, a defense against governments' unrestrained negative-interest-rate policies. And [Switzerland's central bank immediately stated](https://www.reuters.com/article/swiss-banknotes-idUSL8N15V25M), as the issuer of the world's highest-denomination banknote, the 1,000-franc note, that Switzerland would keep its 1,000-Swiss-franc note printed with the portrait of 19th-century cultural historian Jacob Burckhardt.

[The Swiss love cash.](https://www.wsj.com/articles/swiss-are-frank-about-their-love-of-cash-1483378809) According to Bank for International Settlements data, in 2015 the value of cash held per Swiss resident was $9,214; the figure for Americans was $4,433, and the eurozone average was $3,571. At current exchange rates, 1,000 Swiss francs equals 866 euros or 1,010 US dollars — second only to Singapore's 10,000-dollar note, whose discontinuation was announced in 2014.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180528090434eAsxJj4gaKwSLoBz.jpeg-w600)

"The Swiss find using paper money very practical, and the Swiss have always had high expectations of privacy," said Silvia Oppliger, a spokesperson for the Swiss National Bank. "Paying in cash is one expression of that — its popularity doesn't mean illicit use."

Switzerland's love of banknotes includes beauty as well. The winners of the International Bank Note Society's (IBNS) annual Bank Note of the Year in 2016 and 2017 were, respectively, the new 50-franc and 10-franc notes, featuring dandelions and paragliders, and tunnels, clocks, and the globe — created by Swiss artist Manuela Pfrunder — corresponding to the romantic themes of "wind" and "time."

Founded more than 50 years ago, the IBNS is organized around a group of passionate banknote collectors; its membership has now grown to more than 2,000 people across more than 90 countries. In their eyes, achieving superb anti-counterfeiting with modern technology is only the baseline; what they care about more is whether a banknote "possesses artistic beauty and imaginative design."

"Banks want banknotes to be as secure and reliable as possible, but the public will focus on the overall design, color, and distinctive features," said Jonathan Callaway, the IBNS's UK director. "If you ask what makes a successful banknote design, you get very different answers from the public, from numismatists, from central banks, and from printing companies." For collectors, the more unusual a banknote's story, the better.

The new franc notes satisfy every imagination. Designer Pfrunder broke with the portrait designs of the previous two franc series, using actions, graphics, and landmarks to show every facet of Switzerland. Those two soft, delicate hands present a lifelike texture; look closely and you find they are composed of countless "field"-shaped elements with anti-counterfeiting functions. The text on the laser foil represents the names and lengths of more than twenty Swiss tunnels.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180528085701JNkTgaAUvfmncDYo.jpeg-w600)

The new franc notes are not made of the usual pure cotton fiber but of a cleaner hybrid polymer (simply put, a plastic banknote). This material means a broader creative space in color for designers, greater durability, and, reportedly, colors that will never fade.

Australia, the UK, Hong Kong, and Singapore have all issued plastic banknotes. But as technology advances, whether paper money itself needs to keep existing has become an open question.

The *World Payments Report* published in October 2017 by Capgemini and BNP Paribas noted that non-cash transactions grew 11.2% in 2014–2015, the highest growth in a decade. A 2018 report from Boston Consulting Group estimated that by 2020, non-cash payment methods represented by electronic payments would account for 20% of total global transaction volume.

Even in cash-loving Switzerland, cash usage has been declining since 1990, falling from 90% to 60% in 2015. Credit cards, mobile payments — all these new things are making cash ever scarcer.

Governments are riding this trend: reducing cash can solve the counterfeiting problem once and for all, tax more efficiently, monitor the flow of funds, and fight crime and terrorism.

But once cash is replaced by accounts at banks or internet companies, [every transaction can be analyzed by the companies that process it](https://www.qdaily.com/articles/38616.html), and what little privacy you had becomes even less.

The withdrawal of the 500-euro note has intensified a worry not directly related to the reason for its abolition: namely, whether governments recalling cash has become a trend, aimed at taxing more efficiently and further encroaching on citizens' privacy. Another question is: when the monetary system moves to a virtual network, what should design do?

Bitcoin, once hoped to be the privacy savior of the digital age, has become a tool of speculators. The financial institutions it sought to overturn are becoming its investors, profiting from its roller-coaster swings.

"Design" matters less in this medium-less system. When Mastercard began launching its own digital payment app, it updated its visual identity system, simplifying the logo into two overlapping circles.

In the transition from precious-metal money to paper money, humanity built trust in paper transactions with the modern banking system and financial regulators, exquisite artistic design, and rigorous anti-counterfeiting technology. Now these currencies are increasingly reduced to symbols like $, €, ¥, circulating through wireless networks. We have gained convenience, but what we have lost is more than beautiful design.
