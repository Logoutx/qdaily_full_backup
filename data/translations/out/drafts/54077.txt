"Low-end globalization" refers to global trade that, unlike multinational corporations with deep pockets and large staffs, can be carried out with a small amount of capital — or even by a single person.

The office of the Somali Ali Mohamed Ali sits in the Yidong Mansion in Xiaobei, Guangzhou: one sitting room, one inner room, no more than 20 square meters in all. The floor tiles date from when the building first went up in 1999. Apart from a few appliances and two Chinese-style teak-colored sofas, there is no decoration whatsoever.

"Sorry, it's such a mess in here, like a warehouse," Ali said as he ushered me into the inner room. He moved aside a 28-inch suitcase by his feet — it looked heavy — a box of goods a client had left in temporary storage.

In 2013, Ali moved from Hong Kong to Guangzhou to set up a freight-forwarding agency. This year is his sixth in this office.

It is April, one of the busiest stretches of the Guangzhou trading calendar. Alongside the European and Middle Eastern faces common at the spring Canton Fair held that month, large numbers of African traders pour into the city. They need to ship their goods home before Ramadan begins in mid-May, so that by the Eid al-Fitr celebrations in June everything is in place to meet the swelling consumer demand.

Ali estimates that several hundred Somalis streamed into Guangzhou this March and April. Most stay only 14 days or a month, and make the trip to Guangzhou four or five times a year. These buyers place orders with Chinese manufacturers, and Ali helps them choose logistics, book shipping containers, and follow up on the subsequent freight.

The competition is fierce. Within the Yidong Mansion alone, small trading companies and freight agencies like his are everywhere. Like Ali's room, they occupy 20 or 30 square meters with two or three employees, handling the transfer of every imaginable kind of goods.

But within Guangzhou's Somali community, what sets Ali apart is his standing. His business has grown to a certain scale, and he rarely acts as a middleman anymore, except occasionally helping friends negotiate a few words with suppliers in the markets. Because of his reputation, his daily affairs extend beyond his own business: settling delivery disputes, giving newcomers advice, vouching for people — or even doing nothing at all, letting the sitting room become a place of everyday Somali social life, where conversation ranges from hometown news to personal quarrels and lawsuits.

Most Africans have no idea what Guangzhou's weather is like until just before their plane lands. One man once arrived in China carrying Kenyan shillings, assuming it would be easy to find a currency exchange. "The first lesson they have to learn is that outside Kenya, the Kenyan shilling is nothing."

The existence of people like Ali not only passes on the most basic "business know-how" by word of mouth — never pay the full amount up front, always pay in installments, keep a close eye on product quality, and don't habitually bargain so hard that Chinese manufacturers send you goods whose quality is discounted just as steeply — it also provides the "lubricant" indispensable to the traders' business.

"Lubricant" is the term used by Gordon Mathews, a professor of anthropology at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, who studies these African traders common in Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Bangkok and Dubai. They carry cheap goods back to Africa, achieving globalization in their own way — but unlike multinational corporations such as Apple and Nike, they have no top-down decision-making or global layout of the supply chain, and no dedicated legal departments or teams of lawyers. Mathews calls this "low-end globalization."

Mathews says that freight agents like Ali also serve as ["cultural brokers"](https://journals.sub.uni-hamburg.de/giga/jcca/article/view/916) — smoothing over trouble and lowering risk while also acting as a bridge, letting Chinese and Africans understand each other's business logic and rules of conduct.

### Ali's Sitting Room

A Teochew man walked into the office looking for Ali. He had already shipped the goods, but his Somali customer — also a client of Ali's logistics services — had failed to pay on the agreed date and could not be reached. The Teochew man said he "had to find Ali, and could only find Ali," because the customer was Ali's friend.

Half an hour later, Ali finally got his friend on the phone. The man had already returned to Kenya; some cash-flow problems meant he would need a few more days to pay. Ali asked him to give a firm date for the transfer, and said that until the money arrived he would hold the shipment in his own warehouse.

The Teochew man said "*mafan*" — "what a hassle" — but expressed satisfaction with how Ali handled it, and left.

"*Mafan*" is one of the few Chinese words many African traders know how to say. Ali says it carries no offensive meaning; dealing with these big and small hassles is simply part of their working day.

Late payments are a hassle, and many Chinese manufacturers have to keep asking when the money will come. On the other side, African traders often find that what they receive is defective goods.

On the office sofa, a Somali complained to me about a money-losing deal of his from a few years back. It was a batch of tablecloths; only when they reached Kenya did he discover they were not the size he had ordered and matched Somali family dining tables not at all. When he pressed the manufacturer, they said the warehouse had shipped the wrong goods, and that if he sent the goods back they would arrange a new shipment.

"How is that even possible?!" He was still angry recalling the episode. "You Chinese are too good at cheating people!"

![Christiane Badgley and Erica Marcus's documentary [*Guangzhou Dream Factory*](https://www.gzdreamfactory.com/about-the-film/), which filmed a group of men and women from Cameroon, Kenya, Nigeria and Uganda (Image / Guangdong Times Museum)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180608110010FNOx1MXPpr3jhlkG.jpg-w600)

At five in the afternoon, the Somali traders have more or less wrapped up their day's rounds — some have successfully placed orders, others have just finished their "homework" in the wholesale markets. They file into Ali's sitting room one after another.

Ada asks them "Coffee or tea?" The place keeps a Kenyan black tea on hand year-round, offers free Wi-Fi, and has satellite TV showing sports matches and Somali news.

Ada is Chinese and works for Ali. Her desk sits right at the office entrance; besides assisting with logistics as Ali's assistant, she doubles as a "greeter."

For Ali (and for the other Somali traders), the concepts of customer and friend cannot be separated — only friends can make business happen. He once considered moving to a newer office tower one street away, where the offices are roomier and the decor and lighting more modern.

"I wanted to become a bit more 'elegant,' but my friends told me that if I moved there, nobody would come. It's too far from the metro station." Ali's "too far" is nothing more than a 15-minute walk from the station exit, but his customers would rather sacrifice comfort than convenience.

A Somali employee, Yanyo, reminds Ada that once Ramadan begins she can no longer ask people what they'd like to drink. In 2018, Ramadan runs from May 17 to the Eid al-Fitr on June 16. Foot traffic to the office drops sharply during that month.

The Somalis are happy to share the fruits of their trips. One man bought a batch of *dirac*, a kind of traditional Somali bridal dress. Another "newbie," who lives in Finland and is also Somali, was on his third day in Guangzhou; he was looking for motorcycle parts and needed to seek the advice of some seniors in this sitting room.

The "old hands" who already know Guangzhou's major wholesale markets inside out told him where suppliers might be clustered, but also warned him that he had better find someone trustworthy to accompany him.

A Kenyan who has been traveling to Guangzhou since 2014 told me that before coming here, he thought every Chinese person could fly through the air, just as in Jackie Chan's kung fu movies. Many of his compatriots also believed Chinese people only eat dog meat.

"A lot of Somalis don't understand China — that's part of why they say Chinese people are all cheats," Ali suddenly interjected. "But how much do Chinese people understand about Somalia? Besides pirates?"

### Somalia, India, Hong Kong, Guangzhou — the Wandering Life of "Chinese Citizen" Ali

In fact, compared with the Africans who arrived in Guangzhou earlier, Ali came late. He went to Hong Kong first.

Ali's identity papers carry a Chinese name, "Mu Yali." He once posted a photo on WeChat Moments of a sheet of paper covered with the three characters of his Chinese name, "Mu Yali" — those are the only three Chinese characters he can write.

In 2009 he obtained Hong Kong permanent residency; his nationality became Chinese, and he travels between Hong Kong and the mainland on a Home Return Permit. Each time he crosses the border, people still look puzzled — they check his document, then look up again at this dark-skinned "Chinese citizen."

The people who appear in this sitting room are all from Somalia, Ali added — then immediately corrected himself: "from" is not quite accurate. Most were born in Somalia, but after the civil war broke out in 1991, Somalis scattered everywhere. If you asked the people in this sitting room to show their passports, you would find every one a different color. Most come from Kenya; some have emigrated to Europe or America.

For a time, the "Kenyans" doing trade in Guangzhou were actually all Somali immigrants. A few years ago, a native Kenyan came to Guangzhou; when he told Chinese traders he was from Kenya, they thought he was a fraud, because the Kenyans they knew "don't look like that."

Ali's passport was issued by the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region. He still remembers that when he went to the Somali embassy in Beijing in 2009 to process his papers, people said he was the first Somali in the world to become a Chinese citizen.

Ali was born in 1968 in Mogadishu, into a middle-class family; his father was an ear, nose and throat doctor. Somalia was still a "normal" African country then — a military dictatorship with no freedom of speech, but one that provided free health care and education.

The family once ran a fishing business, exporting Somali lobsters to Dubai. But as climate change brought by El Niño drove lobster yields lower and lower, and Somali pirates made their presence felt, the fishing business fell on hard times.

Ali went to Delhi, India to study, learning insurance at a university there. When he graduated and returned home in 1991, the civil war broke out, and the whole family moved to the United Arab Emirates and went into trading. Ali found a job with a trading company in faraway Hong Kong.

![The book cover of Chungking Mansions (Image / Amazon)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201806081117393HDjnBGSkIO48TEF.jpg-w600)

He lived in Chungking Mansions for five years, receiving Somalis from all over the world there. "Somalis have the minds of businessmen," he says — some of his compatriots who had put down roots in Kenya started out in business shipping goods from Hong Kong back to Nairobi and Mombasa.

It was also while doing research on Chungking Mansions that Mathews came to know Ali.

Compared with his older brothers who went to Europe, Ali had no trouble getting Hong Kong permanent residency — seven years of residence and a steady job. His wife, a childhood neighbor, had emigrated with her family to Canada years before.

Now his wife and their two children have moved to Malaysia — closer to Ali, and compared with Guangzhou, Kuala Lumpur offers more international schools at cheaper tuition. Every month Ali flies to Kuala Lumpur to reunite with his family, and the only "souvenir" he needs to bring is Chinese green tea.

But in Hong Kong, Somalis like Ali — employed by companies, even holding permanent residency — are extremely few. More are refugees who, to escape terrorism such as that of Al-Shabaab, made their way to Hong Kong to seek asylum, and Chungking Mansions became a hiding place for many of them.

Beyond work, Ali began helping some refugees as a translator, telling their stories to Hong Kong's immigration authorities. Compared with passing messages between refugees and the local government in his early years, Ali feels the "mediation" he now does between Chinese suppliers and Somali traders is no different — translating between two groups that are strangers to each other.

He once met an overstayer from Nigeria's Igbo people. The man's visa had expired and he had no legal status, yet he held considerable prestige among Chinese suppliers and the Igbo community; people believed this arbiter (whom Mathews calls a "peace-keeper" in his book) could settle these everyday disputes impartially.

But Ali says that compared with the Nigerian Igbo, Guangzhou's Somali community is small. Before 2013, Somali traders' business was in its prime, visa policy was relatively relaxed, and Somalis coming to Guangzhou to seek their fortunes surged — many middle-aged people with zero business experience quit their jobs and headed into the unknown. But now the resident population numbers only a few dozen.

In other words, the "*mafan*" Ali has to resolve as an arbiter are not that numerous.

### Ali's Sitting Room, When Business Is Not on the Table

Among the sitting room's topics related to daily life, the most important is death.

When someone dies in a foreign land, fellow countrymen are needed to help air-freight the body back to Somalia. The Muslim faith does not permit cremation, so Ali and the others help raise funds, process the death certificate and exit paperwork, and organize prayers. In 2016 and 2017, they shipped home, respectively, a businesswoman who had died of illness and a child who had died young.

Another topic is disease. When the Ebola virus broke out, it did not affect Ali and his compatriots — they stress that the virus came from West Africa, while they are East Africans. But they still have to deal with everyday injuries and illness; recently, a young man was hurt in a soccer match, and because he had only just arrived and hadn't saved any money yet, people needed to raise funds to pay for his surgery.

At eight in the evening, Ali's sitting room is full — people on the long sofa, folding chairs and footstools. Some have just come back from the Canton Fair in suits and shirts, others wear traditional robes. Some are curious about my Chinese face; others greet me simply in English, and after a "how are you" quickly switch back to Somali to chat with friends.

More sit quietly scrolling their phones. A young Kenyan-born Somali showed me a picture: an ancient Chinese man with a long beard, captioned as "Lao Tzu" ("Lao Tuz" — apparently a misspelling), paired with an English-language "quotation":

"If you are depressed, you are living in the past.

If you are anxious, you are living in the future.

Only when you are at peace are you truly living in the present."

He had posted the picture on his Facebook, where the likes quickly passed 100. He also uses WeChat, and his Moments feed often features similar "chicken-soup" motivational images — for instance, the silhouette of a runner in sunlight: "People know the me of yesterday; everything I do today will decide the me they see tomorrow."

### The Young People Who Don't Want to Become Ali

Ali keeps an exceptionally simple life in Guangzhou. He lives in Taojin, walks 15 minutes to the office every day, eats at the Turkish restaurants in Xiaobei, and even plays his weekend soccer matches nearby. Apart from his once-a-month trip to Kuala Lumpur, his life never leaves Xiaobei.

Compared with Ali, the younger traders have a far stronger impulse to "expand."

Abdinasir, 28, is a former employee of Ali. The year before last, he quit to strike out on his own, starting as a freelance "individual" broker; now his business has grown into freight forwarding too. His office is likewise in the Yidong Mansion, but he has moved his home from Xiaobei to Zhujiang New Town at the other end of Guangzhou. Ali jokes that anyone who can afford to live in Zhujiang New Town must be "doing quite well."

Zhujiang New Town looks like the opposite of Xiaobei. Where Xiaobei Road is dense with foot traffic, Zhujiang New Town is dense with newly built towers. "It's a bit like Lujiazui," says Abdinasir, who has been to Shanghai — the high-rises, squares and wide avenues, plus the occasional "developed-country faces" brushing past, all made him feel it is a modern city. The area was once an urban village in Guangzhou, redeveloped in recent years into the Tianhe CBD.

"No choice — I had to escape Xiaobei," Abdinasir explains his reason for moving. He had lived in Xiaobei for years. "It's too lively here; you can't even get a decent night's sleep." Work ends after 11 p.m., people arrange a soccer match, and by the time the game ends it's one or two in the morning; back at the apartment, the young roommates still watch Champions League matches together.

Last year he moved to Zhujiang New Town, renting an apartment for 6,800 yuan a month.

To expand his business, Abdinasir goes almost every day to the wholesale markets near Guangzhou Railway Station to look at goods. I met him at the door of Ali's office; the next day he showed me his "all-ears" sensitivity in the Liuhua market.

He speaks Somali, English, French and some simple Arabic. In elevators, at the prayer corners on the stairway landings of wholesale markets, or among the dense clusters of shops, Abdinasir waits for potential clients. By language and accent he can roughly tell where an African face comes from. If it's an unfamiliar East African, he occasionally offers some "free" advice — where to find suitable goods, which restaurants are good.

Abdinasir still remembers that five years ago, on his second day in Guangzhou, he got lost in the metro. He asked a passerby how to get from Guangzhou Railway Station back to Xiaobei; the man couldn't speak English. "I could feel he was already trying his best to slow down, but it was still Chinese! No matter how slowly he spoke, I couldn't understand." The passerby led him into the metro, but he still "lost his way" and spent a full hour and a half on the train.

"Now, I think I could make a pretty good taxi driver — I can find my way to most places in Guangzhou."

Young people like him have already jumped beyond Guangzhou, expanding their "business territories." In March he had just been to Wuzhen, placing an order with a huge garment factory. Last year, he helped a Somali in Norway buy a small kitchen appliance from Shandong — one that can make a traditional Muslim fried pastry.

"Shandong people are the most honest," Abdinasir says, comparing the suppliers he has met in different Chinese provinces. The Shandong one came via Alibaba; Abdinasir has never met him in person, yet the man could answer in great detail a long list of questions about the machine. When the goods reached Norway, his friend said it was the best machine he had ever bought.

### The City and "Freedom"

Near ten in the evening, the conversation in the sitting room turns to the "cost" of this city. Ali offers a figure: "If you can't earn 300,000 yuan a year, you might as well go home." But someone adds that in the early years it was easy money; these past two years, it has gotten harder and harder.

Today a shipping container can reach Mombasa from Guangzhou in just 16 days. But visa policy keeps tightening, and made-in-China is no longer cheap. "For those young people with no business experience, I would advise them not to rush to Guangzhou."

Some Somali traders nearing middle age complain that their children, after receiving higher education, would rather take jobs that look more "professional" — doctor, lawyer, professor.

"They (the kids) aren't willing to do this low-end globalization business — haggling, counting time... it all looks too trivial and too troublesome," a middle-aged Somali told me. Last year, he brought his nephew over from home to learn the trade bit by bit under him.

"Some people praise Guangzhou as an overly free city. I don't quite agree — it depends who you compare it with."

"People coming from Pakistan, Afghanistan or Iran would find Guangzhou too free. To them 'freedom' means alcohol, nightclubs, being able to queue up — by that measure, Guangzhou is completely free compared with where they came from. Some Africans feel that coming to Guangzhou lets them escape hunger; that, too, is a kind of 'freedom.'"

In this office, Ali and his compatriots try to maintain an orthodox Muslim way of life. Alcohol never appears here; for that reason, Ali once specifically reminded Mathews not to bring any wine inside. When doing fieldwork in Guangzhou, Mathews would occasionally have a drink "after work," which also helped him get to know more African traders and gather more interesting information.

"But not here," Ali explains. Very strict — just like how the TV in this sitting room only shows sports matches and the news, whether in Somali or on the BBC and CNN: "Only the news. No entertainment programs."

Ali feels Hong Kong is more like his "home." He still remembers that in 2010 he registered his company in Hong Kong, and the paperwork took only half an hour. "That's unimaginable in London" — where his two older brothers live, working as security guards.

His Hong Kong neighbors once used to remind him it was time to go to the Kowloon Mosque for prayers, and would jokingly ask: "Can I ask your God to bless my business with prosperity?"

In Guangzhou, Ali can likewise enter mosques freely; he once took Mathews to visit the Xianxian Mosque. But he still feels something is different — he can sense invisible red lines. Discussing religion is inopportune; discussing the community requires stressing that it is "informal."

Ali's "freedom" rests on his Hong Kong travel document. In the sitting room, a middle-aged man who had said nothing suddenly cut into our conversation: "Everyone wants a 'more privileged' passport, right? It just makes things more convenient for us. Deep down, we are still Somali."

He then explained what counts as being "Somali" — the same language, the same faith and culture, marrying only Somalis — and said he couldn't quite understand those Africans in Guangzhou who had married Chinese wives. "West Africans do that; we don't. A family should still be built on a shared cultural foundation."

What Ali worries about is his children. He wants to return to Somalia after retiring, or go to Hong Kong's outlying islands and live as a contented fisherman, but his children seem to have no interest at all in the unfamiliar homeland.

His two children, 13 and 11, were already a bit unhappy when they left Canada for Malaysia. "They'd probably rather be Canadians than Malaysians or Chinese. For us, Guangzhou is a place to make money, not a place to live."

"Of course, in Guangzhou you can escape Hong Kong's high housing prices — that counts as a kind of freedom too, at least for now."
