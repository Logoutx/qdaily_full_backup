QDaily will publish at least four articles examining the current state of the organic business in China. The first is about Sun Commune: the farm is the foundation of this business, yet it has not become the source of profit.

After receiving 600 visitors over the Qingming holiday, Sun Commune in Lin'an, Zhejiang, was very quiet.

The fallow rice paddies were blanketed with milk vetch and rapeseed flowers. No farmers were working; only a few chickens wandered the fields. The pigsty — which could almost count as a tourist attraction — was under repair and cleanup, and the hundred-plus black pigs known as "the happiest pigs in all of China" were nowhere to be seen.

![Photo: Li Lirong](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704240212350NQfAgKEIOLR9Prx.jpg-w600)

Three and a half years earlier, Shuangmiao Village in Taiyang Town, Lin'an, had been just as quiet, but back then half of the village's land lay abandoned. The population was mostly elderly and children, and there was barely any labor left to work the fields.

In 2013, after managing an organic farm in Moganshan for four years, Chen Wei decided to lease 700 mu of land in the Zhuyiwu valley of Shuangmiao Village to raise pigs. He had spent nearly a year searching for land before that, but nothing satisfied him. The ideal place had to meet certain conditions: a small valley where pigs could be raised separately; a clean drinking-water source for the pigs; land where organic feed could be grown; and a location within a two-hour drive of Hangzhou, where Chen Wei lived.

Li Xiaosheng, who once managed production at Sun Commune and now runs the Deguyuan organic farm in Zhengzhou, told QDaily that they decided to raise pigs because they found that the market already offered safe, healthy rice and vegetables — but no pork that met the same standard.

Chen Wei's parents are both professors at Zhejiang Agricultural University, and he says he grew up in the fields; after changing jobs more than a dozen times, he still wanted to work in agriculture. After four years of running an organic farm, the 50-year-old Chen Wei felt that running an all-purpose farm was too exhausting. "I'll just live here, raise some pigs, and earn a few million, a million a year — that seems fine too," he thought. So he teamed up with four other partners, invested more than 10 million yuan, and began preparing to raise organic pigs.

![Chen Wei](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424021308oEwS8rzse2QlfJKP.jpg-w600)

Just as with the conditions Chen Wei laid out when choosing the site, raising organic pigs requires that the surrounding environment — the soil and the water — also meet organic standards. That meant Chen Wei and his partners had to persuade every farmer in the area to switch to organic cultivation.

The farmers were not eager to farm the organic way. After decades of habitually using chemical fertilizers and pesticides, they believed organic farming simply couldn't grow anything or make money.

To persuade them, Chen Wei rented four minivans and took the whole village to see the farm he had previously managed. He also presented the Japanese model of agricultural operations — which he and Li Xiaosheng had learned during an inspection trip to Japan — where farmers grow only a few of the same crops for generations, leave sales to agricultural cooperatives, and earn no less than they would working in the city.

Chen Wei told QDaily that after hearing this, the farmers of Zhuyiwu said they too could grow only vegetables or raise only chickens, just like the Japanese farmers. So Sun Commune leased the land at 600–700 yuan per mu per year and took on the job of marketing the farm produce.

And so Chen Wei's business became an all-purpose farm again. Li Xiaosheng held meetings in Shuangmiao Village every week, explaining their organic philosophy and recruiting farmers willing to do organic agriculture, then assigning them to different positions according to the skills they had.

At the same time, Sun Commune abandoned its original idea of recruiting sales agents to sell pork. Chen Wei applied the subscription-delivery model he had practiced at his previous farm: customers prepay a certain fee, and the commune packs up the products and hands them to a courier company for home delivery.

Today, about 300 households find Sun Commune's approach convincing enough to prepay either 25,000 yuan or 16,800 yuan a year for the commune's weekly deliveries.

And Chen Wei has set a cap of 500 households for this model. "After that, membership will become very precious."

With prepayment, the farm can lock in demand before production, produce to order, and obtain the funds needed for production in advance.

But the pigs still had to be raised. Chen Wei made a surprising decision: he hired a designer to build the pigsty.

The designer was Chen Haoru, a professor at the China Academy of Art, whose other identity was a partner of Sun Commune. For him, designing the pigsty was an opportunity to put into practice the years of rural fieldwork and bamboo-material research he had done — but it also served a purpose beyond production: he hoped that, unlike an ordinary pigsty, it could represent the image of the commune. "Why did they ask me to do it? Mainly to create a new image, a brand."

Before construction began, Chen Haoru spent half a year on research and design, inviting local farmers and pig raisers to discuss their needs. The final pigsty was 8 meters wide, with no walls; because no earth could be excavated, the building was connected directly with stone blocks. Besides the pigs' dormitory area, there were feeding areas, an outdoor toilet, an outdoor exercise ground, and a swimming pool.

![Interior of the pigsty. Source: Chen Haoru](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424021752XY2kQEbqNRiDcCJt.jpg-w600)

![The swimming pool](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424022048K6TNA5OtID8ZvyfJ.JPG-w600)

Chen Haoru described the design as "detached from the modern construction industry system." He did not hire a construction crew or a cement plant; using local materials — bamboo, thatch, and cobblestones — he had local farmers build it, even inviting the whole village to help weave the thatched roof, something rarely seen in the countryside anymore. The final cost was only half that of an ordinary pigsty.

The pigsty was completed in May 2014 and, living up to expectations, became what the media called "China's most beautiful pigsty." Nearby villagers came to see it, city people came, out-of-towners came; Hangzhou media reported on it, [government officials came to inspect](http://www.yogeev.com/article/55339.html), and the Venice Biennale invited it to exhibit.

In 2015, the local government even built a road specifically for Sun Commune, making the stretch from the highway to the commune more suitable for driving.

It also brought Sun Commune its first customers — seventy or eighty households prepaid the 25,000-yuan annual membership package. Each week Sun Commune delivers 12 portions of vegetables, 2.4 jin of pork, 5 jin of rice, and one box of eggs, plus a chicken or a duck every month. Only later, after members complained that the portions were too large, did Sun Commune add the 16,800-yuan annual package with correspondingly reduced contents.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704240224209FJ7jlSubacKfAmg.jpg-w600)

Of course, these households were paying not just to eat "the happiest pigs in all of China."

The organic market in 2014 was not as big as it is now, but it was far better than before. Li Xiaosheng, who had been in organic agriculture since 2002, said that before 2008 organic was especially hard to do: the market was small and consumers didn't understand it. Only in 2010 did the organic market begin to expand, and by the time they founded Sun Commune, "the organic market was already doing pretty well."

"Back then it was like: I want to support a farmer — you see he can't sell his stuff, so I'll buy some. Maybe you don't know anything at all about product standards." This is how Shi Yan, founder of the Shared Harvest Farm, described organic consumers in 2006. At the time she was pursuing a PhD in agricultural development at Renmin University of China, and she observed that farmers who wanted to go organic found that organic products had neither a market nor a viable delivery channel.

In 2009, Shi Yan helped found China's first CSA farm — Little Donkey Farm. CSA stands for community-supported agriculture: in simple terms, members prepay part of the cost to support local producers, and the farm delivers the harvest regularly. The relationship is more one of mutual trust and cooperation than the buying-and-selling we are used to.

By 2012, Shi Yan had founded her own Shared Harvest Farm, also run on the CSA model. She told QDaily that around 2014 she clearly sensed that consumers were interested in organic products or knew something about them — and were no longer in the mindset of doing something akin to charity. By her estimate, China now has roughly 1,000 CSA farms.

![Shared Harvest Farm. Source: Shi Yan](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424023256ftUwF0P3kng9EpXV.jpg-w600)

Just take a stroll through any supermarket and you can easily feel that organic products cost considerably more than non-organic ones. And this is determined by the very standards of organic farming: giving up chemical synthetics such as pesticides, fertilizers, and hormones means lower yields and higher costs in production alone — so the selling price naturally goes up.

And in China, more and more people can afford organic food. According to the National Bureau of Statistics, [per capita disposable income of urban residents was 11,769.6 yuan in 2006](http://data.stats.gov.cn/easyquery.htm?cn=B01&zb=A0501&sj=2016D); by last year that figure had [grown to 33,616 yuan](http://www.stats.gov.cn/tjsj/sjjd/201701/t20170120_1456174.html).

On top of that, demand for organic food — the kind that looks safer — keeps growing. Endless food-safety scandals have made Chinese consumers increasingly distrustful of the food they buy, and at such a moment, organic food — free of all kinds of chemical synthetics and non-GMO, the kind Europe and America's middle classes are chasing — looks considerably safer.

A report from the consultancy Mintel last August showed that in China, 56% of organic product buyers cite "free of harmful substances" as their purchase motive, followed by "organic food production is environmentally friendly" (49%).

When summarizing why their customers consume organic, every organic farm interviewed by QDaily mentioned food safety first — and many customers hope to guarantee their children's diet.

Nearly all of Sun Commune's roughly 300 member households, for instance, have children. Even Chen Haoru, who prefers to talk about Sun Commune's impact on the countryside, mentioned that the architects and professors around him became commune members because they felt the food problem was "almost beyond saving."

In reality, in this environment of widespread distrust of food safety, organic food has not been an exception either.

Jiang Yiying, who lives in Hangzhou, told QDaily that before becoming a Sun Commune member she had long insisted on buying food labeled "organic," but she doesn't fully trust organic standards that require certification by specialized bodies, worrying that today's air, water, and soil pollution will seep into food — and whether certification takes those factors into account "is hard to say."

She is not the only one who thinks this way. Shi Yan said many people believe "the air, water, and soil in Beijing — even the water and soil of all of China — are polluted; how could there still be anything organic?"

In such an atmosphere, whether a farm spends money to get organic certification from an institution seems to become less important. In a post on its WeChat official account, Sun Commune said it would not pay for organic certification: "Real organic is in people's hearts." Sun Commune, a 1.5-hour drive from Hangzhou, limits its reach to the Yangtze River Delta region. Chen Wei expects members to drive themselves to visit the farm, and to believe in the organic safety of the products after seeing the site in person and chatting with the local farmers.

"Our certification is on the farmers' faces," Chen Wei said, explaining the reliability of his business. Beside every plot of land and every livestock area, Sun Commune has put up posters of the person in charge, printed with their name, photo, responsibilities, and personal motto. What's more, the packaging of every product a member receives carries a photo of the corresponding producer.

![Photo: Li Lirong](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424025808rB5iplYQ9KTSy08O.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704240234089eJV0NTY2qWf1EnU.jpg-w600)

Both Chen Wei and Chen Haoru believe this way of naming the producer is better than organic certification. Chen Haoru said: "You city people come to the countryside, take part in farm work, see these people, and then the farmers' portraits are pasted on the packaging of the rice and pork — the trust that creates cannot be replaced by any certification system. Honestly, nobody really trusts that kind of system."

Of course, some farms do see organic certification as a basic standard. Cao Naizhen, business development director of BioFarm, an organic farm in Shanghai, stressed that they are "not doing it to show off that certificate to customers," but believe organic certification carries some credibility and treat it as 60 points of a farm's trustworthiness score; the other 40 points are "a proof the ecology gives us."

Sun Commune has a mechanism for supervising farmers and guaranteeing product quality, including centralized purchasing of seeds and other production materials, regular random testing, and pulling any single product that gets more than 10 complaints in one quarter; in a WeChat official account post they also once said they had fired a vegetable farmer who used pesticides.

But most of the time, Sun Commune prefers to show the harmonious rather than the strict side of the commune. Chen Wei is very proud of the commune's good relationship with its farmers: "We don't make money; our farmers always make money. Our first priority is protecting the farmers' interests, so the farmers also have a good reputation," and he often mentions hoping to give farmers high incomes so as to attract their children who work in the city back home.

Sun Commune regards WeChat as its most important channel of promotion. In its official account it doesn't mention selling products; instead it shows the commune's scenery, farm work, and relations with farmers. In the first two years, Chen Xinwen, who ran Sun Commune's WeChat operations, often showed members and potential customers the progress of farm work at the commune, believing this could let them take part and gradually build trust.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424024903aGqBbljt70iMr5gR.jpg-w600)

Chen Xinwen believes users pay them "with payment (sharing) in WeChat Moments." For Sun Commune, where about 60–70% of customers come from friend referrals, members sharing in their Moments became an effective form of promotion. Another Hangzhou-based member, Luo Hua, told QDaily that she first learned about Sun Commune from posts about it that a friend shared in Moments from time to time.

Clearly, not every family can — or is willing to — pay annual membership fees of 25,000 or 16,800 yuan. Chen Wei said their pricing is cost-based: the profit margin on membership packages is around 40%; vegetables, pork, and rice are priced at 15, 50, and 15 yuan per jin respectively, while eggs are 3 yuan each.

Li Xiaosheng explained that Sun Commune's pricing is not high. They had learned from past experience: at the previous farm, they priced products according to the market price of organic food. Prices were too high, sales were poor, and the unsold products added further to production costs, pushing prices ever higher — a vicious cycle.

By Li Xiaosheng's account, the cost ratio of organic rice to ordinary rice is 3:1; for organic versus ordinary vegetables it is between 2:1 and 3:1; and organic animal husbandry costs about the same as ordinary animal husbandry. He does not believe organic agriculture is more profitable: "You can't make money growing vegetables alone." All the organic farms he has seen in China are based on agriculture and then integrate secondary and tertiary industries.

Shi Yan, whose farm has a profit margin of around 10%, holds a similar view. She said that, as far as she knows of current organic production, as long as it is produced to genuine organic standards, "there is no possibility of windfall profits." Moreover, even with a 10% net profit, Shi Yan still needs to put the money back into production and maintenance to prepare for the coming year.

Under this model, Chen Wei set a membership cap of around 500 households, and Sun Commune recently reversed its "no sales agents" stance: it now sells individual products such as pork and eggs through both its own WeChat store and agents, with home delivery afterwards.

Sun Commune did this because organic vegetables are markedly more complex and unstable than other farm products. Chen Wei said they grow only seasonal vegetables; the varieties are relatively few in summer and winter, and some varieties can be wiped out by pests and disease. "It's truly local; there's no rich variety of products." Some members declined to renew the following year because there were too few vegetable varieties.

And vegetables are not that profitable a category either: at Sun Commune, the profit margin on vegetables is around 30%, while pork and eggs are around 40%.

Chen Wei told QDaily that China's organic consumers are still very limited and the market demand is not that big: "About 90% of organic companies don't make money." Right now, he is developing other, more profitable businesses — such as nature education, which started last year: letting city children come to Sun Commune to play and attend summer and winter camps. A year in, it has begun to turn a profit, whereas agriculture has yet to make money.

In Chen Wei's vision, Sun Commune will become an "integrated complex" built on agriculture. They are now preparing to build tent hotels and treehouses, and may in the future get into the cultural-creative industry, using the various natural materials in Sun Commune's environment to make products. Other businesses will drive food consumption, but agriculture's share of revenue will keep declining.

![The "Big White House," used mainly as a nature education venue](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424025021fJFv5e1VniLO3lKx.jpg-w600)

At the same time, Chen Wei also plans to "export the Sun Commune model" or run a chain brand. He did not address whether the fact that Shuangmiao Village's Zhuyiwu valley has been able to take initial shape is a fluke or a replicable model.

Chen Wei, who repeatedly mentioned that the organic industry has seen no change or breakthrough in recent years, denied being pessimistic about the organic food business: "I think the future will be great. Right now we (need to) blaze a trail and work out a model."

When asked whether Sun Commune had any model it imitated or borrowed from, the three people who had worked, or were still working, at Sun Commune all quickly answered no. They agreed that Sun Commune is a completely new model and that everything had to be figured out on their own; Chen Haoru even described it as "a kind of social experiment tinged with idealism."

As for whether Sun Commune's model can become a "representative" of Chinese organic agriculture — perhaps we should wait until it has successfully replicated itself before drawing a conclusion.
