### Agenda Setters

**[The New Yorker editorial: Starbucks and the problem of "white space"](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2018/06/04/starbucks-and-the-issue-of-white-space)**

Three years ago, Elijah Anderson, a sociology professor at Yale, published a paper titled "The White Space," exploring the complex racial dynamics of majority-white urban environments. "A city's public spaces, workplaces, and neighborhoods gradually come to be conceived as white spaces, black spaces, and cosmopolitan spaces," he wrote, and in white space black people tend to be "absent, marginal, or presumed not to belong."

A recent string of incidents has borne this out. Beyond the now-famous April 12 [incident](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52266.html) in which Starbucks employees called the police on two black men who hadn't ordered anything but wanted to use the restroom, an employee at a New Jersey gym called the police days later, claiming two black men were using the equipment without paying (they had in fact paid); a few weeks after that, a woman in California reported three black women acting suspiciously to the police, when they had merely been carrying bags out of the Airbnb they had rented; and in early May, a white Yale student called the police saying someone was behaving strangely on campus, when the black graduate in question was simply dozing in a chair.

Thousands of people of color have shared on social media how they are treated in white space. The so-called "implicit racism" of late seems to have become the norm, which owes both to its value in academia and to the way it explains certain racial problems. People's usual understanding of racism derives from the vicious mob that drove a car into pedestrians in Charlottesville. But that bar is set so high that even a New York prosecutor who, just last week, threatened in a restaurant to have Spanish-speaking employees deported could later put out a statement insisting he is not a racist.

Implicit racism detaches racism from overt, open malice. Lately there has been discussion of whether implicit racism also exists among the police; as in the Starbucks incident, the police have become a kind of vector by which to gauge whether an individual citizen harbors discriminatory attitudes. But the crux of the problem is not "whether police officers carry bias into the performance of their duties"; it is "whether the call that brought them there was itself the product of bias." What makes this incident worth noting is not whether a company like Starbucks can erase employee bias with a single training session; it is the more worrying fact it lays bare — that this kind of biased attitude has become so common in America that a company now needs to shoulder the responsibility of eliminating it from its own workspace.

In 1883, the Supreme Court ruled the Civil Rights Act of 1875 unconstitutional in the Civil Rights Cases. The 1875 act held that everyone should have equal rights to enjoy public accommodations, which could not refuse to serve citizens on grounds of race, color, or previous condition of servitude; it was grounded in the Fourteenth Amendment, but the Court held at the time that the amendment's equal protection clause restricted only government action, not private conduct, and that racial discrimination between private parties was not unconstitutional. Justice Harlan issued his famous dissent: "Today it is the colored race which is denied, by corporations and individuals wielding public authority, rights fundamental in their freedom and citizenship. At some future time, it may be that some other race will fall under the ban of that discrimination."

Justice Harlan foresaw a regime bent on turning America into a white space. Implicit racism always finds a way to become explicit, unmistakable discrimination.

### 24-Hour News Makers

**[Ireland's referendum has repealed one of the world's strictest abortion bans.](http://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-44270098)**

On the evening of the 26th in Dublin, Barry Ryan, the referendum's returning officer, announced that nearly two-thirds of Irish voters had voted to repeal the Eighth Amendment of the constitution, backing the legalization of abortion.

The final turnout was 64.1%, higher than the figure for the legalization of same-sex marriage three years earlier. Of those who voted, two-thirds (66.4%) voted Yes to repeal, while 33.6% chose to keep the amendment. With the repeal side prevailing, the Dublin government will now amend the abortion law and loosen its restrictions: the original Eighth Amendment gave the pregnant woman and the fetus equal legal status, and abortion was illegal except in extreme circumstances — when the woman's life was at risk or the fetus had died in the womb. Now, women up to 12 weeks of pregnancy may end the pregnancy with a doctor's certification.

Once Ireland's High Court certifies the referendum as valid and it is signed by the president and the prime minister, Health Minister Simon Harris will seek cabinet support on Tuesday and then set about drafting the new bill, which is to take effect by the end of the year.

Repealing the abortion ban was the choice of Ireland's mainstream across society. Prime Minister Leo Varadkar said the result marked the victorious conclusion of "a quiet revolution" and that the public "trusts and respects women to make their own choices"; Foreign Minister Simon Coveney stressed that both the turnout and every constituency showed a Yes majority, proving the result was not "Dublin versus the rest of rural Ireland"; and Yes campaign supporters said it was a day to remember for Irish women, a sign that the whole country rejects treating women as second-class citizens.

Like the same-sex marriage referendum three years ago, the result is seen as a seismic shift in Irish society. Pope Francis will visit in three months and will find a country very different from the one the previous pope saw — John Paul II traveled to Ireland in 1979, and four years later Ireland wrote the Eighth Amendment into its constitution. Since then, many Irish women were forced to go abroad, especially to Britain, for abortions; more than 3,000 women made the abortion journey each year, and since 1983 a cumulative 100,000 women had traveled abroad to terminate pregnancies.

"This is the day we said 'no more,'" the prime minister added. "No more doctors telling their patients there is nothing that can be done for them; no more lonely journeys across the Irish Sea; no more secrets and shame and the isolation they bring."

After this vote, Northern Ireland is the only region in Britain and Ireland that still bans abortion (women who have an illegal abortion there can, in theory, face life imprisonment). Irish citizens carried signs of support reading "The North is next" at referendum rallies, but Theresa May said on Sunday that she opposes members of parliament voting on their own initiative to repeal Northern Ireland's abortion ban, arguing it should be decided by the local government in Belfast. She will face enormous parliamentary pressure.

### What Else Happened in the World

**[Alan Bean, the fourth person to walk on the moon, died on the 27th at age 86](http://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-44268960)**. Alan Bean was a U.S. Navy test pilot, joined NASA in 1963, and flew on the Apollo 12 lunar landing mission in 1969. In his later years he became a well-regarded artist, painting a number of works inspired by space. In human history, 24 people have flown to the moon, 12 have walked on it, and 4 are still alive. Of the first four moonwalkers, only 88-year-old Aldrin survives.

**[Kim Jong-un holds "firm conviction" that the Singapore summit with the U.S. should proceed as planned](http://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-44268964)**. Prospects for the U.S.–North Korea talks shifted repeatedly over the weekend. After Trump said last Friday that he was canceling the summit set for the 12th, he then said he hoped the meeting would take place, and the U.S. team flew to North Korea to prepare for the summit with the North Korean side. On the 27th, Kim Jong-un and Moon Jae-in met unexpectedly, agreeing that the Panmunjom Declaration should be implemented as soon as possible to achieve denuclearization of the peninsula; both sides expressed hope that the U.S.–North Korea meeting would go smoothly.

**[Italy's prospective prime minister gives up on forming a government; new elections may be needed](http://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-44275010)**. Giuseppe Conte, Italy's presumptive new prime minister, abandoned his effort to form a government on the 27th after the president rejected his choice for economy minister — meaning Italy may be forced to hold another general election this year. Since the election on March 4, Italy has been without a government for nearly three months.

**[Germany's far-right AfD party confronts opponents twice its number in Berlin](http://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-44273617)**. The anti-immigrant far-right party Alternative for Germany marched in Berlin on Sunday, more than 5,000 people chanting "We are the people," while roughly twice as many counter-demonstrators shouted "Nazis out." Police used pepper spray to stop the two sides from clashing. It was the largest rally since AfD became the biggest opposition party, after it won 13% of the vote in the general election at the start of the year.

**[A South African team has invented ZiBiPen, a cheap alternative to the EpiPen adrenaline auto-injector](http://www.iflscience.com/health-and-medicine/medical-engineers-have-built-a-epipen-replacement-that-costs-16-a-shot/)** — the injector costs $80, lasts five years, and only needs a $16 cartridge replaced after each injection. The EpiPen costs $600 for a two-pack, lasts only 18 months, and can be used just once.

### Somebody Said

> "If they lied about that, who knows what else they've kept from us."

**[So said a believer in the "flat earth" at Britain's first Flat Earth Convention.](https://www.theguardian.com/global/2018/may/27/is-the-earth-pancake-flat-among-the-flat-earthers-conspiracy-theories-fake-news)**

More than 200 people paid to attend the convention, which consisted of a week of lectures and study groups where attendees could discuss and exchange flat-earth theories with like-minded people "open to ideas." NASA has become a punch line here, treated as shorthand for elite-orchestrated conspiracy.

Flat-earth belief has long thrived on YouTube. Believers hold that the earth is round and flat like a pancake, ringed on all sides by Antarctica — an impassable wall of ice — with a dome above it. In the early phase of belief, multiple theories and ideas tangle and contradict one another in the mind, but believers soon come to feel a sense of "awakening," convinced they have seen a truth that others cannot grasp. "It feels great, and you want to tell the whole world at once — but what you don't expect is that the world isn't ready to accept it."

Chris French, a psychology professor at the University of London, commented: "Science isn't perfect, and it's good that some people question authority, because in a democracy you need that. But you should also have the critical-thinking skills to analyze evidence... certain psychological crises can produce dramatic so-called insights."

Conspiracy theorists are bound together by their rejection of, and contempt for, orthodox theory; that rejection is a way to regain a sense of personal control, and also an attempt to belong to a collective. "It's like a coping mechanism," said Rebecca Ownens, a psychology lecturer at the University of Sunderland. "Believing 'I can still be in control of this' — once they have that awareness, they feel they understand something, and the chaos of real life seems to have an explanation."

### You Might Also Be Interested In

**[Repin's famous painting *Ivan the Terrible Killing His Son* has been vandalized once again.](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2018/may/27/famous-russian-painting-of-ivan-the-terrible-seriously-damaged-in-pole-attack)**

Ilya Repin, the great Russian painter of the late 19th and early 20th centuries and a leading figure of the Peredvizhniki (the Wanderers), created the painting *Ivan the Terrible and His Son Ivan on 16 November 1581*, which depicts the scene in which Ivan the Terrible, in a fit of rage during an argument, strikes and accidentally kills his son with his scepter.

The painting was on display at the Tretyakov Gallery. On the evening of the 25th, just as the gallery was about to close, a man pushed past staff into the exhibition hall, broke the glass display case with a metal barrier, and damaged the canvas in several places. The gallery said the impact shattered the thick glass protecting the painting from changes in temperature and humidity, that the canvas suffered three tears in the middle of the prince's body, and that the original frame was also damaged by the glass — though fortunately the faces and hands of the tsar and the prince remain intact.

The suspect told police he had drunk 100 ml of vodka and was not of sound mind at the time. His reason for destroying the painting was that he believed the history it depicts is incorrect. The court found that his actions caused at least 500,000 rubles in damage.

*Ivan the Terrible Killing His Son* had already been vandalized once before, in 1913, when a mentally ill man slashed it three times with a knife — making it the first painting in Russian art history to be attacked. Repin, who was still alive at the time, spent 12 years personally restoring it.

**[Hugging calves has become the latest trend for modern people to heal their minds.](https://www.bbc.co.uk/bbcthree/article/f3077982-4f1c-4b40-81be-7f96c4235334)**

A farm in New York has opened its services to people seeking emotional comfort — 90 minutes of interaction with a herd of cows. They explain that cows are "sensitive and intuitive animals" that "can sense what you're thinking and detect whether you're happy, sad, down, anxious, or excited, and they respond on their own judgment." Cows are said to help people relax, sharpen perception, build confidence, and overcome fear. Since cows' body temperature is higher than humans', leaning against one is like "resting on a giant, furry hot-water bottle."

However, animal behavior expert Mario Becker has raised doubts — cows are not there to be hugged, and hugging humans does not fit a cow's essential needs. At the same time, the New York service doesn't come cheap: 90 minutes alone with the cows costs $300 — about $2.5 a minute. Services in Europe are cheaper, at 50 euros for three hours.
