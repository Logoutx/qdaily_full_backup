@@QD_BODY@@
Since taking over in 2014, Satya Nadella has led Microsoft's aggressive transformation toward the cloud era, doubling the company's market value in three years and adding $300 billion.

When Satya Nadella took over as CEO of Microsoft in early 2014, the company was in serious trouble. Global PC shipments, after decades of steady growth, had peaked and begun to decline. Quarterly PC shipments still numbered in the tens of millions, but they paled in comparison with the hundreds of millions of smartphones — the new darling device of the mobile and cloud eras — shipped each quarter.

Fewer PC shipments meant fewer Microsoft software suites preinstalled on them, and therefore less royalty revenue for Microsoft. Meanwhile, as PCs and smartphones traded places as end-user devices, Microsoft found itself at a disadvantage in the battle for operating systems: measured against Windows, usage of Google's Android and Apple's iOS was rising sharply.

Microsoft tried to catch up in smartphones and thereby restore the prestige of Windows. In 2012, Nokia announced that it would adopt Windows on its new smartphones, which seemed to herald victory for this strategy. In September 2013, Microsoft announced the acquisition of Nokia. Yet what looked like a marriage of two giants produced a pairing of two weaklings: Windows Phone sales were dismal, and in the race for operating-system market share, Microsoft harvested nothing but the bitterness of defeat against rivals iOS and Android. It proved once again that the market has no room for a third player, and that trying to overtake others on the curves of a fixed track is a near-certain losing proposition.

Having lost the opening move in the mobile era, Microsoft's only way out of decline was to start a new game on a new market or battlefield. For Satya Nadella, that new battlefield was the cloud platform and cloud services. PC sales were falling, but the consumers connecting to the internet through devices, sensors, and the Internet of Things would soon reach 3 billion. Microsoft therefore had to set its sights on winning billions of connected devices rather than lamenting a shrinking market. The first task was to meet the cloud-service needs of billions of customers, whatever phone or platform they chose. If Microsoft could reverse its fortunes in the cloud game, it could in turn recover lost ground in the mobile game: providing device-management solutions for the mobile world and giving business customers the technology they needed to protect and manage Windows, iOS, and Android devices.

To that end, Satya Nadella set a $20 billion goal for the cloud business, which required persuading the entire company to shift its gaze from the suite products that had once made Microsoft the world's richest company toward the far more promising cloud services — the Azure platform and Office 365.

Satya Nadella's career at Microsoft made him one of the earliest leaders on the executive team to understand the cloud era. His experience building Bing while in charge of the search-engine division taught him where the information industry was headed: online, cloud-based businesses. In practice, the Bing team came to understand that if all search queries arrived at a single server, that server would crash; but if the queries were spread evenly across a network of servers, the network's enormous computing power could deliver relevant search results to consumers instantly — that is the core of the cloud business, and its primary access device is the phone rather than the desktop computer.

Amid fierce competition, the search team also realized that the key to successful search lies in the aggressive use of machine learning. A good search engine must know how to do two things at once: identify the intent of users searching the web, and then match it with accurate information drawn from the web. This form of machine learning is an important form of data analysis and the foundation of artificial intelligence. It is no exaggeration to say that Bing became the training ground for Microsoft's cloud services.

In his book *Hit Refresh: The Quest to Rediscover Microsoft's Soul and Imagine a Better Future for Everyone*, Satya Nadella recalls that shifting the company's focus from the enormous server-and-tools business — whose steady stream of revenue paid everyone's salaries — to the trivial cloud business, which had almost nothing to it, was no easy task. It required persuading the server-and-tools division to optimize its products and technologies for cloud services, rather than, as before, for proprietary, self-built servers inside organizations.

Rather than make a clean, one-stroke break, Satya Nadella adopted a transitional strategy. Although the company embraced a cloud-first strategy, building on Microsoft's server strengths it chose to offer customers hybrid-cloud solutions — private, on-premises servers combined with the public cloud. This both motivated the existing server-and-tools teams and accommodated the transition to a cloud-first strategy.

But once the timing was ripe, Satya Nadella decisively promoted the cloud-platform project Windows Azure from a marginal initiative to a major one within the server-and-tools division, and renamed it Microsoft Azure to demonstrate the company's resolve to transform as a whole. In the process, Microsoft rebuilt its business, management, and technology architecture around the cloud-first strategy.

Beyond the internal reorganization and technical restructuring, the cloud-first strategy also meant rearranging external competitive and cooperative relationships. Microsoft took the initiative to support the Linux operating system it had long suppressed, offering first-class support for Linux on Azure — the same Linux on which Google had built Android to put Windows on the back foot. It also meant forming partnerships with old rivals such as Apple. Apple, of course, was happy to build such a relationship for mutual benefit: Apple wanted Microsoft to help optimize Office 365 for the new iPad Pro. Partnering with Apple helped carry the value of Office products to everyone, and Apple in turn promised to showcase the power of Office on its iOS platform — which further cemented Microsoft's standing as a top-tier Apple developer.

In the summer of 2015, Microsoft released Office apps that ran across all devices, including the iPhone. Today, a device not connected to the cloud is almost unthinkable; consumer apps such as Office 365 and LinkedIn live in the cloud. On the cloud technology that is now changing the world, Microsoft is already in the lead — yet only a few years ago such an outcome seemed hard to imagine.

The cloud-platform strategy opened up a blue ocean of business for Microsoft. As products grow ever more intelligent, all companies are moving toward digitalization. By one forecast, the number of "connected things" in use will reach 20 billion to 50 billion by 2020 — a major opportunity for every company's digital transformation, and in turn a major business opportunity for Microsoft.

In Satya Nadella's view, if other companies establish strong market positions through their products or devices, Microsoft must find clever ways to partner with them, so that Microsoft's products can take up residence on their popular platforms. Classic applications such as Outlook, Skype, Word, Excel, and PowerPoint, along with newer mobile apps such as Dynamics, OneNote, OneDrive, Sway, and PowerBI, all found a home on Apple's iOS platform. And the reverse holds as well: General Electric, which has grown into a mature digital company, put its industrial internet operating system, the Predix platform, onto Microsoft's cloud platform to connect industrial equipment, analyze data, and provide real-time insights.

Since taking over in 2014, Satya Nadella has led Microsoft's aggressive transformation toward the cloud era, doubling the company's market value in three years and adding $300 billion, pulling the company out of decline in one stroke. Now, having secured its footing in the cloud era, Microsoft has put forward a "beyond the cloud" strategy, turning its attention and resources toward three frontier technologies it sees as representing key trends: mixed reality, artificial intelligence, and quantum computing.

The secret to every successful transformation is the same, as is the recipe for every failed one. A company that misses a wave is doomed if it chases the leaders along the same fixed track; to rise again, it must open a new battlefield. Microsoft missed the smartphone era, and after the disappointing Nokia acquisition, it resolutely chose to go straight to the cloud platform, serving all mobile and connected devices — achieving a transformation beyond expectations.

Beyond the "intangible" ingredients such as changes in mindset and culture, Microsoft's success rested on the data and computing power accumulated over the long term, the enormous financial investment in the strategic transformation, the cajoling reform of vested interests and old profit centers — and the willingness and capacity to take on enormous risk, because a company so big and established could absorb the losses if the transformation went wrong. Seen this way, it is easy to understand why the overwhelming majority of transformations are bound to fail: a shrinking old profit model that is hard to change, plus piecemeal, me-too innovation built on models that others have already played out.
