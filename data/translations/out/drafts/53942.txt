“Pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will”

Media studies scholar [Bingchun Meng](http://www.lse.ac.uk/media-and-communications/people/academic-staff/bingchun-meng) is an associate professor at the London School of Economics and Political Science (LSE) and deputy head of its Department of Media and Communications. For many years, her research has centered on the relationships among institutions, technology, markets, and social class.

In analyzing cases such as [copyright](http://eprints.lse.ac.uk/78394/), [Super Girl](http://eprints.lse.ac.uk/36989/), [Double 11](http://eprints.lse.ac.uk/75196/7/Meng_Patriarchal%20capitalism.pdf), and [spoof videos](http://eprints.lse.ac.uk/36988/), she has examined how power operates in contemporary communication practices — from the micro to the macro, spanning government, big corporations, and individuals. In 2018 — the year [the founder of the digital rights organization EFF passed away](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/50084.html), [the Meta–Cambridge Analytica scandal broke](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/52022.html), and ["Goddess Day" was invented to boost consumption](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/23500.html) — these questions became increasingly impossible to avoid.

Her college years began almost simultaneously with China's market transformation. Deng Xiaoping's "Southern Tour" became China's economic engine and official philosophy; ideological debate faded from public discussion, replaced by a pragmatism keyed to efficiency, technology, and wealth. Real estate was rising, the internet was brewing, and class divisions were rapidly forming. Rarely had people felt "opportunity" so close at hand — even though history shows that the ones who actually "seized the opportunity" were only a small minority.

The stories of this "minority" were enough to create a myth of optimism. It is a myth about "development," which lends full-speed-ahead progress an unchallengeable allure; it is also a myth about "consumption," which places goods at the center of life, tailors people's desires and lifestyles to measure, and rewrites human nature the way code is rewritten. Those who missed the train, those who feel they didn't benefit, those who are no longer happy — all are blamed for their own stupidity and incompetence.

Bingchun Meng tries to explain media culture in a consumerist context, and the social conditions that create this media culture.

In many ways, Meng is more restrained than some — for instance, she calls Chai Jing's *Under the Dome* "a very limited act of righteousness by the middle class out of self-preservation." Nor does she agree with the elitist position that is "intellectually most convenient and morally most advantageous."

We talked with her about some "serious questions related to life," and we hope these condensed but not brief answers will be helpful to you.

### 1. The death of the public, and the commodification of leisure time and creativity

**Q: Since the 1960s — Guy Debord, for example — Western Marxism has had deep anxieties about the "society of the spectacle" constructed by mass media such as advertising and television. What keywords would you use to describe today's cultural landscape?**

**M:** Western Marxism's critique of the culture industry did not begin in the 1960s; it can be traced further back to the essay co-written by Adorno and Horkheimer of the Frankfurt School ("The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception"). As Jewish scholars who fled to the United States during World War II, they suddenly confronted America's mass culture industry and were deeply shocked. They thought they had escaped Nazi ideological control, but in America they realized that commercialized mass media's control over people's thinking and its power to shape culture seemed no weaker than the fascist propaganda they had experienced in Germany.

If we follow the line of Guy Debord's *The Society of the Spectacle* to describe the contemporary cultural landscape, the most important keyword would probably be commodification. When that book talks about the "spectacle," it means that the commodification logic of media products is omnipresent, all-encompassing, penetrating every aspect of society. Media products and media content are produced as commodities and circulate in the market in commodity form. "Produced as a commodity" means it is made for profit; "circulating in commodity form" means it is assigned a price. And a media product made for profit must pursue the maximization of profit.

But in the context of modern digital media, commodification has gone beyond the commodification of products and content to include many other dimensions. Take "leisure time." In the 1970s, the Canadian political economist Dallas Smythe proposed the concept of the "audience commodity" — at the time he was still discussing commercial television. He felt that North American commercial television content looks free, but in fact it sells the audience's attention and leisure time to advertisers as a commodity. When the audience thinks it is watching a free media product, relaxing, "leisure" actually becomes work done for the TV station: leisure time is commodified, because the station charges advertisers based on ratings.

In a digital media environment, the commodification of leisure time is even more obvious. All your activity on social media leaves a digital trace; all of it can be recorded, packaged, and categorized by social platforms as data used to predict all kinds of user behavior, and sold to advertisers and political marketing firms — the recent Meta–Cambridge Analytica scandal is a very clear example. This commodification process is extremely pervasive and extremely hidden; you don't even notice it exists.

Beyond the commodification of leisure time, creativity has been commodified too. Look at many so-called user-generated content platforms, like YouTube. At first users produced content, and the corresponding discourse was "the creativity of the masses has been unleashed; you can make your own media products; the threshold for entering media production and circulation has been greatly lowered; everyone can tell their own story."

But this quickly entered the logic of commodification: content users post on the platform brings traffic to the platform, greatly increasing the platform's advertising revenue; the more active users are on the platform, the more data the platform obtains, which in turn leads to the further commodification of personal information and privacy. Before digital platforms appeared, such information and privacy had not been classified, managed, and traded as commodities.

So you can see that as digital media plays an increasingly important role in everyday life, the logic of commodification keeps expanding — expanding into domination over leisure time, over individual creativity, and over personal information and privacy.

The consequence of this is the death of the public. Under the logic of ubiquitous commodification, the relationship between individuals and cultural products becomes entirely that of consumer and commodity. What kind of relationship is this? First, the consumer needs a certain purchasing power, because cultural products have a price — pay per view, pay per service; second, in developed capitalist societies, the relationship between consumer and commodity is one of disposability. This goes back to capitalism's fundamental contradiction — the relationship between consumption and production. The media or information products we see today are overproduced: far more content is produced than people can consume. We know that one of the origins of capitalist economic crises is that when there is overproduction and insufficient consumption, capitalists cannot obtain enough profit to reinvest and reproduce — and then the chain breaks.

So in *The Condition of Postmodernity*, David Harvey strongly emphasizes the relationship between culture and ideology and the capitalist political-economic system. You need an ideology to sustain consumption capacity; you need to manufacture a discourse that expands and intensifies the desire to consume; you need to tell people that everything — defining the self (the marketing discourse of brands and individuality), meeting social role expectations (being a good mother means making the right consumption choices for the whole family), solving any predicament in life (buying emotional and companionship services) — must be achieved through consumption. Today's entire culture industry keeps reinforcing this logic: you must upgrade and renew, just like with the iPhone, and this requires a consumerist ideology to constantly encourage consumption. In this way, when individuals imagine their relationship to media and cultural products, they naturally imagine it as the relationship between consumer and commodity.

Moreover, digital media has also brought about the problem of mediatization. The logic of media has penetrated every aspect of everyday cultural life. In a society with democratic elections, a politician has to design a policy platform for their campaign and find every possible way to persuade their constituency. Many of the people who run election campaigns for politicians are now senior media professionals, because political elections cannot do without mass media; all communication with voters must take media logic into account. Often what matters is not what your policy is, but what image you want to project to voters. You have to consider which platforms to place ads on, and what those platforms' main audiences are. So considerations of media logic have already penetrated the operation of politics.

Cultural life goes without saying: media logic brings a tendency toward pan-entertainment. This is also connected to politics — often political elections become media spectacles, talk-show-like programs. The logic of media can turn any serious public issue into a talk show, into a spectacle. Even when people sometimes argue over something quite earnestly, once one excitement point passes another appears, and no excitement point lasts very long. While excitement points are being hyped by media, people have neither the time nor the energy to attend to the context, history, and causes behind the issues.

Any public issue can be turned into entertainment; there is no room for serious, thorough discussion, and it often becomes about taking sides and shouting matches. This is far from the picture Habermas once hoped for — media constructing a public sphere in which everyone can deliberate and reach consensus.

Source: [Douban](https://movie.douban.com/subject/11502153/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180605120040fyRxwn95jp18B3Fr.jpg-w600)

Then there is everyday life. Baudrillard discussed simulacra and simulation, arguing that the binary opposition between sign and reality, between signifier and signified, has been dissolved. Originally, a story or a TV drama was a representation of reality; you could say such representation is a distortion, that it conceals something or misleads — but that still discusses the relationship between the two on the premise of acknowledging a binary opposition between sign and reality. But in the ACG world — a world I'm really not familiar with; I often hear about it from students — the question of whether there is still a signified behind the signifier is suspended. A student told me her master's thesis will be about an online game — you probably know it, *Mr Love: Queen's Choice*. One of the male characters is Li Zeyan, and when this character had a birthday, female fans raised money to buy him a neon-lit billboard wishing him a happy birthday. I think this is exactly what Baudrillard meant: behind the signs of a Disney theme park there is no signified, because all those cartoon images never corresponded to reality in the first place — the theme park becomes the sign of signs. You could say this is another example of "high mediatization," and it also reflects the connection between mediatization and commodification.

From the standpoint of left-wing scholars, the worry these two trends bring is a scarcity of alternative imaginaries. Guy Debord's pessimistic view is that there is no longer any chance to propose and develop another ideology. This was also a key point of Adorno and Horkheimer's critique of the culture industry: mass-reproduced cultural products create an illusion.

You feel you're in a very rich media environment and can't decide what to choose — a "spoiled for choice" feeling. But Adorno says your choices and your freedom as a consumer are all within boundaries drawn by others: you merely choose among the commodities offered by this system, and you don't even know whether alternative options still exist.

### 2. The logic of short video, and: does livestreaming really have "diversity"?

**Q: When you talked about disposable consumer goods and the "digital labor" style of user participation, the two examples that came to my mind were livestreaming platforms and short video. Do they represent some characteristics of contemporary cultural production?**

**M:** Yes, both are quite important examples. Short video was born of its moment. It has a technological context: you can see the smartphone as an integrated technology platform — with one device you can participate in many different activities — and short video is of course related to that. I've also talked with many short video platforms, and they have one consideration: what is the optimal length for a video? They found that many people watch short videos on their commute to and from work, so the video can't be too long. This already imposes many constraints on the content short video can present and on how it presents it: it should be suited to visual presentation; topic selection can't be too complex; the exposition of complex topics must be simplified as much as possible — a problem that takes an investigative report to explain clearly cannot be presented through short video. From this perspective, the logic of technology, the logic of media platforms, and the logic of user habits have already prescribed much of the content.

Livestreaming platforms are the same. They can fly the banner of "empowerment" and "letting ordinary people have a voice," similar to platforms like YouTube. At the same time they can also claim authenticity, because in the past content was produced by professionals and had to pass through gatekeepers who ultimately decided what audiences could see; now this intermediate step has been removed, and the masses speak directly to the masses.

But in fact this returns to the Frankfurt School's questioning of "diversity." You'll find that the stars of livestreaming platforms — their looks, the content of their streams — are not that diverse. One category is the spectacle kind: eating all kinds of strange things, showing jaw-dropping skills. Another is women's performances and interaction with viewers. The aesthetic of the "influencer face" is extremely uniform — the term itself says: if you want to be famous, you must conform to a single standard. And in women's livestream performances there is a clear component of emotional labor, fully continuing the gendered division of labor under the patriarchal system.

### 3. How precision marketing on digital media shatters the sense of community

**Q: There seems to be a hierarchy of disdain between platforms like Kuaishou and Douyin; audiences of different media have developed barriers — even hostility — toward one another, making effective dialogue hard. What do you think are the roots and effects of this fragmentation?**

**M:** Even before the rise of the internet, the consumption of cultural products had always been class-marked. A [large-scale longitudinal social survey](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/265557/social-class-in-the-21st-century/) by Mike Savage of LSE's sociology department in the UK is very telling. They were studying class differentiation in British society; the questionnaire included questions about culture, one of which was: what is your main leisure activity? Going to concerts, the opera, and ballet — or going to pubs and listening to rock and pop music? It is fair to say that the consumption of cultural products has always been inseparable from class differentiation. Pierre Bourdieu's [*Distinction*](https://book.douban.com/subject/26354330/) is also about this problem. Of course, in the UK, public-service television like the BBC has a strong mission: it must have publicness, reach most of the country's people, and build an "imagined community" — it has a sense of public mission. Even so, if you ask Britain's working class, many will still say the BBC is like Oxford and Cambridge: a very elitist cultural institution.

But with the development of social media, and as platforms' grasp of users' behavioral habits and preferences becomes ever more precise, the problem of audience segmentation and fragmentation does exist. Media culture studies call this the shift from mass media to niche media. We said earlier that the relationship between individuals and cultural products is that of consumer and commodity; the more precisely the institutions producing these commodities understand consumers' characteristics and habits, the more it helps them achieve precision marketing. Their logic has always been: push users what they want. This is the logic behind the Meta–Cambridge Analytica scandal. All of this marketing can be seen as a form of persuasion, and persuasion is most effective when you fully understand the person being persuaded — knowing what rhetoric, what manner of speaking, can persuade them to the greatest extent possible.

This indeed brings the problem you just mentioned. As marketing becomes ever more fragmented and precise, publicness is lost. When citizens' information channels are completely different, the common ground for discussion disappears. Take the US election: the information pushed to you on social media may be completely different from what your neighbors and the people around you receive. Everyone lives inside silos, their views constantly reinforced by precision media marketing and political marketing. Why do public-service media like the BBC emphasize the "imagined community"? Because they believe democracy can only function if people share a basic consensus: roughly the same sources of information, at least a common understanding of public affairs at the factual level, and a sense of identification with, responsibility toward, and participation in the community as a whole. But when precision marketing on digital media shatters this sense of community into pieces, everyone lives in different discursive spaces. Getting these people together for political deliberation makes consensus even harder to reach, because even the most basic starting point — facts — cannot be found. This is also why the West now treats fake news as a scourge: it truly shakes the basic premise of the democratic system. If you can't find even a basic factual consensus, opinions are even further out of reach.

### 4. Why "Goddess Day" is a step backward for gender politics

**Q: The "precision marketing" you mentioned seems to include marketing targeted at women. Many merchants have launched publicity offensives against women through internet channels — "Goddess Day," perks like priority ride-hailing dispatch, and so on. What changes will this bring?**

**M:** I once wrote an article using Double 11 as a case for discourse analysis, focusing on discussions in the media and on social media around Double 11. The discourse system of the so-called "shopaholic women" is very prominent in it. Jack Ma himself posted a Weibo after one year's Double 11, thanking China's vast female internet users, because they consume not only for themselves but also for their husbands and children, and thus deserve great credit. I cited this as a typical manifestation of "patriarchal capitalism with Chinese characteristics."

To sort out the logic of this, we may have to go back to gender politics and gender equality in the socialist period, and to how these issues changed after the reform and opening up. In the socialist period there was the saying "women hold up half the sky," which had important progressive significance: it encouraged women to walk out of the home and into the workplace, to participate in so-called socialist construction. This actually broke through the traditional gender division of labor. There was such a broad political orientation at the time, the state played a very important and very positive role, and state-owned enterprises did not have such obvious gender discrimination as in a marketized environment.

But after the reform and opening up — especially after the reform of state-owned enterprises — women's employment and participation in production regressed greatly. Because when you use market logic to calculate the use of labor, it is clearly not cost-effective to employ female labor: there is the issue of maternity leave, for one thing. Some job categories are also deemed unsuitable for women. In this process, the state gradually withdrew from intervening in gender equality in labor and employment.

Concomitant with inequality in women's employment was the emphasis on returning women to the family and to motherhood. And under consumerist ideology, these roles are inseparable from being a qualified consumer. Look at Jack Ma's words: a woman's most important social roles are "wife" and "mother." She holds the family's consumption power and must care not only for herself but for everyone else in the family. So she becomes the natural target of commercial advertising and consumerist discourse.

Back to gender politics: the whole "goddess" discourse is also a great regression compared with the socialist period. In my view this is a very reactionary gender politics. On the surface it says to worship the "goddess," to hold the "goddess" in high esteem, but the expectations this discourse assigns to women are situated entirely within the male gaze. Think about what kind of female images are prescribed by a holiday like "Goddess Day": women are simply objectified, highly sexualized, to fit the image of a "beauty" in men's eyes.

This discourse is also a backlash against the gender-neutral discourse of the socialist period. The great 1980s discussion of "human nature" held that so-called "human nature" had been suppressed in the past. One voice at the time said that women in the socialist period were like the "iron girl" figure — a de-gendered or neutralized image that erased femininity, and that represented, you might say, the oppression of human nature by an authoritarian statist regime. But from the perspective of gender politics, the later so-called "search for femininity" was a complete regression: it defined the difference between femininity and masculinity as "softness and gentleness" — "liberated" women were to use cosmetics and clothing to recover and highlight their femininity — which retreated to very backward gender roles and a gendered division of labor. On the other hand, "femininity" dovetailed seamlessly with consumerism: only through consumption, through particular types of commodities, can you maintain and craft your femininity. So Double 11 and Goddess Day seem to respect women, but in fact they set gender politics back — even back to feudal times.

**Q: Some merchants have adopted rhetoric like "girl power," confirming women's independence by affirming women's economic freedom and the legitimacy of women's desires. What do you think of this kind of promotional discourse?**

**M:** Many Western feminists have discussed the problem of "girl power." This discourse about girl power is linked to the logic of neoliberalism. One aspect of this logic is its strong emphasis on the individual: everyone is held responsible for the circumstances of her own life — the so-called self-responsibility. As a woman, if you look unkempt and disheveled, don't attend to your image, and don't conform to the usually prescribed "goddess" image, you have failed in your individual responsibility, failed to take care of yourself. So how do you take care of yourself? Through consumption — and "the right kind of consumption." So individual responsibility for one's life is still to be fulfilled through commodities.

On the other hand, the affirmation of the female body and of women's desire is also neoliberal — or what the West calls post-feminist — thinking. It means: we no longer need to talk about feminism; gender equality has been achieved; we are in a "post-feminist" era. But this discourse has two problems.

First, is the so-called "attractive female body," the celebration of women's desire, really for women themselves — or is it to satisfy the male gaze? Are women really expressing their individuality and making choices, or are they catering to highly socialized male desire?

Second, the representation of the body and the celebration of women's desire are, again, shaped and governed by the logic of commodities. Everything — including the demands of the feminist movement — can in the end become a commodity. For instance, you can have a T-shirt printed with "This is what a feminist looks like." Your political demands and emancipatory discourses can in the end become mass-reproduced commodities, just like Che Guevara's portrait.

### 5. "The resistance of the elite middle class"

**Q: We've noticed that some non-traditional groups — feminist organizations, LGBTQ communities, hipsters — are trying to challenge today's mainstream culture by gathering online or offline. How do you evaluate their social engagement?**

**M:** I think evaluating these also depends on context. In the Chinese context, these organizations are, from the perspective of class and stratum, quite rich in cultural capital and social capital; they are well educated and mostly urban middle class. LGBT people especially — I know a scholar in LSE's Department of Social Policy who studies Chinese LGBT communities, and the LGBT people he contacts and interviews feel like a very elite group. Of course, expanding discursive space, expanding the dimensions of identity, and pluralizing values are positive things. But I think these groups are even more elite than the already-elite Western (LGBT and similar) groups. When we pay attention to social movements and resistance, focusing only on these groups is a big problem, because we ignore the majority and ignore the dimension of class. We are only paying attention to the resistance of the elite middle class, while China also has a huge population of new-generation migrant workers — a group without cultural or social capital, and not necessarily media-literate, so that in some key struggles they cannot use media well to make their voices heard. Nor does the media pay attention to them; the media has always cared more about the joys and sorrows of the urban middle class.

**Q: The media seems to observe and describe the living conditions of new-generation migrant workers and of people foreign to urban space mostly with a sensationalist, curiosity-seeking gaze — the depictions of Pang Mailang, the spectacle-style presentation of platforms like Kuaishou. Does this mark a certain fragmentation of Chinese society?**

**M:** This voyeuristic mentality definitely exists. After China's media went through that round of marketization and commodification, its position, its ideology, the people it serves, and its producers and professionals are all urban middle class. Its attention to the lower rungs is certainly grossly inadequate, and when it does pay attention, one mode is sensationalism and the other is a condescending pity — the term "disadvantaged groups" itself shows this very well.

[Wanning Sun](https://www.uts.edu.au/staff/wanning.sun) of the University of Technology Sydney has written about Chinese urban news media's coverage of migrant workers demanding unpaid wages. These media rarely pay attention to migrant workers' problems, but once migrant workers climb onto a rooftop threatening to jump — a very spectacular, dramatic form of protest — the media take notice. I think commercial media have a very keen nose: they quickly capture which stories grab eyeballs and which stories can further strengthen the sense of superiority of media professionals and media audiences as the urban middle class. These are all stories that reinforce and confirm the existing class hierarchy, both for those who write the stories and for those who read them. But look at what happened when Fan Yusu appeared: some middle-class intellectual elites became uncomfortable — you, a live-in nanny, read the same books as us, use our vocabulary, don't envy or worship our cultural capital at all, and even poke fun at it now and then?

![Screenshot from: [The White House](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCYxRlFDqcWM4y7FfpiAN3KQ) / YouTube](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180605142635l6j2sJtZAhC4Bzvo.jpg-w600)

The West has this problem too. Especially after Trump's election and the Brexit referendum, Western elites slowly began to realize that elite media institutions are disconnected from a considerable portion of the masses. The media treated those who voted for Trump as "rednecks," assuming they are simply racists, while those voters lumped the media together with the entire elite establishment. Trump's election is a textbook example: by the late campaign, no matter what outlets like *The New York Times* and CNN exposed about him, they couldn't hurt him or shake his voters, because his voters had already decided that you elite media institutions are in cahoots with the establishment — and the fact that you say these things is exactly why we're voting for him; we want to elect someone who will smash the elite order. So the elitism of the media and the political polarization it brings exist not only in China. Western left-wing scholars — David Harvey, Nancy Fraser — have all discussed this problem.

The rise of the groups you mentioned is already a recent phenomenon in China. Identity politics rose in the West after the countercultural movements of the 1960s. Fraser has written many essays on how the rise of identity politics dissolved class issues within social struggle. Harvey has also said that the rise of identity politics creates great difficulty for "solidarity": if everyone emphasizes their own unique identity dimension, is there still a baseline in political struggle around which solidarity can be built, rather than emphasizing one oppressed identity or another? Wendy Brown has also said this seems to have become an "oppression competition": is it my LGBT group that is more oppressed, or people of color? So the rise of identity politics has negatively affected both solidarity and demands in political struggle. Fraser discussed the relationship between the so-called "politics of recognition" and the "politics of redistribution." Reflecting after identity politics had already blazed through a round in the 1990s, she argued that the politics of recognition cannot replace the politics of redistribution: a genuinely emancipatory political project must combine the politics of recognition with the politics of redistribution.

**Q: Do you think Nancy Fraser's prescription of the "politics of redistribution" is the fundamental cure for resisting commodification and preventing the loss of public space?**

**M:** From a left-wing standpoint, today we need to be wary of any critical theorist or left-wing intellectual coming out to offer everyone a cure-all or paint a blueprint, because we have already seen the process and experience of socialist revolutions in different parts of the world. We should be wary of any grand narrative; any progressive politics, and any imagination and design, must be highly historicized and highly contextualized.

But I agree with Nancy Fraser's framing — she also starts from the position of a feminist political theorist. As a feminist scholar, I very much identify with her emphasis that "gender politics and class politics should be combined." A couple of years ago she wrote an excellent article titled ["How feminism became capitalism's handmaiden — and how to reclaim it"](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2013/oct/14/feminism-capitalist-handmaiden-neoliberal). This brings us back to what you mentioned earlier: the danger of this discourse that flies the banner of liberating women, liberating desire, and pursuing "equality." A person has not only a gender dimension but also important identity dimensions of class and race. Your position in power relations as a white middle-class woman is surely different from that of a Chinese woman worker. If you want to defend the rights of a woman worker in a garment factory in China or Bangladesh and help her voice her political demands, you cannot rely on the gender dimension alone — there are necessarily class and race dimensions involved, and it necessarily implicates the global capitalist order.

### 6. The global elite is growing ever more alike in many respects, while within nations there is ever more fragmentation

**Q: In contemporary life, many individual actions are disembedded: people all over the world seem to adopt the same entertainment, the same leisure activities, no longer emphasizing that each culture has its own context and history.**

**M:** I think your premise is a bit problematic. When you say everyone adopts the same leisure and entertainment, you may be referring only to each country's elite class. Talking about culture under conditions of globalization, one paradox is that you can always find many examples to emphasize global connectivity: everyone watches Japanese anime, American TV dramas, and Hollywood films — and the commentators on cultural phenomena are themselves an elite group. Starting from their everyday experience, such groups easily reach the conclusion you describe, as if there's a hint of one world for all.

But on the other hand, as the global elite grows ever more alike across many dimensions — ideological convergence, convergence in cultural and media content consumption, even convergence in lifestyles — within a single country there is ever more fragmentation, and this fragmentation is tied to class differentiation and to the gap between rich and poor. China need not be mentioned again: we already discussed the middle-class orientation of mass media, whose consequence is that a huge number of people have no voice. Even in the West, the examples of Trump and Brexit show that elites assumed everyone's interests and demands, everyone's understanding of globalization, everyone's attitude toward neoliberalism and global capitalist trade were quite positive — that ordinary people should think the same way they do. Not until the voting results woke them with a start did they realize: so there are such people in my own country, and I never knew.

After Brexit, the BBC made a special program interviewing people who supported Brexit. You get the feeling these people simply didn't exist before — they were outside the field of vision of Westminster. American elites say those who voted for Trump were deceived, even that there is a kind of "false consciousness" at work — they may not use this Marxist concept, but if you look at the cultural products "those people" consume, the media content they encounter in daily life must differ enormously from that of the globalized elite.

### 7. Why "blocking" was never a good solution

**Q: Will political pressure aggravate the loss of public space — for instance, some topics basically have no possibility of being discussed in public space at all?**

**M:** Certainly. Speech control and censorship systems also change the space of speech. In this sense, China is now "the worst case scenario": squeezed between political control and capital control, the space for speech is very, very small. Not to mention censorship with Chinese characteristics — capital's manipulation of culture and media is in some ways perhaps worse than in Britain or America. Britain still has a strong tradition of state welfarism, of socialism.

**Q: What impact does the government's crackdown on "vulgar" content in cyberspace and on specific platforms, in the name of combating vulgarity, have on the internet ecosystem?**

**M:** When we analyze media governance, we talk about how the logic of governance differs under different political systems. For instance, the Chinese government's positioning of media is as a platform for publicizing official positions and policies; it has a very important propaganda role. At the same time, when the government conducts media regulation, it adopts a very paternalistic mindset. It treats viewers and netizens as children: it assumes you "don't know," that you "can't tell good from bad," that you're likely to be misled by all kinds of things including vulgar content, and that "I," as the parent, must take on the role of the judge and curate content for you. This may be the orientation of Chinese media management.

But for the management of culture, "blocking" was never a good solution. Managing a cultural product or an ideology by blocking is very inefficient, and it also raises questions of policy legitimacy. This is even more true in the internet environment: rather than blocking things, you might as well undertake the corresponding cultural construction. In this cacophonous, pluralistic arena of public opinion, is it possible for you to better build the kind of culture you want to promote? Is it possible for the cultural products you consider healthy to be loved and accepted by more people?

The term "hegemony" as Gramsci used it was long translated into Chinese by a word that easily evokes military conquest and imperialism. But Gramsci's hegemony emphasizes a party's leadership — its overall control in the ideological realm — so "leadership" might be the more fitting rendering. The consensus and legitimacy involved here do not rest entirely on military force and the machinery of violence. Marx spent his whole life dissecting the capitalist system from the perspective of political economy, while his account of culture and ideology was relatively thin. Gramsci took up this task: his discussions of organic intellectuals, of the relationship between the coercive and consent, and of the strategic shift from war of maneuver to war of position all concern the party's leadership. From this perspective, the more "blocking" policies are issued, the more they reveal the weakness of ideological leadership. If you had truly built a successful hegemony at the ideological level — Hollywood, say — you could keep absorbing non-mainstream, marginal things for your own use while never losing your core. Thinking this cannot be allowed and that cannot be allowed shows precisely that you are in a stretched-thin, straitened state.

### 8. Keeping pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will

**Q: If the logic of commodification is so powerful, to what degree do you think today's consumers still have agency — the ability to decide their own lives? In a blog post you mentioned that your own writing style has been influenced by WeChat: you're more accustomed to relatively fragmented writing.**

**M:** This question cannot be answered in general terms; we have to distinguish specific contexts and different kinds of consumers. Different groups have different levels of media literacy, and so their agency and control in everyday life differ as well. You may also want to pay attention to how agency is manifested and exercised in specific situations, because I think that under digital conditions the relationship between users and platforms, between content and media, is highly fluid, and the power relationship between the two depends on the situation and the group. The contest of power is manifested in the process: you have to look at which side has the initiative at which moment. It is a highly dynamic process.

But on the whole, as a researcher of the political economy of media, I am not optimistic about users' initiative — though I'm not willing to say "there's no possibility left," because that would basically amount to surrendering without a fight.

**Q: As a media researcher, how do you deal with media's dominion in your own life, and how do you use media effectively?**

**M:** If the "dominion" you mention borrows Foucault's concept of "governmentality," then Foucault's power is not merely repressive but constitutive, not merely top-down institutional power but something that penetrates down to the micro-level of the individual, operating in the use and circulation of discourse and the shaping of subjectivity. In this sense, within the highly mediatized context of contemporary everyday life, there is no escape from the dominion formed by the interweaving logics of capital and technology. But as Mao said, where there is oppression, there is resistance. Since Foucault extended the site of power's operation to the micro-level, resistance can also happen at the micro-level of the individual.

As a media researcher, my basic predisposition toward media is certainly one of critical suspicion, and I don't feel I can single-handedly turn the logic of media to my own use. Bourdieu wrote about the problem of "public intellectuals and the media": in the end, public intellectuals submit to media logic and are controlled by it, not the other way around.

Whatever resistance I can have as an individual is, first, to stay vigilant against media's dominion and, second, to look for opportunities to voice different views — accepting your interview, for instance. As a teacher, my profession gives me a very good opportunity and platform to influence young people, and I hope I can help them find a critically reflective vocabulary and build alternative imaginaries different from the logic of capital and commerce. But even elite universities, discourse spaces with natural advantages, are not ivory towers insulated from the world; they too are constantly being squeezed and eroded. So what I can do is probably just Gramsci's famous line: maintain pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will.
