Once Called "Beijing's Brooklyn," What Does the Drum Tower Area Look Like Today?

@@QD_BODY@@
The atmosphere of a tourist zone is seeping into every alley around the Drum Tower.

Old Beijingers probably all know that Nanluoguxiang was called Centipede Street long ago. The lane is wide—that's the centipede's body—and on either side of it run eight neat, narrow branch hutongs, like the centipede's legs.

Out the north end of Nanluoguxiang, walk 600 meters west and you reach Beijing's Bell and Drum Towers. Like most old cities, this was once the place where the time was announced. Around the Bell and Drum Towers there has always been a bustling market. In earlier days, a bamboo broom called a tiaozhou was also sold here.

![The square between the Drum Tower and the Bell Tower, where people are rehearsing a play](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111451NThtGunKHRdk0Ze9.jpg-w600)

In 2007, Zhang Lin opened a café, Cafe Zarah, on Gulou East Street, east of the Drum Tower and north of Nanluoguxiang. What drew him, besides the dense trees lining the road and the wide sidewalks, was the street's quiet, unhurried air. Creating that atmosphere were not only old Beijingers but also a group of foreigners who had come to live in China.

They discovered the area more than 20 years ago. Elsewhere Beijing was a forest of high-rises, but around the Drum Tower large numbers of single-story courtyard houses survived, and the old Beijing hutongs sat next to imperial courtyards and the well-known scenic spot "Houhai." Ju'er Hutong lies on the first leg of the centipede's northeast side; thanks to a hutong redevelopment project there by architect Wu Liangyong in the 1990s, it first attracted foreign architects and then a wave of foreigners who moved in.

![Winter at Houhai, where people swim in the icy water](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111452Wj89sEOlFChzcKZJ.jpg-w600)

Add to that the Central Academy of Drama at the south end of Nanluoguxiang, and theaters, instrument shops, bars, and livehouses clustered there. So much so that when Beijing's Dongcheng District planned in 2010 to spend $730 million redeveloping the Drum Tower area, even the American magazine *Time* felt regret. In that year's "Best of Asia" list, Beijing's Drum Tower area was included under "places to see before they disappear."

![In winter, people play ice hockey on Houhai's frozen surface](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111452DEdbS5JG9H8fWhXA.jpg-w600)

Luckily, the official demolition and rebuilding plan was never carried out. As late as 2013, *The New York Times* was still calling the area "the Brooklyn of Beijing." That neighborhood in southeastern Manhattan holds the largest population of any county in New York State; its people come from all over the world, like a melting pot that breeds underground culture while also allowing free-wheeling commerce, with goods that are exotic yet novel and trendy.

But this "Brooklyn" has now changed completely.

![Single-story houses beside the Drum Tower](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021114531q8HOafX5sL9ohv3.jpg-w600)

1.

What's most popular on Gulou East Street now is shoe stores. People call them "Jordan shoe stores."

Simple shoe racks, no real lighting or color design, but the owners make a point of stocking models designed or endorsed by stars. Sneakers from Nike's Jordan brand are especially hot; in the more than 20 shoe stores on this street, prices for the brand's basketball shoes have been marked up nearly threefold.

"Whatever gets speculated on in Beijing, it shows up here first." Huading Realty is a property agency on Gulou East Street; a clerk there, wearing a pair of Jordans himself, says the shopkeepers on the street are good at riding trends: around 2000, most of the shops here sold game consoles and musical instruments; clothing stores multiplied starting in 2004; after a crop of cafés appeared in 2008, bead bracelets and Thai Buddha amulets had their moments in 2010 and 2012 respectively.

"This street (Gulou East Street) basically changes its whole cast every two years. Seventy percent of the shops turn over," the agent said. "I'm really curious what will be trendy next year."

![Gulou East Street](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111453OJVdGWHsxrNq2jB8.jpg-w600)

The changes around Gulou are hard to predict. No one foresaw the speed of the rent increases, for instance. In 2007, when Zhang Lin rented a shop of over 300 square meters on Gulou East Street, the annual rent was 160,000 yuan—and that was already the most expensive per-unit rent on the street. But starting the following year, the government decided to develop Nanluoguxiang into a tourist zone, and compared with imperial culture, hutong life seemed fresher and more novel. Everything became uncontrollable.

![A residence on the Gulou East Street frontage, with a "do not disturb" sign put up](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111454Ja3DkqURo76wmXjB.jpg-w600)

The road surface was repaved, guardrails were added, and parking on the street was banned. Tourists flocked in. Public data shows that in 2005, Nanluoguxiang drew fewer than 60,000 visitors a year. In 2009, that number rose to 1.6 million. In 2012 and 2013, subway Lines 6 and 8 opened respectively, with Shichahai Station and Nanluoguxiang Station coming into service. During the 2013 Dragon Boat Festival, Nanluoguxiang averaged 20,000 visitors a day. By last October 1, the daily foot traffic here had reached 100,000.

Although the government did not lead the commercial development of this neighborhood, tourism still helped push rents up. An agent at Huading Realty says shop rents here rank among the top five in all of Beijing.

![The subway now runs directly to the south end of Nanluoguxiang](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111454jldIMmrPT3OYGuH2.jpg-w600)

A 200-square-meter street-facing shop in Nanluoguxiang can go for 180,000 yuan a month. Next comes Houhai, where a shop front on the bar street—even one of just 10 square meters—now rents for 8,000 to 10,000 yuan a month. Then Gulou East Street, near the Nanluoguxiang end, where a 70-square-meter shop costs 30,000 yuan a month. And in the branch hutongs nearby, a 20-square-meter shop on the main passage between Nanluoguxiang and Houhai can command over 10,000 yuan a month.

![A shop in one of the branch hutongs of Nanluoguxiang](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111455HAjYgJvlZCUsLo9R.jpg-w600)

A popcorn shop opened just after the Spring Festival near the north end of Nanluoguxiang; the site used to be a shed a resident had put up himself to store honeycomb briquettes. After renovation, the house was rented out for over 10,000 yuan a month.

2.

Most landlords in the Gulou area are old Beijingers. They rarely use an agent to help with leasing (sales are another matter) and prefer to deal privately. Unless it's the peak tourist season of May and June, shop owners also do their best to hold their tongues and not complain that the transfer-for-lease notices hanging at their doorways hurt business. When dealing with these landlords, an agent had better not wear a suit and call them "sir"—if you're a local, just push the door open and shout "uncle," and your odds of success double.

The landlords are the biggest beneficiaries here; rents have never stopped rising, yet shop fronts are always in demand. New shop owners come for the Gulou tourists; as for everything else, it's all up in the air.

Last November, Ma Shusen and his friends opened Pengmu on Gulou East Street, a do-it-yourself plant shop. The shop used to be on Dawanglu, but with little foot traffic they decided to relocate, and their first thought was Gulou.

The Drum Tower sits inside Beijing's Second Ring Road, at the city's center, and its mix of Chinese and foreign residents, its slightly time-spanning atmosphere, has a particular pull on young people. "This place is very fashionable," said a clerk at a Rilakkuma shop on Gulou East Street. "Maybe you'll misread what I mean by fashionable, but in my eyes this place is as fashionable as Sanlitun. You can always see the most fashionable people on this street, the most avant-garde clothes and styles."

![A Gulou street scene](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016070211145525sTSjRtpGVfyW6H.jpg-w600)

Sanlitun is a bustling commercial district in Chaoyang, one of Beijing's newer districts, and the Taikoo Li mall built there a few years ago is more or less the capital's landmark of style. But this street is more flexible than a mall. Traditional malls usually require a one-time contract of three to five years (though that number is shrinking too); the alleyways of Gulou have no such rules. The landlords expect prices to rise, and they're willing to shorten leases to one-year terms. And because the contracts are short, everyone wants to take a shot.

Most of those eager to try are young people. An agent at Huading Realty received two young women who had returned from studying in Korea and wanted to open a café here. But they hadn't thought about how to actually run the business. "I asked them how much they'd charge for a cup of coffee at their shop, and the two girls said they didn't know—it would depend on the cost. They were already about to open the shop and still didn't know what they'd charge per cup."

"Dreams. You know what dreams are? They just wanted to come here and realize their dream," the agent said loudly. But obviously, their dream would be much harder to realize than Zhang Lin's had been back then.

3.

Unable to bear the sky-high rents and the shoulder-to-shoulder tourists, Coast Cafe moved from Nanluoguxiang to Gulou East Street a few years ago.

Zhang Lin thinks the move is easy to understand: a halfway decent café can hardly survive in a tourist commercial zone—the demands of tourists are low, and they have no appreciation of quality. They just want to see the place, and the savvier ones only buy fast, cheap things, so as not to get taken in by a tourist area.

![A candy shop in Nanluoguxiang](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111456gIWfEezGM0u2QYkT.jpg-w600)

But moving away from Nanluoguxiang doesn't mean escaping the tourists. The atmosphere of a tourist zone is seeping into every alley around the Drum Tower. To keep tourists from disturbing its long-term community customers, Cafe Zarah on Gulou East Street deliberately made its shop logo inconspicuous. Yet the resident foreigners are also moving away. Owner Zhang Lin estimates that more than half of them have left.

In 2013, Cafe Zarah expanded: over 300 square meters of indoor space, plus a courtyard of more than 80 square meters. Under Zhang Lin's original projections, even paying 800,000 yuan a year in rent, the big café would still turn a decent profit. But the rise in housing costs drove up labor costs in the area, and Zhang Lin says these past few years the big café has only been able to "keep body and soul together."

The ones doing best now are the shops wholly devoted to tourists' needs. On Gulou East Street there are two "10-yuan stores" claiming oversight by Korean and Japanese businesses. They pay their high rents on the strength of enormous customer volume and rock-bottom costs, and still live quite well.

In the 800 meters of Nanluoguxiang, more than half the shops have turned into fast-food snack stalls—jianbing crepes, quick-boiled tripe, cheese desserts, cold drinks, duck neck, kebabs—with prices mostly around 20 yuan. "It quenches thirst and fills the stomach. If you're not in that business, you really can't make it," said the owner of the failing popcorn shop on Nanluoguxiang; in his store, bottled water outsells popcorn by a wide margin.

![A snack shop on Nanluoguxiang](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111456hPoe6tFyLV3O0D9n.jpg-w600)

Diagonally across from the popcorn shop, a "crab roe" bun shop pays 1.2 million yuan a year in rent, but the owner says a box of buns goes for 20 yuan and he makes 20,000 yuan a day. And next door, the Juewei duck neck shop, thanks to booming business, saw its annual rent rise from over 100,000 yuan last year to 600,000 yuan.

Some small businesses are less the bearers of a rare street-level vitality than simply the only ones that can make money. The grilled-fish boss sets up an extra cart on the street at night to sell luzhu, Beijing's stewed offal. A tobacco-and-liquor supermarket run by a landlord himself occupies the best spot on Nanluoguxiang, with a big storefront, but few people come in to buy cigarettes or liquor; to "keep the shop alive," the boss parks a cart outside selling cotton candy, the "photo companion."

"As soon as the rent goes up again, we'll definitely be squeezed out by these high-margin shops," said the Rilakkuma shop clerk. "It's a sad thing."

4.

Two foreign residents walking toward us said they live here, but Gulou is clearly not as interesting as it used to be. "There are fewer interesting shops, and things are more expensive."

They headed instead to "the Bread Bar," a dining bar frequented by foreigners on Beiluoguxiang. That hutong intersects with Gulou East Street, directly north of Nanluoguxiang. It's close by, but the foot traffic is far lighter. Especially at night, the lane is dark and silent, like Nanluoguxiang in the 1990s.

![The Bread Bar on Beiluoguxiang, with graffiti left by customers](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111457vDIV3sUmQoXwZCub.jpg-w600)

Beiluoguxiang, and perhaps Gulou West Street, another street nearby, might be good places to escape high rents and dense tourists. But Zhang Lin thinks it's not as simple as people assume—the hutongs of Beiluoguxiang are too deep to gather a crowd, and Gulou West Street leans on the Houhai scenic spot with no real life of its own—it could never again produce the scene he found when he first came to open his shop in 2007. What's more, having lost its underground culture and hutong life, these Gulou shops have "lost their meaning," yet have "nowhere to go."

Mao Livehouse, billed as the "number-one venue for rock," has stood on Gulou East Street since 2007. At the end of 2015, founder Li Chi announced that, faced with astronomical rents, they were "powerless to turn things around and preparing to move." But this past February, Li Chi wrote on Weibo that he had "yet to find a place to his liking"; Mao would close in April but would not, for now, relocate anywhere else.

![Stickers on the air conditioner inside Mao Livehouse](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111457vabJsDC0FxnHfr1L.jpg-w600)

The one possible exception is a Xinjiang restaurant across from Mao that everyone calls "Nanjiang." It has been here for 11 years, and the bands playing at Mao often eat, drink, wait for their turn, and do their makeup here. One February afternoon, five groups of customers came through.

![A wall covered with posters inside the Nanjiang restaurant](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702111457OvVHo5xYLiBQzKeW.jpg-w600)

A young local Beijing couple, not wanting to cook, came to grab a bite together; a group of tourists came in, ate quickly, and left; two rock musicians, finished with practice, came to eat, drink, and chat; two migrant workers, who work at a pizza place on Gulou East Street, had just gotten off work; and one big table of middle-aged uncles living in the hutongs, gathered for a meal together.

Many people guess that once Mao moves away, the shops on this street in a "symbiotic" relationship with it will be affected. But Nanjiang may not be. The reason Xinjiang restaurants can survive in many parts of Beijing is much the same: their flavors are broadly popular, their food is noodle- and bread-based, costs are low, and margins are high.

They are doing just fine beside the Drum Tower.
