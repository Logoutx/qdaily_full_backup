What exactly does a degree mean? Between a credential diluted by mass enrollment and an unforgiving job market lies a tangle of individual choices.

In March 2010, Chen Yinuo was a senior at Siyuan Xinyuan, a private high school in Anshan, Liaoning Province. Her parents were both in business and away from home much of the year; she was their only child. They didn't want their daughter living on the road the way they did. The best way out was to pass the gaokao and land a stable job close to home — teacher, civil servant, or nurse were all solid options.

Like most parents, they didn't really know what their 18-year-old daughter wanted for her future. Chen Yinuo had imagined working by the sea, which at the time she thought meant joining the customs service; she also wanted to work for a large foreign-invested company, or "do business and make big money." On her application list she put Shanghai Maritime University and Tianjin University of Commerce.

Whichever dream she pursued, her test scores only raised the odds of it falling through. Liaoning's gaokao is scored out of 750; Chen Yinuo's mock-exam results were just over 300 — not enough to get her into any university for a bachelor's degree. But in the final three months before the exam she pushed her total above 500 and got in. Unable for the moment to persuade her parents to let her leave the province, she "found a school close to home": Dalian Polytechnic University.

"People always mix it up with Dalian University of Technology — that's a 985!" By comparison, Dalian Polytechnic falls well short. The institution, founded in 1958, was called Dalian Institute of Light Industry until 2007. In 1999 it absorbed the Liaoning Textile Industry School. But what really made it balloon in size into a "university" was the "mass enrollment expansion" in higher education launched that same year.

In 1998, China's gross enrollment rate in universities was 9.1%, meaning roughly one in eleven of that year's graduates got into college. In 1999 the Ministry of Education set a target of reaching a 15% gross enrollment rate by 2010, and universities expanded admissions rapidly to get there. In 1999 alone, enrollment grew 47.4%. The 15% target was actually hit in 2002.

![Image from [Good Free Photos](https://unsplash.com/photos/YZsvNs2GCPU?utm_source=unsplash&utm_medium=referral&utm_content=creditCopyText) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=unsplash&utm_medium=referral&utm_content=creditCopyText)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181121182804gYW6yix0tsLeGwIJ.jpg-w600)

By 2010, the year Chen Yinuo took the gaokao, university enrollment reached 6.6 million — the largest cohort ever. That year, 9.46 million people registered for the exam.

Between 2000 and 2009, to accommodate more students, Dalian Polytechnic University (still Dalian Institute of Light Industry in some of those years) opened new majors and set up new schools. The School of Management now has 2,000 students, but before 2004 it was just a department of economics and trade.

In 2010 Chen Yinuo became a student of international economics and trade in the School of Management at Dalian Polytechnic, in Liaoning's second admissions batch. That year her class had roughly 100 students. Their gaokao scores (liberal arts track) were higher than those of students in any other major at the school. Chen Yinuo's score was 40 points above Liaoning's second-batch cutoff.

Chen Yinuo figured she had ended up among the most "miserable" of college students, having landed in a "lousy" university that was "probably a zhuanshengben school" (one upgraded from a junior college), "ranked nearly 300th," that "hasn't had money to put up buildings in years" — even the teaching buildings look "ugly." She was a college student, sure, but "with a school this bad, if I don't study hard now, I might not have food to eat later."

### 1

It's hard to say how Chen Yinuo suddenly developed a sense of crisis upon entering college; in high school she was content to be the one dragging the class down. But hers is hardly an isolated case. "I woke up," says another young woman, Wang Ya, who later took the zhuanshengben exam (the junior-college-to-bachelor's upgrade exam).

One reason may be that the wider environment turned tense and anxiety-inducing: in the job market, the college diploma was depreciating. Starting in 2003, when the first cohort of expansion-era graduates entered the workforce, people began to notice that graduate employment rates were falling. A "2006 Survey of Chinese College Graduates' Employment Status" showed that "60% of graduates faced unemployment upon graduation." When the financial crisis hit in 2009, things got worse still. That year, the government began encouraging college graduates to go to the "grassroots" level, to the countryside.

Companies kept raising the bar, even for jobs that were pure routine, where the difference a degree makes is negligible. Retail is generally considered the least degree-demanding of all industries, but its requirements rose just the same. A retail headhunter who asked to remain anonymous said that for people born before 1980, a company might consider a zhuanshengben credential during résumé screening — but for younger applicants, a bachelor's degree may be a hard requirement.

This is even truer for industry giants. Inditex, the parent of fast-fashion brand Zara, now demands that even interns hold a master's degree or come from a top-ranked school such as a Project 985 or Project 211 institution — even though a procurement department manager who graduated in 2003 says she only has an associate degree. "I think work experience matters more," she says, but she knows times have changed. Another source, a former Zara menswear regional manager who graduated in 2008, said he was promoted to regional manager a few years ago on the strength of a zhuanshengben credential, because the other store managers were all junior-college or technical-secondary graduates — a situation that won't repeat itself.

In Chen Yinuo's field, "world economics and trade," the top of the career pyramid means getting into investment banks, investment firms, or one of the Big Four accounting firms. But she had other options. In the eyes of her college counselor, a student whose gaokao score ranked near the top of her department could try to stay on at the university, sit the civil service exam, join a state-owned enterprise — or of course go to a foreign-invested company. In her first year he gave his students explicit career advice: if she planned to go to a foreign company, Chen Yinuo needed to hold a major student-leadership post, have internship experience at a big company, and keep her GPA looking respectable.

Chen Yinuo followed all three pieces of advice to the letter; she called her four years of college "burning the boats." "It was as bitter as it gets. When I went home on break, the thing I said most to my mom was: do not, under any circumstances, disturb my sleep." Meanwhile her résumé came closer and closer to an ideal job-seeker's template: president of the School of Management's student union, founder of a club of about 100 members, a graduating GPA above 3.6 ("almost every course above 90"). "The only weak point was the school."

None of it ultimately landed her the job she wanted. Before graduation she received a rejection letter from a major foreign bank. The letter stated, simply and bluntly, that the bank's reason for turning her down was that her credential did not meet its requirements.

### 2

One day in 2014, a month after mailing off an internship application to the Dalian branch of Coca-Cola, a Global 500 company, Chen Yinuo had heard nothing back. She picked up the phone and called the head of human resources. It was a marketing position in a retail department; the job posting hadn't even been put up on Dalian Polytechnic's BBS (she'd found it on another school's BBS), but Chen Yinuo believed she could handle it — even though her résumé had an obvious "flaw," she intended to sell herself as hard as she could.

It was the first time Chen Yinuo had contacted an HR head on her own initiative (it later became a habit in her job hunting), and it proved absolutely necessary: the executive told her the company email had been down for nearly a month and they hadn't received any new internship applications. "She just said, 'Fine, come in and try it out.'"

![Image from the film [*We Cannot Become Beasts*](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/2537848012/), via [Douban](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/2537848012/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181121181056jOQReNGh01aPXMK8.jpg-w600)

Chen Yinuo was only asked to make an Excel spreadsheet and a PowerPoint deck — tasks that reveal no difference in ability between candidates — and she began to worry they would decide the shortlist by school ranking. In Dalian, the two most competitive local universities are Dongbei University of Finance and Economics and Dalian University of Technology. Desperate to know whether she would be written off out of hand for her "poor school background," she asked the executive, "I don't know what the students over at Dongbei Finance and Polytech are like."

"If it were today, I'd probably tell him what my strengths are, instead of drawing that kind of distinction — because when you make that kind of school-to-school comparison, you've already placed yourself outside that circle," Chen Yinuo said this past November. But she also noted that "circles" are real.

Chen Yinuo passed the interview and became an intern at Coca-Cola, responsible for helping coordinate materials for supermarket displays and analyzing the returns on the spending. Chen Yinuo believes she got it because calling directly made her look "particularly gutsy" — and retail likes people with that kind of personality; because the company was closer to Dalian University of Technology, saving on taxi fares for overtime; and because it was a job where "a little cleverness and a little effort are really all it takes" — "as long as it's not something hyper-specialized like investment banking, they're all pretty much the same."

### 3

Retail and fast-moving consumer goods don't reject people whose credentials aren't shiny. At bottom, it's a sales job, a labor-intensive service industry. "Most people don't like doing sales, right?" says Tang Yun, who holds a zhuanshengben degree from Shanghai Institute of Technology. The job carries an impression of hard work and constant deference. But surveys show that after the enrollment expansion, a large share of college graduates went into labor-intensive service industries.

Still, the span of sales work is wider than most people imagine. Besides real-estate agents, gym memberships, and sports-car salesmen at 4S dealerships, it includes positions in the retail departments of multinational corporations (usually entry-level, though). Luxury retail, meanwhile, typically favors people with overseas study backgrounds.

Chen Yinuo's first job after graduation was as a management trainee at Mars. With a meticulously prepared résumé, she had secured Mars's offer early. After the foreign bank's rejection letter arrived, she joined this Fortune 500 company. Two years later, having failed to secure a transfer to a city outside Dalian, she began preparing to apply for Apple's "Leadership Development Program," hoping it would take her to Beijing.

The program, which sounds so extraordinary, is specifically about training the store managers of Apple's future retail stores to handle coming expansion. In August 2018, Chen Yinuo stood in Apple's Xidan store in Beijing, iPad in hand. At 5:50 p.m. the store manager would remind her to start wrapping up for the day. In fact it was a job that strictly followed procedure — "you just follow it, and that's that."

The truly extraordinary thing was actually the alma maters of those admitted to the program. School background remained a sore point for Chen Yinuo; she asked around about the credentials of everyone in the program. "One graduated with an MBA from Northwestern; I heard our cohort even has someone from MIT." Everyone's degree outranked hers — except for one person who had already worked at Apple retail for three or four years.

Breaking out on the strength of her credential was, of course, partly a delight. Chen Yinuo shared the good news with a professor from her university days. They had stayed in touch, and she believed that among his students, landing at a company like this was not an everyday occurrence. And once the pace of store expansion slowed, it would become rarer still. "Two or three years from now, when they recruit again, they'll want a famous school — or if not a famous school, a GPA in the top 5% or 10%. That's how it'll be."

![Image from the film *Bad Genius*, via [Douban](https://movie.douban.com/subject/27024903/photos?type=S)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181121183149QT0KjL46foZrEFtw.jpg-w600)

"I understand their thinking — they want to minimize labor costs as much as possible. Say you hire someone from Dalian Polytechnic who happens to be a less-than-diligent student; the company's loss is bigger, so I might as well hire from Tsinghua, Peking, Fudan, or Jiao Tong, where there's an 80% chance the student is excellent. For the company, the cost is lowest and the risk is lowest. But if you hire fairly ordinary people and promote them step by step, turnover ends up much lower."

### 4

The "circle" is always there.

At Apple, Chen Yinuo deliberately sought out her fellow trainees for conversation — "in a way, I was so fixated on the credential issue at first that I deliberately went to find people who'd graduated from famous schools and talk with them" — and in the end reached a conclusion: school background is decisive. "Once you get promoted to a certain level, the constraint is no longer just your degree — it's your horizons."

The evidence for this conclusion was, on one side, accidental and loosely connected. "Back then I was really into a thinker named Krishnamurti, and whenever I talked about him with people, nobody knew who he was. Then one day I was chatting with a colleague who'd graduated from a famous school, and I said I could lend him his books, that I had a set at home. He said, it sounds like I read him in the library, back around my sophomore year."

On the other side, the more tangible experience came from her fellow trainees quitting one after another when they found the job utterly unchallenging. "I found that those famous-school graduates — their options are unlimited. I can be at Apple today, quit tomorrow, and go to EY (one of the Big Four accounting firms) the next day — or not." That was surely an amplified fantasy, but of the roughly 20 trainees in the program's northern region, Chen Yinuo estimates at least five have already left. Some did move into auditing and consulting, and one got into a graduate program at a university in the United States.

Chen Yinuo believes that, unlike them, even if she feels the same tedium and boredom — and even if she seeks greater value from work than they do — she probably can't land a better opportunity than Apple.

![Apple Xidan Joy City, image from the [Apple website](https://www.apple.com/cn/retail/xidanjoycity/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181121175655JY9p1bvgLNRslmOw.jpg-w600)

Chen Yinuo's account covers the two faces people typically bring up when talking about college. One is metaphysical; the other, utilitarian. Xing Ge, an associate professor in the School of Management at Liaocheng University, is a frequent critic of the enrollment expansion, but he believes a college experience has an enormous influence. "Without an undergraduate degree... in most cases you end up in some industry, working as a cook or in sales, and life forces you into a daily scramble to make ends meet. Everything becomes utilitarian. Whatever is useful, you look at; whatever isn't, you don't. But college students are in one of the few free states in life where you don't need to think about anything — you get to observe the world freely."

Yet people also see college as an investment, one that should ultimately show its return in the job market — even if they're not always willing to admit it.

"These days I try not to think about return on investment," Chen Yinuo said this past November. "In the past, for me, the two most important things were probably money and face." But now she's contemplating a career change, hoping to reach the top of her professional pyramid — to get into an investment firm. Of course, from the money angle, that too is a job with a richer payoff.

### 5

Going back to a university campus might help her realize that wish.

It's an industry with extremely harsh standards for school background, as Chen Yinuo learned at graduation. Big investment firms and investment banks still weight the first degree heavily, which naturally puts her outside the "circle." "I also understand why investment requires that track record. I pitch something to an investor, and at the very least the investor has to look at your background — you can't invest blindly. Dalian Polytechnic? Did you even study this?"

Chen Yinuo's father — the businessman who had originally wanted his daughter to find a stable job close to home — agreed with her for the first time. She relayed his words: "If you want systematic learning, reading books is probably the fastest way. An MBA of a year and a half or two years can broaden you — so why would you spend a whole lifetime broadening your horizons?" Her father also believed the experience would help her long-term, stable relationship with her boyfriend. "If one day you want to have a conversation with him, the level of your thinking and your horizons can't be low." Chen Yinuo's boyfriend is a Stanford graduate working in consulting.

The Zara procurement department manager mentioned above is preparing for the entrance exam for the MBA program at CEIBS (China Europe International Business School), though she believes it "won't have any effect on my work." Business schools have also rapidly expanded enrollment in recent years, and headhunter Wei Yicheng believes that for mid- and senior-level managers who have spent years at big companies, the greater meaning of this new credential lies in the networking.

![Image from the [CEIBS website](http://cn.ceibs.edu/media/news/mba/12891)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181121175434p1ijRgmN2KZ37TSP.gif-w600Gif)

Chen Yinuo realized that if she wants to escape her school background once again, she needs to "burn the boats" once more. Her new résumé still has many gaps to fill: a fund practitioner qualification certificate, a handsome TOEFL score, and the notoriously low-pass CFA charter.

Unlike before, the 27-year-old has learned to prepare for both outcomes. Small investment firms are somewhat friendlier, Chen Yinuo figures she might be able to "get some certificates, bring some personal insights of your own, produce some mock reports... it would help you when applying to small investment firms."
