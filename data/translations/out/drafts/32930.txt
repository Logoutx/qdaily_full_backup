As for those people in Hollywood, "it takes me a long time to tell them that, actually, you're not good enough."

In 2012, Ang Lee released *Life of Pi*, his first experiment with 3D technology. The film took in US$606 million at the global box office and won Lee his second Academy Award for Best Director.

Four years later, he has returned with a technically more ambitious film, *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk*. Its technological lead is so great that the world is asking, "What exactly is this concept?" Lee's new film is not just 3D — it is paired with 4K resolution and 120 frames per second ([frames played per second; ordinary films run at 24 frames](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/22612.html)) at the highest specification.

This is no longer a matter of incremental upgrade. It is a leapfrog.

Besides giving the industry and the media something to debate, the film has, to date, kept setting world-first benchmarks. Perhaps we can put it this way: Ang Lee is establishing a brand-new cinematic standard.

That includes a projection specification that is also the highest in the world — so far only five theaters (note: i.e., cinemas) worldwide can support the full 3D, 4K, 120-frame format. The five are located in New York, Los Angeles, Beijing, Shanghai and Taipei; the rest of the theaters will screen a lower-spec version Lee has separately produced. As for ticket prices, the highest-spec version is currently priced at NT$800 per ticket in Taiwan, with the release date set for November 11 — which is also Veterans Day in the United States, fitting for a story about an American soldier who survives the Iraq War and is brought home.

![A still from *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk* (Image / Douban Movies)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201610041312545xZiALmEsNYKI26S.jpg-w600)

Even though only five theaters in the world can support it, Lee insists this had to be done and is certain it is a viable path. "When I was shooting Life of Pi, I could already see a future there." "I'm in my sixties now. I don't want to wait for the future — I want to see technology closer to reality right now." Lee said this recently at a press conference called "The Future of 3D."

Because *Life of Pi* opened Lee to a greater curiosity about the digital realm, he firmly believes it will overturn the way audiences experience and feel movies.

In a 2012 media interview, Lee put it this way: "3D is gradually becoming a legitimate cinematic art form. In fact, without 3D, I honestly don't know how to tell this story. This story had to be conveyed in a very unique way; a flat image could not accomplish it." Clearly, besides being a technical pioneer, Lee has an additional gift: the perceptiveness to notice the unique beauty that technology itself can bring.

Looking back over film history, people seem never to have questioned the 24-frame standard. Lee is dissatisfied with this seemingly indestructible framework: "24 frames isn't actually good — it's just cheap." He has clearly seen the shortcomings of this technology.

In the past, when promoting his films, Lee never emphasized the technology itself — or rather, he always faced audiences with a low-key, gentle and humble attitude. But on the promotional tour for *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk*, we see a more proactive Lee, with much experience to share about his recent exploration of an unknown territory.

Previously, [Taiwan's business magazine *CommonWealth Magazine* went to New York to interview Lee](http://www.cw.com.tw/article/articleLogin.action?id=5078345), who was then in post-production. Lee invited the reporters to visit his [post-production studio](http://www.cw.com.tw/video), explaining every stage in detail.

Two days ago, Lee accepted *CommonWealth Magazine*'s invitation to hold a symposium called "Exploration and Creation," which also featured a dialogue with Pegatron chairman Jason Chen.

![Ang Lee and Jason Chen (Image / CommonWealth Magazine, photo by Liu Guotai)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004131618ozmc9ZKNh8qvB63f.jpg-w600)

In the symposium, through the making of *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk*, Lee told everyone a blunt fact: the film industry is changing dramatically, and this great leap forward could bring unprecedented challenges to every link in film production. Of course, the way industry professionals work must change accordingly. For example, in such high-spec shooting, actors cannot wear makeup, and on-set lighting is completely different from past productions. Lee has made a start with the new technology, but at the same time he worries that the rest of the industry will not follow.

However, when it comes to the problems a brand-new challenge may bring, at least Lee has already encountered them first.

Highlights from the symposium follow:

**Moderator: Why make a film like this?**

**Lee:** Simple — it's good to watch (laughs). I could give many reasons; in interviews I can talk at length about the logic. But when you're doing it, you just want to see it.

When I was making *Life of Pi*, I became very curious. It was my first exposure to 3D, and I found that many things didn't line up for me — including what I had learned in school and my original understanding of how to shoot a film.

When I was at art school, my father gave me a Super 8 film camera. I had a very strong, electric feeling then — I felt all of this could be controlled, and for many years I projected myself into it.

As for the matter of 24 frames, I never thought about it. Thought is produced by overcoming what you don't understand. That is to say, this is why film uses drama to test human nature — if everyone lived their days peacefully, there would be nothing to test. When you run into different situations, things you can't figure out, your true nature comes out. It's the same with technology — we do it three meals a day and live just fine.

I often feel that 24 frames should be a railing of paradise — once you go past that railing, it's as if you've left paradise.

But because I shot *Life of Pi*, I discovered that it was actually unwatchable. That's because 3D is closer to what both eyes see; the more I shot, the more problems appeared. Although in the end I was lucky and the result was fairly successful, in truth I was on tenterhooks — I only dared to use a little 3D, and beyond that the problems came out. I'm not trying to tear down paradise's fence; I just want to push the fence outward a little.

I pursue a higher frame rate, but because I come from a drama background, once the frame rate goes up the film becomes unwatchable — the acting looks silly, the light is wrong, the sets are wrong, everything is wrong. I began to doubt 24 frames. I knew 24 frames had no particular virtue; it was just the cheapest.

Once a film exceeds 60 frames, it starts to look less like a film and more like what our eyes see. That means both the sense of participation and the grammar of cinema have to change, which made me very nervous. I spent more than a year considering whether to go past 60 frames.

At the time almost no one was doing 60 frames, but I really wanted to see a sense of participation as real as reality — with the audience in the first person, not just the third. In other words, the relationship between people and film, and the experience of cinema's situations and stories, would change. So I was very curious.

I don't understand computers at all, but I just kept demanding. If current computers can't do it, build new ones. For the projector, we modified an industrial projector that the U.S. Department of Defense uses for fighter-jet simulation. Only two weeks before shooting did I first see what above 60 frames looked like, so I was very curious what 120 frames per second would look like.

I didn't mention this just now, but my light level is many, many times stronger — about four or five times stronger than the best theaters. When all the information is sufficient, it resembles a term from chemistry, "active energy": when all the materials reach a certain level, a chemical reaction is triggered.

I need to introduce such new things to the audience, but it's hard to explain to the studios, because they just give audiences what they want — and I feel, how can that be? Because the film industry now works like a general election, sometimes it's hard for me to put it into words.

**Jason Chen:** Recently I went to Beijing to see a VR team. In some undersea and aerial footage, they didn't use 90 frames, and in some highly dynamic games it would look unreal. So to be lifelike, going beyond 24 frames is necessary.

![A still from *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk* (Image / Douban Movies)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004131711cabIDgU1rFxBK8os.jpg-w600)

**Lee:** I'd like to add something. I actually think that whether it's 3D or high frame rate, the thing that can present the best is actually the close-up. The most important thing for us humans is reading each other's faces, and that way of reading is very much like our eyes — you can feel a person's feeling from the eyes, see the spirit in them. I think the biggest gain this time may be that it let me see human faces.

So for this film we dared not use makeup — every hair is visible. I think this is very precious; it should be used to shoot drama. When people mention high specs, they always talk about action and spectacle, but I think the most precious thing is filming human faces.

![A still from *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk* (Image / Douban Movies)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004131739ypgdam0OtwKZj6Ss.jpg-w600)

**Moderator: Should we change the way we watch film stories? You just mentioned that in an era of experience, our relationship with the subject of the story changes?**

**Lee:** It's selective. When I want to be close, I can be close. Someone like Trump — I wouldn't watch him that closely; I'd pull back and watch him at 12 frames (laughs).

**Moderator: In 2012, when you were shooting *Life of Pi* in Taiwan, you said, "Taiwan's tech industry is excellent; it can absolutely develop a post-production industry." But four years have passed, and Taiwan still doesn't seem to have found its advantage and entry point in the global film supply chain?**

**Lee:** Actually, I'm not very familiar with Taiwan's industry. I understand computers even less — not only no interest, but no aptitude either.

When I shot *Life of Pi*, I was truly proud. In Los Angeles, in Hollywood, it was absolutely impossible — I got it done in Taiwan. Taiwan is small, but you can find everything. Nobody knows this, but many things were made in Taiwan — for example, the best screens are now made in Tainan. I only learned that when I went to NAB Show.

But for making movies, I feel the industry hasn't been integrated — film people always work alone. At the time I brought outside people in to do it (*Life of Pi*), and we got it done, but once it was finished it was gone; everyone starts from scratch again. It seems experience can't accumulate.

I think it's a real pity that Taiwan doesn't develop post-production, because the people here and the quality of the work are very good. Many young people in Taiwan love film; it's fine to struggle a bit at first, fine to do contract manufacturing. In the past, our imagination of filmmaking was very linear — preparation, layout, shooting, editing, step by step to completion. But the way I make films now, before pre-production has even begun, I'm already doing post-production. After all, Taiwan's shooting conditions are very limited, and a film isn't just about shooting — especially in the digital era, every stage can be controlled; it's not rigidly following one fixed way of shooting.

Like now when I shoot, actors can't wear makeup; in the future we'll have to find a way to solve that — to use the unbeautiful beautifully. I think we are "making films" rather than "shooting films."

**Jason Chen:** (First discussing cost issues in the tech industry) Technology is a good tool and foundation, but how it's applied still depends on people. Over the past 20 years, apart from IMAX, after the shift to digital things have actually stagnated somewhat. Now phones and home TVs can already match the quality of the small screens in cinemas. So I think in the future, cinemas should be built to feel like watching an NBA arena or a Super Bowl stadium — that sense of presence is irreplaceable. When cinemas make films into such a unique viewing experience, audiences will walk into theaters to feel this 120 frames, 3D, 4K. The technology can already do it; it's expensive now, but once it goes into mass production, prices can of course come down. If that's the case, it would be very much worth looking forward to whichever director shoots at 60 or 120 frames. Cinemas should of course use good equipment to present better stories, and then you get a virtuous cycle.

**Lee:** I'd like to add something. Whether in the past or now, I can still defend film to the death, even though film isn't very environmentally friendly. I haven't opposed film or told everyone they must shoot digitally — shoot film if you shoot film, digital if you shoot digital. What I've found is that now everyone uses digital to imitate film, but hasn't reached film — that doesn't make sense; it's going backward. Digital must have its own things; it needs to move forward.

In fact, I didn't create this format; I just reflect what I see. I feel technology and animation have walked ahead of us — it's just that those of us who make films are slower.

So I think we should give back. These companies (technology, animation and related industries) all want to move forward; they need filmmakers to tell them what new things we want. For example: speed is fast, exposure time is short, so you need a lot of light. Once the light is insufficient, depth of field goes shallow. Or: the shooting machine now is so big — how do I shoot a boxing film next time? In other words, to satisfy all these demands, the downstream stages have to keep up.

I never feel technology and art are two different things. People often ask me, "Why do you mess with technology?" But these two are one thing.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004131340bkf2qHMrsLwaQTXh.jpg-w600)

**Moderator: What you're doing is very cutting-edge; many things are still being explored. How do you communicate with the crew, and how do you persuade the financiers? How do you convince everyone that this is right?**

**Lee:** I need to spend a long time telling them that actually you're not good enough — and neither am I. For Americans, "you are not good enough" is not an easy thing to say, but I still said it.

Often by the third week of shooting, I discover that something has been done for a hundred years and nobody knows why it's done that way — and shooting it that way is wrong. You have to spot that kind of error. If you think you're great, then you're done for — so the first thing is humility. That's quite hard. When every industry has gotten this good, it doesn't just have confidence and technique; it has its pride and identity. Tell a Hollywood 3D person "this won't work," and he'll lose his temper — then you have to mediate, to communicate, to tell him the truth, hurt him, and then coax him. With the studios, it's coax and bluff (laughs). So taking this long isn't my work — it's all gone into communication.

Plus how to commercially distribute in the end — how to make each thing happen within this industrial environment, how to connect and link them up — and finally distribution and viewing habits. To reverse viewing habits is of course very difficult.

I think in the end you can dislike my movie, but don't curse the "medium." We've just gotten started. I only hope that when audiences watch, they know this is something worth pursuing — that alone is enough.

**Moderator: In terms of leading the team — are these people long-term collaborators, or do you work case by case?**

**Lee:** Apart from the editor, I don't have a fixed crew; mostly I find people to work with case by case. As for leading a team — basically everyone quite wants to work with me. Once an idea goes out, it becomes everyone's faith; we share the hardships together. Whether feeding lions or whatever, we hold hands — I'm like a missionary. But because it's new, they also have doubts; basically I'm quite honest too, and won't over-coax them, because there's a limit to that as well.

But I think most people feel this experience is worthwhile. Actually, if someone loves this industry, they usually welcome this kind of well-intentioned torment — they like challenges, like things that can't be done. Even if they fail and can't make it, there's still a bit of vitality. I lean toward working with people like that.

People who already think they're great — I mostly ignore them. I'm not even great; how can you be great? Everyone should be learning all the same. And I really dislike the kind of person who, being a cinematographer, talks photography all day — I find that very not OK: just get your own patch right and ignore everything else. With people like that I wonder, why are you even in film? I'd rather have them talk to me about drama, film, life, literature — anything is better than talking about their own technical trade.

**Moderator: You're already very successful; you could play it safe. Why keep walking into risky new territory — especially now that you've entered the age of "no confusion"?**

**Lee:** Past sixty, I'm really starting to be confused again. Simply put, it's like Madonna's song "Like a Virgin" — I hope every time feels like the first time.

I think it's personality; sincerity matters a lot. When making films becomes second nature and you discover the formula, it's best to change even the method.

In the past everyone made films this way without thinking whether it was good or not. Take an example: the waves Hollywood makes are like a bathtub — water sloshing around in a big tank. That doesn't look like waves; it's a bathtub.

If I had shot (*Life of Pi*) in Hollywood, someone would say: I've won several Oscars, I made *Titanic* and so on — but you just don't know how to do it. Hollywood's rain — I've never once seen rain done well; it's all unwatchable, but nobody questions it.

![A shooting location for *Life of Pi*, at Taichung's Shuinan Airport](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004132831p6gadvjyAkJlINcW.jpg-w600)

An ocean's wavelength is actually very long — it doesn't slosh back and forth. I wanted to make a new one; Hollywood just couldn't do it, so I brought it to Taiwan and invented it myself. We cut the waves down so they wouldn't come back at us, and we could make the wavelength longer. Although it wasn't quite a real sea — it was the feeling of "open sea" — I think it's very interesting. But to this day, I still haven't seen anyone do it this way.

Of course telling a story is still very important; but beyond that, I feel what you say and how you tell the story are equally important, because film is an experience. How your body feels it — intuitively, when the audience sees that image, how they breathe with it, communicate with it — that is the essence of film. The joys and sorrows in a story, when you get down to it, are also a form. What's most precious about film is your intuitive relationship with it, and that has a lot to do with how you shoot and with technology.

So content and technology are not two different things; they are two sides of one body.

**Jason Chen:** Everyone thinks Ang Lee is gentle and refined, but when he answers, his essence is a creative, slightly rebellious Ang Lee. You can pretty much tell that after sixty he'll keep exploring and creating, and keep rebelling — he won't settle for the film industry's existing conventions; rules, frameworks or technical structures can't satisfy him.

In fact, for the tech industry, change is its very nature. We often find that hardware and software move at different speeds. Once hardware is developed to a certain point, before the investment is even recovered, new technology arrives again. For hardware makers, of course there's a strong desire for the content industry to drive demand — without content, what would people do with hardware? The tech industry always has the impulse to invent and create, but if applications can't keep up, there is difficulty.

**Moderator: Will you keep challenging higher technology?**

**Lee:** I'm just afraid the next film in this spec will be my next film, and the one after that will still be mine — then I'll retire (laughs). I really hope my peers, whatever they feel, will get their hands on it and try. This has to become an industry. Television is the obvious example: TVs can already show 3D without glasses, but without the software and the demand, making it would be pointless — so I hope it doesn't end up that way.

I'll do my best to sell it everywhere — not just *Billy Lynn's Long Halftime Walk* the movie, but at least to tell my peers this is a possibility. I keep saying: I'm not crazy, I haven't gone mad — I really saw it; it's a very beautiful thing. It's fine if you don't like the movie; just know this thing is worth pursuing.

Bringing audiences back to the theater really isn't easy now, but I believe in the theater — especially now that we no longer go to churches or temples. We need to communicate; everyone gathers to watch one thing — the director shares something spiritual with everyone. You need to pretend to enter an imaginary world, then go verify your life and find order.

I think people are very instinctive. Since the beginning of our history — whether lighting a torch in a cave to tell a story — film will keep moving forward; this is only the beginning. You can watch on an iPhone if you like, but watching and experiencing together with many people — I think that is a very important activity for touching the truth inside people's hearts.

Someone asked me what I think of VR. I said I don't know yet, but as a filmmaker I'd rather believe "my VR is not your VR." I think this upgrade — in technology, in art, in every way.

I really hope to bring audiences back to the theater.
