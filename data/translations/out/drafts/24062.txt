Do You Know the Ins and Outs of Subway Advertising?

@@QD_BODY@@
This underground world charts the rise and fall of industries and companies.

Why are subway cars plastered with e-commerce ads? And why have Liepin ads — endorsed by Hu Ge — recently taken over the Beijing and Shanghai subways? Which subway ad slots cost the most, and who is buying them…

As one of the main daily commutes for office workers, apart from the crush of rush hour, what you can't avoid in the subway is the motley assortment of advertisements. Whether you watch them actively or absorb them passively, they actually reflect a cross-section of the business world. The shift from FMCG ads in the past to e-commerce today is a change in the market landscape. And when a company starts burning money on ads everywhere, that too is closely tied to its business strategy.

This underground world charts the rise and fall of industries and companies.

Since 1997, when the first form of advertising appeared on Beijing subway platforms — a 12-sheet light box — subway ads have become part of life for modern urbanites.

From 2009 to 2013, the number of operating subway lines in China grew rapidly from 39 to 81, and is expected to reach 176 by 2018.

The subway advertising market expanded along with it. [According to China Industry Information Network's 《2014-2019年中国地铁广告市场调研与投资前景报告》 (2014–2019 China Subway Advertising Market Research and Investment Outlook Report)](http://www.qmtmedia.com/yejieshuju/29-108.html), China's subway advertising revenue grew from US$220 million in 2009 to US$580 million in 2013, and is expected to jump to US$3.84 billion by 2018.

In China, the operators of subway advertising in eight cities including Beijing and Shanghai all come from the French out-of-home advertising company JCDecaux. JCDecaux forms joint ventures with each city's subway company to represent subway media resources; in Beijing and Shanghai these are called Beijing Subway Tongcheng and Shanghai Shentong JCDecaux respectively.

In Shanghai, 95% of subway ad slots are operated by Shentong JCDecaux.

Beijing is relatively more complicated: advertising resources across all lines are basically operated by nearly 20 media companies. Among them, Beijing Subway Tongcheng covers about 75% of the ad share; next is Sanmu International, which handles advertising on suburban lines such as Changping, Yizhuang, and Fangshan; Line 4 is commercially operated by Asiaray.

Different ad formats on the same line may also have different agents. For example, Tongcheng represents the mainstream formats on its lines — light boxes, wall murals, interior train wraps, exterior train wraps, and pillar wraps — while tunnel dynamic video ads, carriage handle-strap ads, escalator-side stickers, subway door and window ads, guide-sign ads, and the big screens at station entrances are each represented by different companies.

We chose Beijing and Shanghai as our samples; by passenger volume, these are the busiest and second-busiest subway systems in the world, each with average daily ridership above 10 million.

Because Beijing Subway Tongcheng and Shanghai Shentong JCDecaux operate the vast majority of subway media resources, we talked with people at both companies, hoping to uncover the rules by which subway advertising operates — and the business logic behind it.

## Which station has the most expensive ads? Who is buying them?

Just like ads in the slot before the CCTV Spring Festival Gala begins — whichever company shows its face there is taken as a symbol of brand strength. In subway media, the equivalent would be: which brand has "painted the walls" of the transfer corridor at Beijing's Guomao or Shanghai's Jing'an Temple.

The two areas share much in common — the stations sit in core CBD commercial districts and are transfer stations for several major lines, with heavy foot traffic (the Guomao transfer corridor reaches more than 300,000 transfer and entering/exiting passengers daily).

![The Guomao transfer corridor](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145210v14muk0xDMjYEn8h.jpg-w600)

![The Jing'an Temple transfer corridor](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145211shKGx8bHoFjeDv1Z.jpg-w600)

The Jing'an Temple station is surrounded by an office-tower commercial district that includes Donghai Plaza, Shanghai Plaza, and Jing An Kerry Centre. Running ads for four weeks in the Jing'an Temple transfer corridor costs 2.58 million yuan at rate-card price; at Guomao it is 1.68 million.

The difference between the two prices is not because Shanghai is more expensive, but because the Guomao transfer corridor only has wall murals, while the Jing'an Temple corridor offers more media formats — besides wall murals, there are 28 light boxes and pillar wraps. (In fact, overall, Beijing subway ad prices are about 20% higher than Shanghai's.)

In this Jing'an Temple transfer corridor, priced at 2.58 million yuan for four weeks, the brands that have appeared include Estée Lauder and the badminton brand Yonex, as well as internet companies such as Zhihu, Liepin, and Mogujie, which recently ran video brand ads there.

In the Guomao transfer corridor, the most recent walls were taken by JD.com's "Butterfly Festival" and Liepin ads.

## How are different stations priced?

Before pricing, the agency companies divide all subway stations into three tiers — S, A++, and A — with prices descending accordingly.

Take Shanghai as an example: 19 stations including West Nanjing Road, Lujiazui, Jing'an Temple, and Xujiahui are the top-tier S class, while Changshu Road and Shanghai Stadium rank a notch lower at A++; stations with still lower traffic and surrounding commercial value fall into class A.

![Class S stations on the Beijing Subway](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145212VprH3EMDyAz5qmNf.jpg-w600)

![Class S stations on the Shanghai Metro](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145212cqkgKuBsPtTWlzAY.jpg-w600)

Passenger flow is the main basis for the tiers, but not the only one. Whether a station sits in a core area is also weighed. Tiantongyuan station, for instance, has enormous traffic but is class A, the lowest — because Tiantongyuan is remote, its passenger flow is concentrated in the morning and evening peaks, and the media environment in packed crowds is not great either.

![Distribution of commercial districts along the Shanghai Metro (source: Shentong JCDecaux)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145213S9EkJxuCGODBsIAj.jpg-w600)

Station tiers are also adjusted each year. Take Shanghai's South Shaanxi Road station: the Line 1 section used to be class S and the Line 10 section class A++ (yes, a single station can be split in two). With the opening of Line 12, South Shaanxi Road became a three-line transfer station, and with the dense surrounding commercial district, the entire station was upgraded to class S.

By comparison, downgrades are rare. A person at Shanghai Shentong JCDecaux told QDaily, "In the 1990s subways were built around commercial districts, but now the dynamic is reversed: wherever the subway goes, property developments and commercial districts follow — that is the value of the subway. So the amenities around mature stations only get better; downgrade is unlikely."

Different tiers mean different media value for these stations. Take the most common "12-sheet light box": for a four-week run, the rate-card price at a class S station is 108,000 yuan, at an A++ station 78,000 yuan, and at an A station just 38,000 yuan.

Despite the huge price gaps, companies large and small still most want the class S stations. Ad slots are usually sold in S/A++/A combinations.

There are exceptions. Supermarket, mall, and property ads, for example, only run at nearby stations. Beijing's Babaoshan station is said to have even hosted ads for a cemetery.

It should be noted that "rate-card price" means exactly that — a standard for reference only. In practice, agencies grant varying discounts depending on how much a company books. The Guomao transfer corridor priced at 1.88 million yuan for four weeks, for instance, typically closes at 85% of the rate-card price.

## Besides the most common light boxes, what other forms does subway advertising take?

The "12-sheet light box" is probably the most common form of subway advertising — the kind you see while waiting on the platform.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145214gl85i07NhIDbXMSc.jpg-w600)

Conventional media like light boxes count as "coverage media," achieving reach through higher frequency of contact.

Other, flashier formats count as "impact media" — interior train wraps, exterior train wraps, wall murals — boosting brand image through strong visual impact.

Of course, some cases are less subway advertising than event marketing. Tencent's game 《天天飞车》 (*Tiantian Racing*) once took over an entire subway corridor, designing the floor and walls in racing style, complete with racing sounds as you walked through. The corridor itself didn't have heavy traffic; their real aim was to use it as a gimmick for secondary spread via TV and the internet.

## Who is buying subway ads?

A little over two years ago, subway advertising was the domain of FMCG brands. Back then, you would only spot a few large e-commerce players such as Taobao, JD.com, and Yihaodian.

But starting at the end of 2013, the internet became the number-one industry by subway ad spending. Today, nearly half the ads you see in the subway come from a motley array of internet companies.

In a way, this tracks the market's trajectory. The ads you see are only the surface; behind them lies the rise and fall of companies and industries.

Just as around 2010, when group-buying sites were booming and their ads were plastered everywhere. But as the market withered after the "hundred-site war," the ads quickly disappeared too.

Break the internet industry down further, and e-commerce is hands down the biggest ad spender.

In the Shanghai Metro alone, Alibaba's annual ad budget is around 50 million yuan; Taobao, Tmall, and Alitrip basically run without interruption year-round. Besides fellow "big spenders" like JD.com and Suning, there are also smaller emerging e-commerce players such as Yangmatou and Mia.

From three years ago, when only major e-commerce companies advertised, to now, when e-commerce players of every size are everywhere — you can roughly gauge how hot this industry is.

On the other hand, FMCG — an industry once known for valuing marketing — has clearly cut back on offline ads and shifted toward online marketing. QDaily elaborated on this trend in a previous [article about the youthification of the FMCG industry](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/19998.html).

For big companies, the coming year's ad plan is roughly set by the end of the previous year, then fine-tuned. Startups, by contrast, are more volatile: they usually don't plan that far ahead and decide based on need.

"Companies that can afford subway ads usually got their start a while back and are in a growth phase. Because subway ads are quite expensive — if you buy just one or two light boxes, you get drowned out; to make noise you have to go big. So small companies typically advertise right after raising a round of funding, when they want audiences to recognize them fast," said a person at Beijing Subway Tongcheng.

Take Lagou, which recently wrapped entire stations in green. Previously it was known only within the internet industry. After two rounds of financing, Lagou began advertising at scale from 2015; during this year's recruitment season in February and March, its subway ad spending across Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen reached the tens of millions of yuan.

Compared with bus-shelter ads outdoors, the subway environment is relatively clean, and the brand image more premium. That has to do with the subway audience — according to the 2014 China Marketing and Media Study, 82% of subway passengers are under 45, more than 70% hold an associate degree or higher, and over 80% are white-collar workers.

So some companies hope to lift their brand stature through subway media. Take those "XX Dai" lenders you've never heard of — if you saw one of their ads in the subway, wouldn't you find it at least somewhat more trustworthy?

## Subway advertising is highly "seasonal"

Lagou, Liepin, Boss Zhipin, Zhaopin, ChinaHR.com… If you commute by subway daily, your chances of dodging these ads over the month or so since Chinese New Year are vanishingly small. (Oh, and there's plenty on office-building TV too.)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145219zC4ts8vwjG2bcm7T.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702145219TZgb7PGEUCInk1c5.jpg-w600)

"Collect your year-end bonus, then jump ship" — after Chinese New Year every year is peak season for recruitment ads. Though they make up a small share of subway ads across the year, recruitment ads account for 40–50% in February and March.

The same kind of "seasonal" ad spending happens elsewhere: as the weather warms, April and May are peak season for travel and beverage ads, and the highlight of the second half of the year is naturally "Double 11."

On cost, peak months (January, April, May, September, October, December) carry a 15% media surcharge over the off-season.

## What happens during an ad "gap period"?

Because subway ads come in so many formats, the fill rate is hard to calculate comprehensively. A rough estimate puts the fill rate at class S stations at 75% or above.

Every subway company must meet an annual quota of 3–5% public-service ads. The fire-safety notices and "Beijing's Top Ten Sights" you see all fall in this category.

When a commercial ad comes down, the usual practice is to replace it with a public-service ad or with imagery of the agency's own brand — in any case, a blank panel can't be left up there.

As for movie posters whose films are long out of theaters but still hanging in the subway, that's usually because no new ad has been booked to replace them.
