JD.com Walked the Runway at London Fashion Week, But It May Really Have Been Just a "Show"

Making noise overseas, selling traffic back home. JD.com's approach to fashion is, in the end, a bit crude.

On the afternoon of September 19, Chris Spira braved the rain to get to 116 Pall Mall in central London, where he was going to watch a fashion show by a Chinese brand. It was the second-to-last day of London Fashion Week Spring/Summer 2017, and Burberry's "see now, buy now" show was about to begin that evening, but this partner at private equity firm True Capital — which specializes in investing in consumer goods and fashion — had no interest in joining the crowds at the main venue. Instead, he was saving his time for "Chinese fashion," a world he didn't yet know well.

The show, called "Jing·Zhi," was staged by JD.com, which had come all the way to London to announce the launch of a "customization" business covering both high-end bespoke and personalized made-to-order.

Put simply, JD.com teamed up with domestic custom brands including these six to launch the "Jing·Zhi" apparel customization channel, selling clothing, footwear, jewelry, accessories and more. Through this channel, consumers can choose details including style, fabric and size, then place an order.

Unlike its previous two runway outings at Milan and New York Fashion Week, where it brought the work of Chinese independent designers, JD.com introduced six brands to London this time: EVE de CINA, NE·TIGER, Kevin Kelly, By Creations, X·Lando and Hengmeirui Jewelry. Not familiar? Let's put it this way — reportedly, each of these brands has more or less something to do with customization. EVE de CINA has been doing custom work for 22 years and has 500 stores across the country. NE·TIGER, which has produced the opening show of China International Fashion Week (colloquially "Beijing Fashion Week") 16 times, has long been in the customization business, with a third of its sales coming from Europe, the Middle East and Russia. And By Creations, which already has its own e-commerce platform selling Italian menswear, also focuses on made-to-measure.

Even though JD.com timed the show to coincide with London Fashion Week, "Jing·Zhi" was not on the official LFW schedule, and the venue was not the main site where Burberry and Paul Smith were showing. Instead, it was held at the club of the Institute of Directors (IOD) — the very 116 Pall Mall that Chris Spira went to.

Known as "the association of British chief executives," the IOD was founded in 1903 and today has more than 34,000 members drawn from Britain's business elite. Since 1978, this old house — built in 1826 and owned by the Crown Estate — has been the IOD's home.

On show day, the first floor of 116 Pall Mall remained open to members who pay an annual fee just to come in for coffee, while the "Jing·Zhi" show for the six brands was held on the second floor, in a room hung with 10 oil paintings each roughly 2.5 meters tall and 1 meter wide.

Apart from By Creations, which presented modern tailoring, the other four fashion brands all brought looks steeped in Chinese flavor — embroidery, qipao-style cuts, silk fabrics. As models walked the runway showing custom pieces with Chinese ethnic-style embroidery, the "larger-than-life" figures in the paintings — Lord Collingwood, King George V, Queen Mary — stared intently as they "admired" this China chic, a striking contrast with the strong whiff of the Tudor and Stuart dynasties, and of Bloody Mary, that the old house exuded.

Still, this was probably what excited Chris Spira. In his words, he "really liked an EVE de CINA gown, the first look on the runway, white with red embroidered floral patterns, and the model with her hair in a bun."

But Chris Spira had no idea what "JD.com" was. Although he once invested in Mary Katrantzou, an independent designer brand known for its prints, he knows very little about Chinese fashion brands and had never been to Beijing or Shanghai to see a runway show by Chinese independent designers. He came to JD.com's show this time because "a friend brought him along — he wanted to take the opportunity to see what Chinese fashion brands are doing these days." So much so that he walked in wearing a pair of blue Converse sneakers and a casual canvas jacket.

Unlike Chris Spira, who was there "for the spectacle," Alex Commbe showed up at the venue in a full suit. He is senior business development manager at Debenhams, the London department store founded in 1778, and had come at JD.com's invitation, "considering whether to work with JD.com."

Attracting people like Alex Commbe and Chris Spira to the show, then sending the scene — with its distinctively British flavor — back home for publicity was probably the main purpose of JD.com's London trip.

Five years ago, it would have been hard to imagine this e-commerce company — which had pivoted from 3C (computers, communications and consumer electronics) and whose users were overwhelmingly male in its early days — saying it wanted to sell clothes. In 2011, JD.com began expanding into all product categories. Major appliances, books and general merchandise all became key categories. In 2015, JD.com restructured its organization and elevated apparel to a higher level. The Apparel and Home Furnishings Business Unit became one of four newly established business units (the other three being 3C, Home Appliances, and Consumer Goods), responsible for coordinating the former third-party open platform (POP) operations — which also meant greater autonomy and resource-allocation power.

As a result, over the past year JD.com has been active on the apparel front: several hundred international brands, including GAP, New Balance, Levi's and Hugo Boss, have opened stores on JD.com, with brand launch events happening nearly every month on average. In October 2014, JD.com sponsored the iQiyi fashion reality show *I, Supermodel* (《爱上超模》); starting in 2014, it has held two regular "Shang JD" fashion shows each year, in spring and autumn; in March 2015 it partnered with the China Fashion Association to build an online support platform for designer brands; and then, JD.com took Chinese independent designers including Gu Lin, Xu Xiaoyan, Tan Ali and Zhang Chi to Milan and New York Fashion Week.

JD.com has been remarkably persistent in its fashion ambitions — and although outside opinion on JD.com dabbling in fashion is mixed, the company keeps sending the world signals about its fashion ambitions. Just as it insisted on holding events outside the main venue of Milan's furniture fair in the first half of the year, walking the runway outside the official schedule of London Fashion Week is part of building atmosphere.

"JD.com's customers are mainly domestic. We have an international business unit focused on Russia and Southeast Asia; we haven't expanded to the US or the UK — the main goal is to bring international brands into China," said Xin Lijun, vice president of JD.com and president of its Apparel and Home Furnishings Business Unit. This also explains the purpose of JD.com's London trip — at least, it was not to open up the British market.

Still, selling apparel is a hard business, especially with a super player like Tmall already in the market.

After all, in terms of scale, JD.com still lags far behind Tmall. According to the second-calendar-quarter earnings both JD.com and Alibaba reported in August, Alibaba's gross merchandise volume for April–June 2016 was 837 billion yuan, of which Tmall accounted for 329 billion yuan, or 40 percent, up 33 percent year on year. JD.com's GMV over the same period was lower than Tmall's, but grew faster: excluding virtual goods, JD.com's GMV reached 157.1 billion yuan, up 52 percent year on year. Beyond the gap in scale, Tmall's other advantage is brand authorization, which means consumers trust it more than JD.com on the question of genuine goods.

Meanwhile, although fashion e-commerce players like ASOS — which never quite understood Chinese consumers — have exited the Chinese market, the remaining e-commerce companies are each pursuing their own "little calculations."

Vipshop does nothing but flash sales, hoping to win on price. NET-A-PORTER, the luxury e-commerce site with brand authorization, is thriving in the Chinese market: according to its first-quarter fiscal 2016 results released in May, its sales in China rose 15.9 percent. Farfetch takes the "buyer's boutique" route, selling niche products and taste. The latecomer WOOLALA focuses on Asian designers and even borrows the gimmick of the American dating app Tinder, letting consumers swipe left or right on its interface to quickly register their preferences.

So what can JD.com, with its 169 million active users, actually do in fashion?

"The people JD.com serves are middle- to high-income consumers in China, mostly aged 25 and above. Compared with domestic competitors, we won't fight over the very young consumers under 25 — 17- and 18-year-olds are not JD.com's users," Xin Lijun, vice president of JD.com and president of its Apparel and Home Furnishings Business Unit, said at the "Jing·Zhi" show launch. "We only do the high-end quality market for our demographic; we choose designers according to our positioning and build a designer platform."

Twenty-five. At JD.com, that age apparently already serves to describe "middle-to-high-end," and it's the kind of age annotation internet products use when they want to say their users aren't entirely young. But for the fashion retail industry, 25 is far too young — Nike targets 22-year-olds. Not to mention that the vast majority of 25-year-olds simply cannot afford "high-end" and "custom-made."

What's more, the independent-designer business is not an easy one. This group has its innate advantages — young, innovative, even unique; they are the future of fashion, the ones from whom the next Tom Ford or Alexander Wang might emerge. But they also have a fatal weakness: generally speaking, their designs are not easy to commercialize and hard to push into normal retail channels.

In international markets, fashion magazines and buyer boutiques are currently the main arenas for discovering and making independent designers famous. Anna Wintour, editor-in-chief of American *Vogue* and the "ice queen" of fashion, was the pioneer of this cause — she successfully made Alexander Wang a star. In addition, in this field you will often hear the names of buyer boutiques that made designers famous: H.Lorenzo in Los Angeles, or Opening Ceremony. What they do is simple — give independent designers a chance to become known, and get their designs into retail channels where they can meet consumers.

At Shanghai Fashion Week Autumn/Winter 2016, Sara Maino, a senior fashion editor at Italian *Vogue* and head of its emerging-designer program Vogue Talent, shuttled between trade fairs and showrooms in Shanghai hoping to discover more interesting designers — but too few of them met her standards.

This is indeed the predicament facing Chinese independent designers: if the design is unremarkable, mass production and entry into retail channels become problematic further down the line.

In terms of influence, JD.com of course cannot compare with the fashion bible *Vogue* or the promotional power of streetwear boutiques, so the odds that an independent designer can make a name by simply opening a store on JD.com are slim, and sales won't be too encouraging either. Now that JD.com has entered the territory of Chinese independent designers, it naturally cannot avoid these problems.

This explains why JD.com touts its "supporter" image — because apart from technical support and traffic, it has very little to give designers in return.

"This customization business requires a strong supply chain behind it — the manufacturing craftsmanship has to support customization. This time the concept is different from the previous two shows, and the participating brands are different too," said Liu Hong, general manager of the Apparel Department of JD.com's Apparel and Home Furnishings Business Unit.

That said, brands playing at customization in China do seem to need an e-commerce company like JD.com, and Eve Group is an obvious example. For this London trip, JD.com brought along two of Eve Group's brands in one go: EVE de CINA and Kevin Kelly.

Founded in 1994, Eve Group currently has five brands under its umbrella: EVE de UOMO (menswear), NOTTING HILL, Kevin Kelly, JAQUES PRITT and EVE de CINA. With 500 stores across the country, it offers not only made-to-measure tailoring but also butler-style service that provides styling advice.

In the telling of Xia Hua, chair of Eve Group, Yu Minhong — who previously wore nothing but T-shirts and jeans — is one of her loyal customers. "The butler service lets him wear a suit in everyday situations without having to tie a tie — it keeps him at ease. That one thing turned Yu Minhong and his more than 300 school principals into our customers," Xia Hua said. Another source of pride for her is that her customization service can "take a customer's 24 measurements in 2 minutes without putting them off."

Although Eve Group has developed its own distinctive approach to customization, Xia Hua has never considered doing e-commerce herself. In Xin Lijun's words: "In China, it's unrealistic for a proprietary brand to do e-commerce on its own, because you'd be competing with JD.com and Alibaba."

"All traditional companies have been hurt this way. Management today isn't about managing competition; it's about managing cooperation. I definitely won't do everything myself," Xia Hua said. "JD.com can bring me precisely the traffic I want."

Once a consumer places an order, Eve Group kicks off what Xia Hua calls the "butler service" — an important step that doesn't sound like it needs JD.com's involvement at all. This is still quite a distance from how young niche foreign brands use e-commerce to play with customization.

Fame and Partners, a Sydney brand that had just entered the US market in 2015, also uses the internet to do customization. This niche brand no longer emphasizes the basic concept of fit — it emphasizes style.

Say you want to buy the Ada, a halter-neck, open-back dress with a side slit. First, you can change the nude and pink colors of the sample Fame and Partners provides — there are 31 colors to choose from, including black, brown and champagne. If you want a custom color, you pay an extra 16 US dollars.

If you don't like the Ada as is, you can remove the front slit, or add a second slit to mirror the original one so the front becomes double-slit, or shorten the dress to mini length.

Essentially, Fame and Partners' customization concept puts e-commerce to use: you can complete all your design requirements at the moment of ordering, without seeking help from an offline "tailor" or "butler."

If JD.com can't support customization down to that level of detail, it will land in a slightly awkward position: in the face of traffic, there is no such thing as loyalty.

"We go wherever the traffic is," Xia Hua said. Eve Group's other brand, EVE de UOMO, simply opened a store on Tmall.

And for Lorenzo Caramalli, general manager of BY CREATIONS' European operation, he is likewise interested in getting onto Tmall.

"JD.com came to us," Lorenzo Caramalli told QDaily.

NE·TIGER isn't putting all its eggs in one basket either. After six months of talks with JD.com, NE·TIGER started this customization business with the company — but at the same time it is building its own customization e-commerce platform. "We're building a fairly large global quality-of-life platform, due to go live around October, aimed at designers worldwide — not like JD.com, which only targets Chinese designers," Zhang Zhifeng, NE·TIGER's creative director, told QDaily.

Another important issue is that "customization" carries a whiff of premium. Beyond the craftsmanship — where Xia Hua places great weight on "made-to-measure" and "styling experience" — partnering with an e-commerce company raises problems at the consumption-experience stage.

If you click on an EVE de CINA custom garment sold on JD.com, the product description that comes up is quite terse — nowhere near as thorough and considerate as an ordinary Taobao seller's. Below the garment, JD.com will make other fashion recommendations for you. And going by JD.com's algorithm, besides similar custom-brand products, a 699-yuan Vero Moda dress will also pop up.

This may be a bit like the mixed feelings Chris Spira had watching the show at 116 Pall Mall. "I don't know what this show ultimately wants to express," Chris Spira said, "but I suppose we all need to keep learning."
