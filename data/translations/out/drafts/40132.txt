Seven years after 3/11, they built 14 "Homes for All" for disaster victims — is this a performance, or does it genuinely work?

In the seven years since the Great East Japan Earthquake, the Japanese government has been repeatedly criticized for its inefficiency on disaster resettlement and community rebuilding. The discussion has focused on material repair: the government-provided temporary housing has safety hazards, and how to cut costs and produce it at scale. Meanwhile, a group of architects began building public facilities inside these temporary resettlement sites, hoping to heal psychological trauma. Is that a luxury?

![The Home-for-All in Higashi-Matsushima (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425150500TQPB0G8wqtelURgW.jpeg-w600)

The "Home-for-All" (Home-for-All) project was initiated by Japanese architect Toyo Ito to build public facilities — indoor children's playgrounds, fishermen's clubhouses, farm-produce markets and the like — in the three northeastern prefectures hardest hit by the earthquake, the tsunami and the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear leak (Iwate, Miyagi and Fukushima).

The Home-for-All project is included in "Toyo Ito: On the Stream," a retrospective that opened on April 22 at the Power Station of Art in Shanghai. Compared with the signature works of Ito's four-decade career — Sendai Mediatheque, the Taichung Opera House, the Baroque Art Museum in Mexico — the Home-for-All projects are gathered on a single circular platform, with no models, and even the project's name is plain: "everyone's home."

!["Toyo Ito: On the Stream" exhibition view; the circular platform documents the Home-for-All practice (Photo / Power Station of Art)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704251426030OaSz736FCkrn8QR.jpeg-w600)

![At the exhibition, Toyo Ito standing behind the Taichung Opera House model (Photo / Power Station of Art)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704251506027fxz0KMyRFJ9TCIv.jpg-w600)

At the Japanese pavilion of the 2012 Venice Biennale, Toyo Ito and three younger architects — Sou Fujimoto, Kumiko Inui and Akihisa Hirata — presented a Home-for-All they designed for the city of Rikuzentakata: a watchtower-like structure on a seaside slope, ringed by 19 thick raw logs about 60 centimeters in diameter, timber selected from local Kesen cedar trees that had died after saltwater inundation left the soil saline. The exhibition documented, in a documentary manner, the protracted back-and-forth of this public building: of the 120 scheme models, some were vetoed by the architects among themselves, others rejected by frowning residents.

The exhibition won that edition of the Biennale's Golden Lion, and the following year Ito received the Pritzker Prize. It posed a valuable question: Architecture, possible here?

The Home-for-All was derided at the time as "a politically correct performance," yet it endured as an action. In 2014 a nonprofit organization was established, and 14 Homes-for-All of varying sizes were gradually built across the three northeastern prefectures.

![The Home-for-All in Rikuzentakata; the 2012 Venice Biennale Japanese pavilion presented the project's entire process (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425142736yBTUi0E91dwILkc3.jpg-w600)

This is not the first time Ito has addressed the publicness of architecture. Thirty years ago, in Tokyo still in its bubble era, Ito set up an installation inside a shopping mall, "Tokyo Nomad Women's Dwelling": inside a model made of translucent material were displayed a bed, a radio, a dresser and a simple tea corner. The young Kazuyo Sejima was the model in this experimental installation. Recalling those days, Ito describes how people off work crowded into taverns, and after the gatherings the streets were still lively — you couldn't even hail a taxi. "We were like nomads, able to make our home anywhere." He stripped away, one by one, the functions a traditional "home" provides: single migrants living in the metropolis of Tokyo could leave their houses and obtain food, entertainment and social connection in the city itself.

Thirty years later, however, the people walking out of evacuation shelters and temporary housing faced a reality that had collapsed. The top-down government relief and resettlement plan could satisfy only food, shelter and safety, with no room for the spiritual dimension of community repair. The temporary housing was container-type prefabricated steel-framed units, whose poor performance was a frequent topic of discussion; public space was a distant luxury.

"But what bothers me more is a whole row of houses that look exactly alike — such an inhumane setup. This kind of egalitarianism, this uniformity, is expressed not only in the temporary housing but also symbolizes how spiritually barren Japan has become," Ito wrote critically in his book 《反建筑》 (Anti-Architecture). "To ensure privacy, each household only closes itself off further; whether neighbors beside you or rows in front and behind, people may cease all contact — I've heard that quite a few people, after moving into temporary housing, became hikikomori." According to official Japanese statistics from September 2016, "disaster-related deaths" had reached 3,523 — including those who died from deteriorating health and those who took their own lives due to mental illness brought on by the prolonged evacuation life.

![Sendai Mediatheque (Photo / Power Station of Art)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425143838gE7PQnSmJ28ZaGqb.jpg-w600)

The Sendai Mediatheque designed by Toyo Ito "survived" the 3/11 earthquake; the main building structure was unaffected, yet Ito's team still spent two months repairing the damaged interior. During those two months, architects like himself visited the disaster zones, the evacuation shelters and the temporary resettlement sites, hoping to do something to reduce the "man-made disaster" that follows a "natural disaster."

Gymnasiums are common evacuation shelters; people stay there briefly, at most a few months, before drawing lots to move into government temporary housing. The last evacuation shelter closed in 2012, and within a year all disaster victims in the shelters had been transferred. But the "temporary" in temporary housing often lasts years. According to a Kyodo News survey, as of February 2017, 123,000 people from the three hardest-hit northeastern prefectures were still living as evacuees away from home, and of those who remained, 34,000 still lived in temporary housing units, the government's housing reconstruction plan progressing slowly.

Ito once compared evacuation shelters with government temporary housing: the two embody entirely different spatial logics. Shelters are inconvenient and life is hard; small shelters often suffer from scarce supplies, while large ones, because of media coverage in the early days, tend to receive abundant aid, even periodic surpluses. Yet shelters can still sustain the pre-earthquake village or community; people who know each other can support one another in gymnasiums and other shelter spaces. But in the name of fairness and efficient administration, the transfer from shelters to temporary housing is decided by lottery — original social ties are forcibly severed, people are split apart and settled one by one into "modernist, rule-bound spaces." Ito criticizes this modernist logic, identical to urban studio apartments, as doing nothing to encourage communication among residents.

![Dense temporary prefab units surround Hirata's Home-for-All (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704251439319ntyuHlqpcTLv6rQ.jpg-w600)

![Inside Hirata's Home-for-All, people sitting together around the hearth (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425144133cotvMb9YNPTDegSQ.jpg-w600)

The standard temporary housing promoted by the Japanese government: prefabricated steel-framed units with 30-centimeter eaves. Ito chatted with the people living there, and he notes that the place for conversation is inconvenient — you have to stand on bare gravel, in a narrow passage — such spatial arrangements discourage people from talking to one another. People told him, "If only there were an earthen-floored veranda, we could play chess with people," "under the old veranda we could stack firewood"... Almost every complaint about the current space implicitly longed for the social life inside traditional wooden houses, even if such nostalgia poses fire hazards. "Faced with these people, I really couldn't say something like 'the modern four-square-box building is better,'" Ito said. He is not naively demanding a return to tradition, but the unreasonable part of the temporary resettlement sites is the lack of any place where strangers living there can gather and chat.

This is the origin of the Home-for-All plan: to build a public space for people in the post-disaster temporary resettlement sites.

"Why can't architects design temporary housing? Why aren't architects invited to participate when planning the city's reconstruction? Could the problem lie in architects' own behavior up to that point? I have a sense of crisis that if we don't ask ourselves these questions, architects will still be exactly as they are now even a hundred or two hundred years from now."

![The Soma Home-for-All (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425144532pdtJVQ0kqLenPlob.jpeg-w600)

The first Home-for-All came from a plan called Kumamoto Artpolis: Kumamoto Prefecture aided Miyagi Prefecture in building a public facility, providing timber, funding and other assistance, and selecting a suitable architect. In 《那天之后的建筑》(Architecture After That Day: Toyo Ito's Post-3/11 View of Architecture), Ito discussed the sensitivities behind such aid projects. At the time, many local governments receiving aid would refuse this "gift": the hot potato would compromise the fairness of the administration — a public facility built in one place would be seen as favoritism and breed resentment in other areas — and a public facility requires dedicated staff to manage, which creates yet another headache. But Miyagino Ward in Miyagi later accepted this Home-for-All.

Ito believes the government's understanding of "publicness" differs greatly from architects' ideas about urban structure and publicness. In fact, the government, the public and the residents each hold different versions of spatial imagination. Bureaucracy is only the first step of the wrangling. After the site is chosen, the next question the architect must answer is: what kind of public space do the disaster victims actually want?

In the aforementioned Home-for-All project in Rikuzentakata, Akihisa Hirata was one of the main participants. He still remembers his teacher Toyo Ito's admonition — "The first thing you must do is forget your own design language and personality, and listen carefully to opinions and needs from all sides. Because when you release countless ideas and ultimately fail to reach consensus with the other architects and the local residents, you're back at the starting point."

His first draft for this seaside slope was a fully open dome, but the residents protested, demanding a place where they could sit on the ground, sheltered from wind and rain. Only after nearly a hundred models and schemes did this two-story watchtower, satisfactory to all parties, emerge.

![The Miyatojima Home-for-All (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704251446586MCvcr47Lzoj3FEa.jpeg-w600)

In 2013, a local nonprofit in Iwanuma and an IT company in Tokyo jointly launched an agricultural revival plan; the Home-for-All designed under it became a base for sustainable agriculture. Inspired by traditional Japanese farmhouses, the tall interior ceiling and the relatively low eaves together form a deep, layered structure. Local farmers can sell their produce locally and online, and can also use communication tools like Skype to see friends living far away and let them know they're safe.

In February 2015, the Home-for-All by Klein Dytham architecture in collaboration with Toyo Ito was an indoor playground, providing children aged 0 to 4 in Soma with an indoor play space shielded from radiation, since they could no longer play outdoors. The building's overall structure resembles a parasol held up by trees.

Chinese architect Zhao Yang, selected in 2013 as an architecture protégé in Rolex's Mentor and Protégé Arts Initiative and mentored by Kazuyo Sejima, designed a Home-for-All for local fishermen by the sea in Kesennuma. He still remembers that his initial scheme attended to the Japanese detail of removing one's shoes when entering a house, only to be told by locals that this convention of the modern city was superfluous in a public space where fishermen lived.

![The Kesennuma Home-for-All (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425144318Aqej6KDlVQmyiUTn.jpg-w600)

On Omishima in southern Japan, Toyo Ito folded the Home-for-All into a more ambitious island revival plan, in which students from the "Ito Juku" (Ito Architecture School) also participated. They converted idle houses into Homes-for-All and rebuilt the seaside schoolhouse, abandoned due to severe aging of the population, into comfortable guesthouses. To supplement the island's originally inconvenient transport, the plan also developed new modes of transport, introducing Yamaha's concept electric vehicles "05GEN" and "06GEN", in preparation for tourism.

Ito and his team plan to build a winemaking industry on Omishima's citrus fields, which no one cultivates anymore. Grape planting began in 2014, taking advantage of the climate conditions similar to the Mediterranean and the sloping terrain — also the first challenge to winemaking in the Seto Inland Sea. The first phase of the Omishima wine estate is expected to be completed in 2019. It sounds like yet another architect's version of a rural-revitalization plan, following the Echigo-Tsumari and Setouchi art triennials.

![Children at activities in the Minamisoma Home-for-All (Photo / Home-for-All official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425145301URcN1GlIrMJX3wQ8.jpeg-w600)

As architects such as Toyo Ito, Shigeru Ban and Alejandro Aravena have won prizes for socially engaged architectural practice, one strand of skepticism asks: where is the boundary of architects' social exploration? A leading detractor is Patrick Schumacher, partner at Zaha Hadid Architects: "I would rather see architectural innovation that helps solve and promote urban development and restructuring — that advances the discipline, which is what juries should value... I worry that the judging criteria have shifted toward political correctness, so that unconventional figures like Wolf Prix of Coop Himmelb(l)au or Peter Eisenman can no longer stand out."

Such worries about architects "riding the wave" out of political correctness are not without merit. The questions that cannot be dodged remain these: where is architecture possible? Are there really forbidden zones for architecture? If it is possible, how is it possible?
