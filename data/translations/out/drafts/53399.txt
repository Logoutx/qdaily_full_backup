Service is a poor match for shifting tastes and the health-food trend

In 1994, Zhang Yong opened a Haidilao hotpot restaurant with only four tables in Jianyang, Sichuan. Back then, KFC and McDonald's were just beginning to expand aggressively across China, and every outlet in every city was packed.

With Western fast food booming, one big question was hotly debated across the restaurant industry and the media: why hasn't Chinese cuisine produced a big company like KFC or McDonald's?

The common answer was that Chinese food resists standardization. Yum Brands, which made its money on KFC and Pizza Hut, tried to replicate a KFC within Chinese cuisine, launching "East Dawning" in 2004.

The boxed-meal-style Chinese fast food never took off. The market proved that hotpot — tasty even without a chef — was the easiest business in Chinese cuisine to standardize. [Yum bought Little Sheep in 2011.](http://www.infzm.com/content/59116)

In the end, though, the first Chinese restaurant brand to reach 10 billion yuan in annual revenue was Haidilao, whose average spend per head is nearly 100 yuan — more than double the revenue of the runner-up. On May 17, Haidilao, which had 320 stores (as of Q1 2018), filed its [prospectus](http://www.hkexnews.hk/APP/SEHK/2018/2018051601/Documents/SEHK201805170006_c.pdf) with the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, planning to fund rapid expansion through the listing and add 180–220 stores this year.

### Haidilao's operating performance is still climbing, and second-tier cities contributed half its revenue

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180521003002spXKEagBihw813xN.jpg-w600)

According to the prospectus, Haidilao's total revenue last year was 10.64 billion yuan, with profit of nearly 1.2 billion yuan. Founders Zhang Yong and his wife Shu Ping hold 62.7% of the shares.

Despite exposés of poor kitchen hygiene and questions over whether it was "falling out of favor," Haidilao's growth remained rapid: over the past two years, revenue and profit posted compound annual growth rates of 35.9% and 70.5% respectively.

Last year Haidilao's table turnover was 5 times a day, up from 2014, but same-store sales were essentially flat — meaning the amount spent per table fell. On the other hand, average spend per customer at Haidilao rose from 94.5 yuan to 97.7 yuan. The only explanation is that a higher share of customers are now parties of one or two; it is no longer just a place you go for group gatherings.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018052100295057nW2Umls1gDPEHo.jpg-w600)

It isn't cheap, but second-tier cities have become an important market for Haidilao: it has the most stores there, they generate the largest share of revenue, and table turnover is fastest. Third-tier and below cities are also growing quickly: in 2017, same-store sales growth reached 16.3%, outpacing first-tier cities.

New Haidilao stores generally reach first-month break-even within 1–3 months.

In 2015–2016, most Haidilao restaurants recouped their cash investment within 6–13 months. By comparison, other major Chinese restaurant brands typically need 3–6 months to reach first-month break-even and 15–20 months to recover their cash investment.

Haidilao is not only twice the size of the number-two Chinese restaurant company; on metrics such as annual growth rate, table turnover, and annual revenue per restaurant, it also leaves major competitors in the Chinese restaurant industry like Grandma's Home, Xibei Youmian Cun, Huang Ji Huang, and Xiabuxiabu a clear distance behind.

![By table turnover and same-store growth, Haidilao beats Xiabuxiabu; on margins and restaurant operating margins the two are comparable, though their positioning differs](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180521015856vVfEHSLYGegaOk2j.JPG-w600)

### In hotpot — a standardized business — Haidilao found differentiation through service

Hotpot is the easiest business in Chinese cuisine to standardize: it needs no master chef, and both the process and the product are easy to standardize — whether the soup base, the prepped vegetables, or the dipping sauces. Because other full-service Chinese categories suffer from insufficient product standardization and find going public hard, hotpot is more favored by capital markets.

[Longhui Catering — whose brands include Huige and Little Huige — has recently been reported to be planning a backdoor listing in Hong Kong via Daqing Dairy](http://www.bjnews.com.cn/food/2018/05/09/486289.html); Little Lamb, whose stores are mainly in lower-tier cities, is a company listed on the NEEQ. And Haidilao is not even the first hotpot restaurant company to list in Hong Kong: [Little Sheep in 2008](http://www.caijing.com.cn/2008-06-13/100069276.html) and [Xiabuxiabu in 2014](http://finance.sina.com.cn/roll/20140329/010718650923.shtml) came before.

Haidilao differentiated through service from the very start of the brand. When he opened his hotpot restaurant 24 years ago, knowing nothing about cooking and new to the restaurant business, founder Zhang Yong could only keep customers through warm, attentive service. He believed most diners can't tell the difference between spicy hotpot flavors, and that with location, price, and environment all similar, service is the decisive factor in hotpot dining.

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, the service industry hadn't fully shaken off the surly "state-run" standard of service. Haidilao offered to carry your bags, handed out snacks and drinks to people waiting for a table, and provided manicures and shoe shines — and that's how it grew from Jianyang, Sichuan to Xi'an and Zhengzhou, entering Beijing in 2004.

Such meticulous service was extremely rare even among first-tier city restaurants, and it made consumers spread the word about Haidilao on their own. A viral 2011 post on the Tianya forum — ["The Invincible Haidilao!!!!! Those stories about Haidilao!! Anyone who's eaten there, come dish"](http://bbs.tianya.cn/post-funinfo-2740630-1.shtml) — is one example.

Haidilao employees have relatively broad latitude: they decide on their own what dishes to give a customer or which extra services to add, without asking the restaurant manager. The prospectus notes that restaurants are evaluated on "customer satisfaction" and "employee effort" rather than store financial metrics, because Zhang Yong believes each store's financial performance depends heavily on site selection.

In the early days there were also plenty of internal promotion opportunities, and many of Haidilao's store managers and senior executives today started at the lowest rung in the restaurants. On top of that, Haidilao built a school in Jianyang, Sichuan to support the education of employees' children — rare in the industry. [Harvard Business Review called Haidilao "a standout in emotional-culture management among Chinese companies"](http://www.hbrchina.org/2016-03-08/3910.html).

Behind the attentive service and good word of mouth is investment in labor. According to the prospectus, Haidilao spent 3.1 billion yuan last year on 50,000 employees (49,000 of them restaurant staff) — equivalent to an average annual salary of 61,200 yuan per person, above Xiabuxiabu's roughly 39,000 yuan. Haidilao's average pay, though, is lower than Quanjude's and Tang Palace's; in those two, chefs likely pull the average up.

### Later expansion relied on the supply chain

When people praise Haidilao, what they mention most is its service — but founder Zhang Yong says: ["Our real strength is actually the supply chain."](http://www.hbrchina.org/2016-03-08/3910.html) What is listing this time is Haidilao's restaurant business; without the independent operation of six affiliated companies (especially the hotpot-base and ingredient supply chain), it might have struggled to reach its current scale.

Haidilao's affiliates touch nearly every link upstream in the restaurant business — Yihai International, which processes hotpot bases and dipping sauces (already listed in Hong Kong); Shuhai, which covers the restaurant supply chain: food warehousing, logistics, and sales; Shuyun Dongfang, which manages restaurant interior fit-out projects; and Honghuotai, which handles restaurant cloud technology, plus a separate company doing human-resources consulting. The reason for splitting the businesses up may be that this strategy spreads risk in capital operations.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180521002744OwDJ0BjkQU5dMebA.jpg-w600)

Early on, Haidilao was already optimizing processes to prepare for later scale. The prospectus puts it this way: "The founders recognized a lack of specialized service providers for large restaurant chains in China's restaurant industry — especially in ingredient processing, warehousing and logistics, store construction, and labor."

Before 2004, Haidilao, like most other hotpot restaurants, bought ingredients from local wet markets, and staff mixed the soup base themselves every day. Finding that dish quality couldn't be guaranteed, in 2005 Haidilao set up four logistics centers nationwide, and its soup-base factory was established in Chengdu.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180521014900lgLBRbrU948GunMh.png-w600)

Yihai International, the hotpot-base operator, [listed in Hong Kong in 2016](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/25154.html). [Last year its revenue reached 1.64 billion yuan](http://www.yihchina.com/investor/pdf/c_1579.pdf), with net profit of 260 million yuan — smaller than Haidilao, but with higher net and gross margins. From 2013 to 2017, related-party transactions with Haidilao consistently accounted for about 55% of its revenue. Beyond that, its soup bases are sold directly to consumers at supermarkets like Walmart and Carrefour. Recently Yihai has also moved into food processing — self-heating mini hotpots (instant hotpots).

Shuhai began operating independently of Haidilao in 2007. Its biggest "customer" is still Haidilao (which bought 2.6 billion yuan worth from it last year), but [revenue from outside Haidilao has already reached 1 billion yuan](https://xw.qq.com/cmsid/20180323A07N1E00). It has 11 logistics center bases, 7 food-processing centers, vegetable-growing bases, and a lamb-processing plant, among others. [Shuhai supplies prepped vegetables and semi-finished products](http://www.bbtnews.com.cn/2018/0208/229726.shtml) to 7-Eleven convenience stores in Beijing; it also provides supply-chain services to restaurants like Da Ka Sha Jie, Mala Youhuo, and Jindingxuan, and is developing new dishes.

![Vegetable farm. Photo/Haidilao official website](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180521015330EawkYBPnfZ7iTdJo.jpg-w600)

Once a restaurant company reaches a certain scale, what it's really competing on is the supply chain — that is, procuring, processing, and delivering raw ingredients. Nearly every link in the supply chain touches food safety, and ingredient quality also determines whether the food that comes out of the kitchen stays consistent; done well, it can also optimize costs — transactions between companies within the system come at prices cheaper than dealing with outside firms.

After Haidilao, other large restaurant companies also began moving into the supply chain and (semi-finished) food processing. One example is [Xinliangji](http://www.xinliangjishipin.com/), incubated by Xin La Dao Catering, which is currently developing standardized dishes like crayfish and suan cai yu (pickled-cabbage fish), serving companies like Bifengtang and South Beauty. Xiabuxiabu and Xibei Youmian Cun [are also building out their supply chains](http://www.cemat-asia.com/CN/Co/?CID=9&AID=319). O2O companies like Ele.me and Hema are already providing, or planning to provide, services for restaurant supply chains.

### To accelerate expansion, Haidilao — which once said it wouldn't go public — has had to go public after all

Like most companies that choose to list, Haidilao needs capital to expand.

According to the prospectus, it plans to use 60% of the raised funds for opening stores; 20% to develop new technologies to improve restaurant operating efficiency (including site selection, kitchen operations, and inventory supply management); and 15% to repay loans.

Haidilao plans to add 180–220 stores in 2018. Capital investment for each new domestic store is about 8–10 million yuan. Adding in store renovations and technology investment, Haidilao's capital expenditure in 2018 is expected to reach 2.5 billion yuan. But cash and borrowing alone can hardly cover that — at the end of Q1, Haidilao had 518 million yuan on its books and just over 23 million yuan in unused bank credit lines, leaving a huge funding gap.

It needs to open stores faster to grab market share and prime locations, especially in first- and second-tier cities where restaurant competition is fierce.

Haidilao once tried to grow faster through food delivery, launching door-to-door hotpot before Ele.me and Meituan Waimai even existed.

Haidilao's food delivery revenue in 2017 was 210 million yuan — very small within its 10-billion-yuan revenue base. And its delivery growth was not only more than 10 percentage points below the delivery industry as a whole, it also lagged well behind the company's own growth rate (30.8% vs 35.6%).

Haidilao can hardly rely on delivery for growth; to keep growing, it still has to open more stores.

### Rapid expansion inevitably brings management problems

With capital behind it, Haidilao will open stores at an unprecedented pace in 2018. Adding 180–220 stores in a year means growing the existing base of 320 by 60–70% — nearly doubling. The prospectus shows no particular geographic emphasis: first- and second-tier cities, third-tier and below, and overseas markets are all mentioned.

Rapid expansion can bring many problems. The hygiene problems [exposed by the Legal Evening News last August](http://www.kanfanews.com/web/news/index/607516/1%EF%BC%8C) — kitchen staff using tableware to unclog drains, and brooms, dustpans, and rags washed together with dishes — actually reflected management problems amid last year's rapid store growth.

Although Haidilao responded quickly and apologized, immediately closed stores for rectification, and added kitchen cameras at its Beijing stores while inviting consumers to supervise, it's hard to say similar food-safety problems won't recur. Each Haidilao store is staffed with 132 restaurant employees; counting toward the goal of 180 new stores, it needs to add at least 23,700 people, and ensuring that such a large workforce all complies with food-safety laws and regulations will be far harder.

The expanding Haidilao also faces the fading of the "emotional-culture management" it built in the past. Zhang Yong is aware of this.

[He said](http://www.hbrchina.org/2016-03-08/3910.html): "We used to have lots of incentives, like model-worker awards, but we're cutting some of them now. Because when you're small, you can afford to give extra care to employees with long hours and low educational attainment."

### Harder to control and predict still is the change in consumers' tastes

Inner Mongolian-style hotpot represented by Little Sheep once swept China. Although the brand's decline had much to do with its own management, the flavor it popularized has indeed fallen out of favor.

Taste trends are fickle, and when change comes, even the most successful companies struggle to resist it. Coca-Cola, Pepsi, and McDonald's, which run countless ads every year, have all had to reinvent and repackage themselves amid the craze for healthier options.

In Chinese cuisine, where tastes are highly fragmented, trends are even harder to pin down. Even Haidilao, the largest player, holds only 0.3% of the entire Chinese restaurant industry.

Sichuan-style hotpot remains the biggest hotpot category, but more diversified flavors are rising. One example is [Chaoshan beef hotpot](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/35043.html), which we have covered before: with its demand for fresh, healthy ingredients, it became a breakout hit from late 2015 into 2016.

Although Chaoshan beef hotpot has passed its peak, the "fresh" flavor it represents has become the new taste Chinese consumers favor.

According to surveys by Meituan and the [China Cuisine Association](http://www.ccas.com.cn/Article/HTML/108593.html), in 2017 consumers preferred ["sweet-fresh"](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/BUZjg4aUXnUgsbDopNzzdQ) and ["savory-fresh"](http://www.ccas.com.cn/Article/HTML/108593.html) flavors, and spicy-numbing was knocked off the top of the favorite list for the first time — an unwelcome signal for Haidilao, which is built on Sichuan-style hotpot.

Meanwhile, in Beijing and Shanghai, stores specializing in "fresh"-forward, lighter hotpots — pork-tripe-and-chicken, coconut-chicken, and Japanese sukiyaki — are increasingly available. Some chains, also company-operated and targeting the mid-to-high end (spend per head of 100–200 yuan), such as Laowang, Yeting Coconut Chicken, and Yezi Buyu (part of the Shanjiantang Group), are expanding. These hotpot brands are still small and no immediate threat to Haidilao, but they may siphon off some customers who would otherwise pick Haidilao.

Competition in first-tier city restaurants is becoming fiercer than ever, and the data in Haidilao's prospectus illustrate the point. Although Haidilao's average daily revenue per store in first-tier cities is higher than in second-tier cities (152,100 yuan vs 147,100 yuan), same-store revenue — for "older" stores open more than 300 days — in first-tier cities (154,400 yuan) is lower than in second-tier cities (156,200 yuan). This suggests Haidilao's stores can stand their ground in second-tier cities as time passes, with relatively strong influence and competitiveness, while in first-tier cities that effect is much less evident.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180521014808hF3REjkxuBMoZVpm.jpg-w600)

With consumer tastes diversifying, and on a "don't put all your eggs in one basket" strategy, quite a few mid- and large-size restaurant companies are trying multi-brand portfolios. Grandma's Home has launched 16 brands including Lu Yu, Guo Xiao Er, and UNCLE 5; [Xiabuxiabu launched Coucou in 2016](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/23093.html) — a Taiwanese-style hotpot chain with average spend of around 150 yuan per head. Coucou sets up a milk-tea stall at the store entrance, and from its service slogan "don't disturb the customer" you can tell who it's benchmarked against.

Haidilao's multi-brand attempts all sit outside its main corporate entity. Through its (indirect) affiliate Youdingyou, it runs maocai operations. Youdingyou's actual controller, Zhang Shuoyi, is Zhang Yong's younger brother; the company transacts directly with Shuhai, Yihai, and Shuyun Dongfang, and Haidilao has also advanced payments on its behalf.

But Youdingyou's [results have consistently been poor](http://www.neeq.com.cn/disclosure/2018/2018-04-19/1524144555_206587.pdf). It lost more than 3 million yuan in both 2015 and 2016. Although it returned to profit in 2017, net profit was a mere 220,000 yuan on revenue exceeding 109 million yuan. U Ding Maocai closed 7 stores in its main market of Beijing, leaving only 2 stores in Shanghai.

Haidilao then made a series of investments and acquisitions. In 2017, Youdingyou invested in the fast-food brand [Haidao Xiafan](http://www.sohu.com/a/139757313_417694) and the malatang brand [Xu Xiaoshu](http://www.canyin88.com/zixun/2017/12/06/56212.html) (the amounts were not disclosed). It also [acquired a 25% stake in Shanghai Yuangu Catering Management Co.](http://cj.sina.com.cn/article/detail/1834507152/357610) (whose brands include Cai Ni Yunnan Cuisine, Yunnan Yuncaini, Dianyouji, and Cang'er Xiaozhen) for nearly 10 million yuan.

In its post-listing development plans, Haidilao ultimately chose to open new stores — replicating an already successful model more densely across more cities. And that means it will bear more risk when tastes change.
