The Paradox of “Internet Celebrity” Wang Cuncun: “I Do Need an Audience… But I’m Not Performing”
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
He grew a bathtub full of rice, but “hopes it won’t be rice in its next life.” He’s an internet celebrity, but doesn’t much care for the label.
@@QD_BODY@@
He grew a bathtub full of rice, but “hopes it won’t be rice in its next life.” He’s an internet celebrity, but doesn’t much appreciate the identity.

On July 23, 2016, Wang Cuncun posted a four-minute selfie [video](http://t.cn/RtyrgFO?m=4000552574929066&u=1895761874) on his Weibo account, titled “This video is boring, not funny, and I’m posting it only to prove I tried…” In the video, wearing the same floral T-shirt Ge You wore in *I Love My Family* (《我爱我家》), he kept licking a lollipop in his hand large enough to cover his mouth and chin, which he mocked as “a lollipop you can never finish licking in a lifetime,” and in between sips drank from a goblet as big as a fish tank, calling it “the nobleman’s goblet.”

In editing, the licking was sped up and set to upbeat pop music. The clock behind him showed he licked from 11:40 p.m. to 2:33 a.m., but in the end he couldn’t finish the candy. His tongue was clearly numb, but he still mumbled for more than a minute, ending with “I’m Cuncun, I hope you’re happy,” looking both over-the-top and ridiculous.

At the time Wang Cuncun was 24, having worked at a Beijing advertising agency for more than two years, earning three to four thousand yuan a month and sharing a rental in Tiantongyuan with roommates. He also ran a Weibo account called “My Name Is Wang Cuncun” as a video blogger, and because he worked during the day, he often stayed up late after getting home at night to record videos—what he called “revenge bedtime procrastination.”

The lollipop-licking video was arguably his first “viral hit,” ultimately getting 15 million views and pushing Wang Cuncun’s follower count from 150,000 to 2 million in one month. The post now has 46,000 comments; beneath the flood of “hahas,” one person wrote, “You’re boring enough, but then I thought about how boring I am too, watching you lick a piece of candy for more than four minutes.”

More than two years later, this March, he spoke about the lollipop episode on the lecture platform [“Yi Xi”](https://www.yixi.tv/speech/769), calling it “classical boredom.” The talk was a kind of retrospective on his Weibo output over the previous four years; other “classical” works included applying more than 200 layers of screen protector to a phone, counting the 16,250 grains of rice in a bowl, and the 289.2 seeds on a strawberry. “In the parlance of our field, it’s doing nothing after a full meal,” he said of his “classical” period. “The defining feature is that it wastes time and means absolutely nothing.”

Besides “classical boredom,” he said he had passed through stages of “realist boredom,” “magical realist boredom,” and “romantic boredom,” which sounded like a personal art history. As he recounted these episodes and tried to sum them up, the audience kept erupting in laughter. On stage, he stood with his hands clasped behind his back, a little nervous, occasionally looking down at the floor, his voice and expression barely changing, and asked the people around him, “What’s so funny about this?”

Still, his “boredom” was indeed evolving.

Some of it came from minute observation. He once pried out the 90 seeds of a pomegranate, picked them up with tweezers as if they were gems, measured each one, examined its “cut,” and arranged them on a table in six rows and fifteen columns at identical intervals, then photographed them and uploaded the picture to Weibo. He used this to hold a “Pomegranate Seed Beauty Pageant,” inviting netizens to vote in the comments for the “most beautiful pomegranate seed.” By his account, he sifted through and tallied all 537 comments under the post one by one to crown a winner, even though what set that seed apart was so slight it would have been lost if swallowed whole.

Another strand of “realism” looked more like clever ideas plucked from daily life—dismissed as “boring,” perhaps, because they had nothing to do with real needs. For instance, he designed a storage and warehousing system at home, vacuum-sealing the unfinished lollipop, the Ge You–style T-shirt, leftover dried fruit, and an old phone, labeling each with a barcode, and scanning them into a computer “inventory-management system” the way supermarkets do. Or, after receiving an ad for a villa with a swimming pool and envying it while feeling wealth was impossibly far away, he smashed the 0.7-meter-wide, 1.7-meter-long bathtub in his rental, tiled it himself, installed spotlights, snorkeled in it for photos, and named the pool “Maldives.”

The talk did indeed make Wang Cuncun famous a second time for being “boring.” By then he had 3.48 million Weibo followers, but the few thousand reposts were nothing compared with his earlier viral hits. The comments were no longer mostly “hahahaha”; most praised his line about “getting as close as possible to the life you want within limited conditions,” and some thought he had “turned boredom into interesting art.”

We met Wang Cuncun a week after the talk went online. He was dressed in black clothes and a black hat, smiled little, spoke in a calm, low voice that was sometimes drowned out by the café noise. That week he was in touch with the media almost every day. He thought it was “pretty cool that someone wants to document what I do,” and mentioned that media coverage might help his parents understand him a bit better: “My dad sometimes worries I’m doing pyramid selling.” But he was wary of journalists who wanted to make him a representative of some group or slap a label on him: “I’m just a 27-year-old. How big do you want to blow me up—how big *can* I be blown up?”

He didn’t seem to enjoy his past achievements or the easy charm that had animated the live audience; instead, he was caught in contradictions. Several times he said the people who really deserved to go viral were the other speakers at Yi Xi, “because they’re actually contributing to society,” and that “maybe deep down I don’t really respect what I do.” “I don’t want kids to see internet celebrities making money and want to become internet celebrities, or to look down on scientists because scientists are poor,” but at the same time, “I really love doing all these messy things I’m doing now,” and “even if I had the chance to become a scientist, I’d still want to be who I am now… maybe a bit improper, a bit restless.”

As an internet celebrity, he didn’t seem to like “internet celebrities” very much.

Wang Cuncun is not, of course, his real name; he wouldn’t reveal it. Like the bathtub, the pomegranate seeds, the strawberry seeds, and the lollipop, “Wang Cuncun” is a sign, a collection of “things that are interesting because they’re boring,” and a focal point refracting certain internet moods.

![After the “classical boredom” phase, Wang Cuncun mapped out a direction for his content: “tech, traditional handicraft.”](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190328105526Pog2xZTNYVcGzkX6.jpg-w600)

When Wang Cuncun registered his Weibo account in February 2015, he intended to turn it into a marketing account; before that, he rarely used Weibo. At the time he had dropped out of a graduate program at a business school in France, where he had already spent four years including his undergraduate studies. The immediate reasons were his father’s building-materials business running into trouble and the family possibly being unable to afford the tuition if he continued. The coursework also exhausted him, because every time he returned to China he saw the internet rapidly changing people’s lives—hailing rides, payments, reading—while the school’s curriculum barely touched on any of it.

At the end of 2014, he went home to Chongqing for winter break and wanted to start a business with four or five high-school classmates. The idea was to build a news app; he believed there was a market for content curation when people’s time was limited and the amount they could read kept growing. To build a user base for the still-unformed product, he registered on Weibo, fixated on traffic: “Get followers first, talk later.”

The follower-growth process was unremarkable; Wang Cuncun called it “account construction.” First, he spent ten days brainstorming “basic content” he thought would attract fans: furniture-design recommendations, poems that were more like gags, and the emotional journey of raising a Samoyed named Nuomi. The second step was piggybacking on trending posts by influencer bloggers to capture traffic—he spent days refreshing the hot posts of then-popular accounts like “Huiyi Zhuan Yong Xiaomajia” and “Tiancai Xiaoxiongmao,” then wrote gags in their vein. Finally, he spent 70 yuan on Taobao to buy 15,000 zombie followers, and within a month he had the more than 35,000 followers he wanted.

During this process, the startup plan with his friends gradually stopped being discussed because schedules shifted and the group was loosely organized, but the Weibo account “My Name Is Wang Cuncun” survived. In July 2015, Wang Cuncun left Chongqing and began the “Beijing drift” life, writing copy and making plans at an advertising agency. The 1,000-yuan rent he split with roommates each month ate up one-third of his income; what was left was just enough to eat. “When buying instant noodles I had to check whether it was 2.3 or 2.4 yuan, afraid I’d grab the wrong one.”

Running Weibo became an after-work hobby; doing “a little something” online at night made life less dull, and his tight finances were probably part of the reason. The 35,000-plus followers had stalled for half a year with no growth; Wang Cuncun chalked it up to “a product-positioning problem” and decided he needed to find another field to attract new fans.

The positioning he landed on was “boredom,” because he judged this “field” to be empty on Weibo. “Anyway, no one’s doing it, so who cares how big the market is—I’ll claim the spot first.” “Boredom is the state most people live in now, because everyone has too many things they want to do but are too lazy to do, or can’t do. I’m doing those things for them.” Once that was clear, he decided to label himself “the most boring person” on Weibo and abandon all his previous content.

The “My War with Netizens” series was born, marking the start of his “classical boredom.” On April 8, 2016, Wang Cuncun posted a video in which he spent six hours counting the grains of rice in a bowl. The provocation was a challenge from a netizen—he had earlier posted a poem on Weibo with the line “a bowl of rice has more than 18,000 grains,” a figure he had found online. Someone commented, “How do you know it’s that many?” Wang Cuncun replied, “I won’t admit defeat,” and actually counted the rice, filmed it, and set it to war-scene music from a blockbuster. This was essentially adding an interactive mode to “boring things” to attract traffic and followers.

Before that, he had also posted photos of stars woven from drinking straws and other handicrafts. One comment said, “Give you a few steel bars and you’d weave a Bird’s Nest, right?” He went to an architecture student friend, researched the structure of the “Bird’s Nest” stadium, and built a model out of wire, which he photographed and uploaded.

![Screenshot of the rice-counting [video](http://t.cn/Rq5eRP6?m=3962081625757546&u=1895761874).](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190328110238EviVmzjAtZWbrFSG.png-w600)

![The handmade “Bird’s Nest” model from the “My War with Netizens” series.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190328110646Wp8KkfHL5DbvaunI.JPG-w600)

Wang Cuncun was quite confident in his “internet sense” at the time; he felt the “boredom” model was like the only road from his house to the village entrance when he was a kid—“if you keep walking, you’ll definitely get there.” “Focus on boredom,” a clear positioning, keep filling it with content, and one day it’ll blow up, “though I don’t know which day.”

That day really did come. On the night of July 23, 2016, after posting the lollipop video he had recorded the day before, he scrolled through Weibo as usual to see if any big influencers had reposted him. The speed of circulation was a little beyond his expectations: the video went up close to midnight; within half an hour it had five or six thousand reposts, and soon passed ten thousand. Recalling his emotions that night, he said, “Of course it felt great, but also baffling—I didn’t know why something like this blew up,” and he went to sleep on time, “because I had work the next day.” Looking back, besides having foreseen that “this day would come,” the reason he stayed calm was that the content at the time cost almost nothing and involved little of his own creativity or aesthetics, “so there was no extra pressure.”

He vaguely recalled that he became a bit swollen-headed then, “a little less humble about new knowledge,” and “felt great” about the sudden attention. Knowing that one of his Weibo posts could get lots of reposts and comments, he once posted only a comma and still got several thousand reposts. But he quickly mocked himself to me: “Ah, how inflated can you get living in Tiantongyuan? Could I really be the king of Tiantongyuan?”

The high point still brought Wang Cuncun a steady stream of advertising income—enough not only to “get by,” but also to send money to his mother. In October 2016, he was invited to the “Weibo V Influence Summit” hosted by Sina Weibo, where he gave a talk titled *How to Make an Account Go Viral* (《如何把一个号做火》) and was named one of the “Top Ten Influential Humor Bloggers.” The same award went to accounts such as “Guan’ai Zhizhang Ertong Chengzhang” and “Leng Xiaohua Jingxuan,” whose Weibo verifications mostly read “comedy blogger.” In that talk, he said he had already passed through the “early stage” and “growth stage” of running a Weibo account; next should come the “maturity stage” with a stable business model.

![Wang Cuncun at the 2016 Weibo V Influence Summit. Image from Weibo @MyNameIsWangCuncun.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190328111133IW6D3Cl21KkF8gbt.jpg-w600)

Anxiety arrived at this point. Original videos became his relatively fixed posting format; besides the “War” series, he started a food-themed series called *Cuncun’s Food Diary*, but the form and content were simple, more like maintaining the “boring” persona he had established. This maintenance mode—no rising costs, little creativity, day after day with no change—began to actually bore him; moreover, from his marketing perspective, there was always an aesthetic cycle regardless of content quality. If he kept running it like this, after “maturity” would come “decline.”

His salary at the advertising agency didn’t improve much; over two years it rose by 1,000 yuan. When doing marketing planning, he had suggested several times that the company invest more in original videos—“after all, the video boom has arrived”—but never got his way. In April 2017, Wang Cuncun quit the company, and around that time sold the house his parents had left for him back home. From May to October, he posted only four times on Weibo, becoming a “missing person.”

“The road to the village entrance is already finished,” he said. “Then there were many new choices, but no reference points, so I didn’t know how to go.”

![Wang Cuncun’s October 2017 Weibo post.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190328112001aJ1lsUVhrz3tMIK7.png-w600)

Wang Cuncun insists on defining himself as “someone who does creative work”; “different from everyone else” is what he mentions most.

After selling the house back home, he temporarily had a sum of money in hand. He moved out of the 1,200-yuan-a-month rental in Gaobeidian and into a 30-square-meter studio apartment. On his floor, his was the only doorway flanked by two stone lions, one lying face forward and the other on its side; on the door was a Spring Festival couplet only as long as the door handle, and in the center, where a “fu” character usually goes, he had put the character “Cun.” He added these furnishings in early 2018, while he was competing with the neighbor across the hall over “whose home looks more respectable,” and for a while changed his Wi-Fi name to “Big Household” to send a message to the neighbor.

He never spoke directly with the neighbor, but the neighbor later found his Weibo and one day sent him a private message saying he was preparing to leave Beijing. Wang Cuncun ran to a flower shop and bought a sunflower, stuck it in the neighbor’s door, and that day changed his Wi-Fi name to “Plant Some Seeds to Eat on the Road.”

The room was a little messy. On the bookcase sat the trophy from his “humor blogger” days, surrounded by vacuum-packed objects from the “warehousing system” he used for storage. One item was a palm-sized teddy bear and a quilt the same size—when the neighbor moved away, he gave Wang Cuncun a cotton boll in return; Wang Cuncun picked the cotton, carded it into a small quilt, and kept it as a memento.

Inside the vacuum bag were also three longan he hadn’t finished eating. When asked, “Why keep these?” Wang Cuncun looked a little annoyed. “To eat later,” he said. “Can’t dried longan be eaten?” He scratched his head and recalled that his girlfriend often asked why he kept photos with no people in them on his phone. “They’re not even good-looking.” “I just think, this is a moment of my time, friend. Why does it need to look good?”

The “Maldives swimming pool” in the bathroom was already unrecognizable; only the blue tiles at the edge were visible. The rest of the “pool” was filled with soil, and in November 2018 he planted rice there; it was now taller than a person. Although about half looked yellow and withered, it still gave off the smell of plants. “The rice is dying. I’m anxious,” Wang Cuncun said beside it. “When it was growing well, I really liked staying next to it; it felt like nature.” Above the rice was a row of lamps mimicking sunlight; around the bathtub were switches and wires connected to instruments measuring carbon-dioxide concentration, soil pH, and other data. The showerhead, used for bathing, hung outside the bathtub, one step away from the rice.

![The rice growing in the bathtub.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190328112714MiJyW1ZvQbuXTaB8.jpg-w600)

When he started growing rice, his words on Weibo sounded as if he were still waging “war” with netizens: a netizen had challenged him, “If you’ve got the skills, grow your own rice,” so he decided to do it. Actually, Wang Cuncun’s original idea was more complicated: to document the entire process of “transgenic rice”—altering gene segments so the rice seedlings looked different in shape, length, and width from the start, then actually making a bowl of transgenic rice, “putting it out and asking myself whether I dare to eat it.” He felt this was a very artistic question, but also realized it was a sensitive topic. Out of caution, he grew ordinary rice at home, uploaded photos of himself raising seedlings and tilling the field, and told netizens that he woke up on time to turn on the lights for the rice, enjoying a farmer’s schedule and joys.

It was clear that Wang Cuncun’s creativity and cleverness weren’t entirely tied to his online persona; all kinds of ideas kept popping up. Walking down the street, he pointed at the sidewalk and said cars often parked there, blocking the way. “That’s not right,” he said. “I keep thinking that one day I’ll hire workers at night to seal the car in with cement, then call the neighborhood committee and tell them there’s an illegal structure here.” He also recalled a time when cotton cloud decorations were popular indoors; his first thought was, “What’s fun about that? It would be cool to build a cloud in a room that can actually rain.”

In December 2017, after nearly half a year of silence, Wang Cuncun posted a [video](http://t.cn/RY02PQe?m=4180575541277789&u=1895761874) on Weibo titled “How Many Balloons Would It Take to Lift a Pig?” The filming and editing were noticeably more polished. This also grew out of a curiosity he’d had even earlier. During his “disappearance,” he roughly budgeted, hired an event-planning company on a mountain in Chongqing to round up extras, ordered helium, bought a pig, and spent four and a half hours inflating more than 10,000 colorful helium balloons.

Recalling the making of the video, he could remember the details of calculating the helium and balloon quantities, the healed feeling he had watching the balloons after filming ended, and the question he asked himself when he had long been curious and eager to realize the scene: “Can this be done? It can. Do it!”

The pig ultimately escaped and couldn’t be successfully lifted, but the video became Wang Cuncun’s “comeback work” at the time, the kind of “romantic boredom” he loved. From here on, the videos he posted looked more like small science inventions or handicraft tutorials in traditional techniques—though in his own words, they were sometimes “not very serious” or “boring”: for example, using the principle of [maglev](http://t.cn/RBUh3nr?m=4249417316294014&u=1895761874) to make instant noodles, a car ornament, and a desk lamp float; growing [water spinach](http://t.cn/EbU4a2m?m=4321890652369802&u=1895761874) at home and hand-forging a wok; and using a Tesla coil that generates a high-voltage electric field to light a [fluorescent lamp](http://t.cn/ReNlu3d?m=4267543655468965&u=1895761874) from a distance. He learned the techniques on the fly from acquaintances and the internet, which made video releases much slower than before.

He considered these things “cool,” and you could tell he had reached a kind of self-consistency at this point. He had also used “cool” to describe the American documentary *Curiosity*—in one episode, the production team bought a Boeing plane and crashed it into the desert to study airplane crashes, filming the whole thing. “I hope my future kids can watch things like this that make them curious,” he said several times, thinking of his future children. “I also hope the videos I make can be shown to him or her… I wouldn’t want to show the lollipop kind of video.”

It’s hard to say what Wang Cuncun would be like without the internet and social media. From hustling for traffic to make a living to trying to find value in it, he explored his interests and personality in step with his rise to fame, and never stopped laying them out on the internet. As he summarized when talking about “how to make an account go viral,” “You need to keep strengthening your persona [on Weibo]… but these personas aren’t made up; they’re all found within my own personality.”

“I probably really do need an audience,” he said. Although a life without the internet was impossible to imagine, after thinking for several tens of seconds he still said, “But I think I’ve always been this kind of person… I’m definitely not performing. Why would I?”

The contradictions continued in his life. The day after we met, he sent several WeChat messages in a row. He mentioned that making tech videos now is expensive, the pressure of real life is heavy, and because it doesn’t make money he doesn’t get recognition. “I don’t know why, but not being able to make money is almost the same as being useless.” “I’ve been struggling lately. I think I might also do some traffic-grabbing stuff this year… The right way to open this era may be like Mimeng: don’t worry about whether the content is right or wrong, just grab the traffic first, and then whatever you say is right.”

He is tired of repetition; every time he releases an idea, he feels “the thing is already over, I need to find something new.” He had said several times that he wanted to make videos with “aesthetic” value, to care about content and about his future children seeing good things—“it’s human instinct”; but he also thought this was “like a mantis trying to stop a carriage, blocking the big vehicle of traffic, which devours everything you’re interested in.”

He was preparing to hold a funeral for his dead rice, and showed me the tombstone he had made; the epitaph read “A yield of ten thousand jin per mu.” “I hope it won’t be rice in its next life,” he said. “Rice is all about yield. People treat you as food; looking good is useless.” He thought it would be better to be a tree in a forest, “not always being judged and compared on the meaning of your existence. Being rice like this is a bit tragic.”
