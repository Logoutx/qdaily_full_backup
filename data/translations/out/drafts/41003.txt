A taste for design flair and the backing of a global conglomerate's supply chain — you can't have one without the other.

Two months before its official opening, the W Shanghai — located on the North Bund in Hongkou District — opened to the media for a preview, showing off its still-unfinished public spaces and two guest rooms.

Designed by G.A Design, the studio behind the W Taipei, the W Shanghai adopts an "East-meets-West" design language. The lobby walls are painted with cheongsam-clad women; neon signs bearing the names of Shanghai streets hang in the atrium; and even the bottom of the terrace pool overlooking the Huangpu River is dotted with peony patterns.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170519121655tKeb956yISWfoDz0.jpg-w600)

Inside the rooms, designers deployed large swaths of highly saturated color — vivid red, orange, and bright blue acrylic panels laid across the walls or used as room dividers. Each bed holds a silver "xiaolongbao" dumpling pillow and a pair of "chopstick" cushions; on the wall by the desk hang two Sonos speakers. Eighty-five percent of the rooms have a floor-to-ceiling window looking out over the Huangpu River.

Christian Humbert, the newly appointed general manager of the W Shanghai, wove through the crowd, asking visitors from time to time: "Can you tell me how you felt after the tour?"

"Very W." That was the answer he got most often.

W is easy to spot. It has opened 50 hotels and resorts in some 40 of the world's liveliest cities and islands, and most Ws sit in the busiest part of town — a blue sail-shaped tower at the port of Barcelona, a neon-glowing curved building at the junction of Chinatown, Leicester Square, and Soho in London, and in New York, W's home turf, you can still find early work by designers Philippe Starck and David Rockwell.

![The W Barcelona, Spain.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170519122038sDLmfy0Q57anJ3tN.jpg-w600)

![The W London, Leicester Square](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170519122438V9A2wiDdMFP8QXcf.jpg-w600)

Search #whotel on Instagram, and among the more than 380,000 photo results you'll regularly find the bold white W letter, athletic young people in sportswear, beaches, and cocktails at parties.

At many W hotels, once evening comes, girls in tight skirts and high heels queue up at the door waiting to get in, and puzzled tourists assume it must be some famous club — perhaps the best footnote to W's glitzy, high-profile hedonism.

Born in New York City, the first W hotel was opened in 1998 by Barry Sternlicht, founder of the Starwood real estate investment firm and the Starwood Hotels & Resorts group. The origin of the W name is a matter of much debate. In [an oral history of boutique hotels published this year by travel site Skift](https://skift.com/history-of-boutique-hotels/), a former W Hotels global development director who had been involved in the early preparations for the first W hotel offers this account:

"It was a really fun time. We didn't know what would happen next or what we were doing ourselves. I don't know whether people outside know this story, but there really are many versions explaining why W is called W. Some say Warm, Witty, Whimsical or words like that, others say Barry's wife came up with the name. But as I remember being there at the time, one reason for calling it W was that if the project failed, we could always turn it back into 'Westin.'"

W Hotels was the first brand Barry Sternlicht built with his own hands. This shrewd Harvard Business School-trained real estate man had lived through the U.S. subprime crisis of the late 1980s and early 1990s, and lost his job over it. In 1991, he founded his own investment firm, Starwood Capital Group, buying up at bargain prices the family apartment buildings the government was selling off amid a fiscal crisis. These successful real estate investments laid the foundation for Sternlicht's later creation of the Starwood Hotels & Resorts group.

In 1995, Sternlicht consolidated the company's hotel assets to form Starwood Hotels & Resorts, and that same year, together with Goldman Sachs, bought the Westin hotel chain for 570 million. Before W opened, Sternlicht went on to acquire ITT Sheraton for 4.3 billion US dollars, gaining three hotel brands: Sheraton, Four Points by Sheraton, and The Luxury Collection.

Frits van Paasschen, former CEO of Starwood Hotels & Resorts, recalled in one interview that when Barry Sternlicht got hold of the 1920s-era building at 541 Lexington Avenue in Manhattan, he wanted to create "a hotel in a great location that doesn't offer traditional luxury service, a light-luxury property that is both business-oriented and geared toward leisure travel."

Sternlicht's idea did not come out of nowhere. Although "boutique hotel" was still a completely unfamiliar term to Americans at the time, change was quietly underway.

Back then, Bill Kimpton — known as the father of the "boutique hotel" — had already opened America's first boutique hotel, the Kimpton Hotel, in San Francisco back in the 1970s, and gradually expanded along the West Coast. By acquiring smaller old buildings in downtown areas, renovating them, and partnering with well-known local chefs to open restaurants inside the hotels, he offered travelers "the intimate, attentive service of a European hotel."

Another inspiration for Sternlicht was Ian Schrager, who built New York's first "lifestyle" hotel. Schrager was the founder of Studio 54, the nightclub that had taken Manhattan by storm, and he was sentenced to three and a half years in prison for evading huge amounts in taxes. After his release, Schrager and his business partner Steve Rubell decided to apply their nightclub-running experience to running hotels.

On November 1, 1983, the two opened the boutique hotel Morgans in an old building at 38 Madison Avenue. Ian brought in a group of product designers from Europe, rather than conventional hotel designers, to design his hotel, and for the first time introduced the concept of "Lobby Social" — turning the hotel lobby into a disco and nightlife venue. Ian Schrager is therefore regarded as the entrepreneur who created the first "lifestyle" boutique hotel.

In the process of creating W Hotels, Sternlicht also approached Ian Schrager and his partners to discuss working together. In the end, though, Schrager did not agree to stay on the project. What did stay was the bar operator he brought in, the Gerber Group. Today, the Gerber Group runs the W Lounge at W hotels.

In November 1998, the world's first W hotel officially opened in the heart of Manhattan, with a grand celebrity party that was "like the opening of a high-end design store or one of those nights at fashion week," [recalled K.C. Kavanagh, Starwood's former global communications director.](https://skift.com/history-of-boutique-hotels/)

The old, run-down Doral Inn hotel was given a complete makeover. David Rockwell, a designer who had collaborated with Ian Schrager, used wind, water, earth, and fire as the base elements of the hotel's interior decoration, and adopted generous warm tones in the rooms — deep red sofas and seats, light green bedspreads, beige carpets, and so on.

Sternlicht absorbed Ian Schrager's creations and refined them. One example: he gave the W hotel lobby a new identity, the "Living Room." This is now one of W's core cultural traits — the lobby is no longer a place for check-in and check-out, but functions like the sitting room of a home, where all the restaurants and bars gather, a fully equipped social space.

Composed of several signature elements — celebrities, Manhattan, design, and nightclubs — W Hotels gave itself a distinctive label and quickly won over its first devotees.

It turned out that Barry was not only perceptive but fast-moving. When W Hotels appeared before New Yorkers and travelers from around the world in the late 1990s with a refreshing new image, New York still had only traditional large luxury hotels like the Park Hyatt and the Waldorf; Marriott had never thought of hiring Ian Schrager to design the Edition, Hyatt had yet to launch Andaz, and Hilton was still expanding its traditional five-star hotels around the world.

At precisely the right moment, Sternlicht spotted a gap in the market, making W the first boutique hotel to be launched by a major hotel group.

"Barry Sternlicht admits he was always watching what Ian Schrager and André Balazs (founder of the New York boutique hotel Sunset Beach) were doing, and then presenting it in a more comfortable, more democratic way," [said Amar Lavani](https://skift.com/history-of-boutique-hotels/): "If you compare Ian Schrager's Paramount or Royalton hotels, they were 'hard,' made up of nightclubs and lots of metal elements, and I remember the rooms weren't comfortable. W was not. It borrowed more from the style of Pottery Barn (an American furniture brand from California) — more comfortable, more feminine."

"I still remember my first time at the W hotel next to Times Square; it was around 2000," recalled Gray Shealy, who served as W Hotels' design director from 2006 to 2013, in [an interview](https://skift.com/2016/09/21/starwoods-hospitality-legacy-after-the-marriott-merger/): "It was like walking through a portal — on one side the madness and energy of Times Square, on the other the sunken lobby of the W hotel. There was a glass cube filled with water that gave you an immediate sense of pressure release, and you felt a sense of calm opposite to the outside world. It carried you into another world, a world of the hotel."

Unlike Ian Schrager's nightclub hotels, Barry Sternlicht's vision from the very start was to combine the distinctive design of boutique hotels with the standardized, reliable hotel service of business hotels.

In W Hotels' first few years, besides hiring outstanding industrial designers, architects, and interior designers to emphasize W's design identity, Sternlicht also devised a series of signature hotel services: the all-white "Heavenly Bed" developed from the Westin hotel, the Whenever/Whatever service delivered by hotel staff who feel like friends, and — starting in 2004 — the Bliss 49 Spa launched in partnership with New York-based skincare brand Bliss. From then on, the Bliss 49 Spa and Bliss skincare products became another eye-catching hallmark of W hotels.

![Every W hotel has a Bliss Spa.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170519125209W2lrB6FjUKZiNH5c.jpg-w600)

On the other hand, the powerful hotel operations management and resources behind the group ultimately made W a global boutique hotel brand. Before it, no hotel with a similar positioning had managed to do that.

Within just a few years, W expanded to Washington, Seattle, San Francisco, Boston, and Dallas. After opening its first overseas W in Istanbul in 2008, the bold white W logo joined the skylines of more and more cities — Barcelona, Paris, London, Singapore, Taiwan, Guangzhou, and beyond.

In fact, precisely because of the emergence of W Hotels in the late 1990s and, in the same period, the Ace Hotel on the West Coast — two hugely influential boutique hotel brands — the concept of the "boutique hotel" became a key word in reshaping the hotel industry over the past two decades.

In 2016, [according to a report by digital marketing firm Highland Group](http://hotelnewsnow.com/Articles/37795/Theres-room-for-growth-in-lifestyle-boutique-segments), the 100 largest cities in the United States already had 63,000 boutique hotel rooms of varying sizes and standards. Meanwhile, more and more large chains were buying up or gradually building their own boutique hotel portfolios — in addition to Andaz and Edition mentioned above, InterContinental Hotels Group created the boutique brand Hotel Indigo in 2004 and acquired the Kimpton Hotel chain for 43 million US dollars in 2012.

As competitors multiplied and consumers became ever more familiar with the concept of boutique hotels, keeping W fresh became the most important thing.

The first thing W did was to keep reinforcing its "design" identity label. Every time it entered a new city, it required its design studios to localize from a hotel-design perspective. But to keep the brand image and culture consistent, this localization was carried out both from inside and outside the company.

"At W, we have a design team, and it's not the kind of design team responsible for building structures — the way most hotels on the market have today," [Gray Shealy explained](https://skift.com/2016/09/21/starwoods-hospitality-legacy-after-the-marriott-merger/): "They don't just focus on mechanical or engineering coordination; they're also responsible for the interior design and for developing proposals for furniture and the like. We'll have a small group dedicated to researching and solving these problems. This was a first for Starwood — recombining and reallocating existing experience and resources so that our teams can create a special experience together in vastly different environments."

Beyond working with experienced design studios, W also focused on uncovering young designers. In 2012, W partnered with Design Miami/Basel to create the Designers of the Future Award. Each year the award goes to three design studios with outstanding work across various design fields, including interior and product design. In the years after it was established, the award unearthed several rising design stars — among them the young British designer Bethan Laura Wood, whose work has entered Milan's renowned Nilufar Gallery.

Second, thanks to the SPG loyalty program under Starwood Hotels & Resorts, W Hotels still draws a steady stream of guests every year from nearly 21 million SPG members. [Anthony Ingram, now global brand president of W Hotels & Resorts, said in an interview on the TV program Behind the Brand](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P1sNrszsCw0): "These members fill 70 percent of the rooms at W hotels."

"Another point is that, compared with other independent boutique hotels, W's advantage lies in its strong relationships with other large multinational corporations," Ingram added. "McKinsey, PwC, Facebook, Amazon, and so on — every year these companies bring in batch after batch of outstanding young graduates around age 23 or 24, each of whom will have a 10-to-15-year career at these big companies while traveling around the world for business. These customers all love W hotels, and they have also become the main pipeline feeding new guests to W."

This matched the target consumers Sternlicht locked onto for the brand from the very beginning — "young people willing to pay for design." While most luxury hotels were still trying to attract middle-class and affluent mature consumers, W built a certain degree of brand loyalty among young people thanks to its first-mover advantage.

At the same time, W also adopted a series of marketing measures along the way to consolidate this new generation of high-income, highly educated guests. In 2014, Anthony Ingram launched an innovative themed initiative, TURN IT UP FOR CHANGE, aimed at showing solidarity with the LGBT community's fight for equal marriage rights and supporting its equality campaigns. W brought in Hollywood star Jennifer Hudson to throw a grand launch party at the Union Square hotel.

![Image from: [Greenhotelier](http://www.greenhotelier.org/our-themes/community-communication-engagement/starwood-brand-w-hotels-fights-for-lgbt-equality-in-all-50-us-states/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2017051912593873DleHnWmcofsKxd.jpg-w600)

In 2015, W Hotels was also first to partner with Snapchat, launching three filters with a hotel-logo watermark to catch the attention of millennials.

"The young consumer group W focuses on has a mindset that values experience over simply purchasing — this is a change happening around the world. And by 2020, luxury travel and related consumption will be driven almost entirely by people under 45," Ingram said at the W Shanghai preview event.

Today that optimism has been brought to China, because "China will have 500 million middle-class consumers in the future." With the opening of the W Shanghai, the W Suzhou followed in August, and four more — in Hainan, Changsha, Chengdu, and Shenyang — are under construction.

Compared with W's global bloom, its original enlighteners — Ian Schrager and the Morgans hotel — remain names known only to a niche crowd. Starwood turned boutique hotels into light luxury, and in doing so inadvertently rode a wave that has swept the entire business world for nearly 20 years: getting the broad middle class to pay for design after it has been mass-produced.
