### What Agenda-Setters Are Talking About

**[*The Economist*: A $100 Billion Bet — What Masayoshi Son's Vision Fund Means](https://www.economist.com/news/leaders/21741973-succeed-or-fail-masayoshi-son-changing-world-technology-investing-meaning)**

Two years ago, when people talked about the most influential figures in tech, the names were all familiar: Amazon's Jeff Bezos, Alibaba's Jack Ma, Facebook's Mark Zuckerberg.

Masayoshi Son, founder of SoftBank Group, is the newest contender. His Vision Fund is pouring huge sums into the world's most attractive startups — disrupting the industries it invests in and influencing other providers of capital.

It all began in 2016 with the Vision Fund's partnership with Saudi Crown Prince Muhammad bin Salman. Son persuaded him to put up $45 billion as an anchor investor, a commitment that in turn drew in money from Apple, Qualcomm, Foxconn and Sharp. Together with SoftBank's own $28 billion, that brought the Vision Fund's total to $100 billion. So far the fund has already spent $30 billion — by comparison, the entire US venture capital industry raised just $33 billion in 2017.

Masayoshi Son's $100 billion fund will have at least three major effects. First, because its investments are concentrated in frontier technology companies such as Uber, WeWork and Flipkart, with plans to invest in 70 to 100 tech unicorns within five years, this enormous injection of capital will shape the future of several industries. Second, it has forced other investors in the venture capital industry to increase the size of their own funds, channeling investment toward capital-scarce regions such as India, Southeast Asia and Europe. Finally, Son's goal of building "a virtual Silicon Valley inside SoftBank Group" means the unicorn companies it backs will be gathered on a single platform, supporting and growing with one another — a concept that is not new, but unprecedented in scale.

Son's plan has a distinctive appeal. It has rattled the complacency of Silicon Valley's venture capitalists, helped small companies compete with industry giants, and given entrepreneurs a way to cash out beyond being acquired by monopolists. But the risks go beyond the loss of money — young companies that receive investment often pour it into sales and marketing, which will in turn push their rivals to spend more on marketing to fight for customers. Backing by large sums also means these companies can go a long time without facing the scrutiny of public markets.

No one can offer a definitive verdict on the Vision Fund. The only certainty is that its impact will be profound.

### 24-Hour Newsmakers

**[Is War Between Israel and Iran Looming?](http://www.bbc.com/news/world-middle-east-44067804)**

According to Xinhua, late on the night of the 9th and in the early hours of the 10th local time, Israel sent warplanes to bomb dozens of Iranian military targets in Syria,

IDF spokesperson Jonathan Conricus said on the morning of the 10th that late on the 9th and in the early hours of the 10th, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps' Quds Force fired nearly 20 rockets at the Israeli-held Golan Heights from bases inside Syria, and that the Israeli military had retaliated against Iranian targets. The airstrikes concentrated on intelligence sites, weapons depots and logistics centers, and also destroyed several Syrian air defense systems.

Israel said the rockets Iran fired at it did not hit any Israeli targets, causing no Israeli casualties or property damage.

But Israel warned that it would never allow Iran to establish a threat against it from Syrian soil, and that the Syrian government bears responsibility for everything that happens on its territory.

The UK-based Syrian Observatory for Human Rights said on the 10th that the Israeli strikes on Syria killed at least 23 people, including 5 Syrian government soldiers and 18 members of other allied forces.

Russia, Germany and France all expressed concern and called on both Israel and Iran to exercise restraint, while the United States held that Israel has the right to defend itself.

### What People Are Saying

> "Noura is not a criminal but a victim. In other countries, victims of rape and domestic violence receive care to ensure they can recover from the trauma of what happened to them — (rather than being executed)."

On May 10, 19-year-old Sudanese girl Noura Hussein was sentenced to death by a local court in Omdurman for the murder of her husband — even though, from Noura's perspective, her actions were self-defense.

At 16, to escape a marriage her father was forcing on her, Noura fled to live with and study at her aunt's home 250 kilometers away. Three years later, hearing the marriage had been called off, she returned home — only to find it was a trap. The wedding went ahead as planned, and with the help of her husband's family, Noura was raped by her husband.

The day after the wedding, resisting the rape, Noura stabbed her husband, killing him. When she returned home, her family immediately disowned her and turned her over to the police. A year later, on April 29, the court found Noura guilty of murder and asked her husband's relatives to choose between monetary compensation and the death penalty. They chose the latter.

The case has drawn widespread attention on social media. Supporters launched the ["Justice for Noura" campaign](https://twitter.com/hashtag/JusticeForNoura?src=hash). Her legal team has 15 days left to file an appeal.

[According to AFP](https://www.swissinfo.ch/chi/%E8%B5%B4%E7%91%9E%E5%A3%AB%E5%AE%89%E4%B9%90%E6%AD%BB-104%E5%B2%81%E6%BE%B3%E6%B4%B2%E7%A7%91%E5%AD%A6%E5%AE%B6%E7%BB%88%E5%A6%82%E6%84%BF/44109998), on May 10, 104-year-old Australian scientist David Goodall ended his own life with assistance after arriving in Switzerland. The words above were his last before passing.

Goodall was an honorary research fellow at Edith Cowan University. He had no terminal illness, but felt his quality of life had declined markedly in recent years and wanted to seek euthanasia. Because of Australian legal restrictions, he set out from Australia a week earlier with the help of the Swiss organization Exit International, stopping in Bordeaux, France, along the way to meet his family, before arriving in Basel, Switzerland, on the 7th.

On May 9, Goodall cheerfully sang a few lines of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony in front of the media. Wearing a sweater printed with the words "Aging Disgracefully," he said: "A person should be free to choose death, if death comes at the appropriate time."

Exit International founder Nitschke said Goodall received an injection of Nembutal at the Life Cycle clinic in Basel and died at 10:30 GMT on May 10.

### You Might Also Be Interested In

**[Filipinos launched a "sunglasses selfie" movement on Twitter as a form of political protest](https://qz.com/1273517/leaveourshadesalone-anti-marcos-activists-are-posting-selfies-in-protest-of-the-vice-presidential-ballot-recount-in-the-philippines/)**.

In the 2016 general election, incumbent vice president Maria Leonor Robredo defeated the son of the dictator Ferdinand Marcos by a narrow margin of 263,000 votes (out of 40 million ballots cast). Just days later, Marcos filed a 1,200-page petition with the Philippine Supreme Court requesting a recount.

On April 2 this year, the electoral tribunal introduced a new counting rule, declaring that a ballot counts only if the circle next to a candidate's name is shaded at least halfway — even though the counting machines used in the 2016 election needed only a 25% shaded area to read a ballot.

To protest, the Filipino public launched the "#LeaveOurShadesAlone" ("leave our shades alone") campaign on Twitter, in which people posted selfies wearing sunglasses — the sunglasses being a satirical jab at the "more than half shaded" counting rule.
