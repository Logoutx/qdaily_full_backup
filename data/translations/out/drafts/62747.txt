After March 31, The Excelsior stood empty; hoardings went up at the entrance, and passing by left a hollow feeling.

A landmark that had stood on Victoria Harbour for half a century could not withstand the pressure of profit and time. Mandarin Oriental International decided to close The Excelsior, invest HK$5 billion, and rebuild the site into a mixed-use commercial building with a total floor area of more than 60,000 square meters.

On the morning of October 9, 2018, no employee yet knew the news. Just as he had for the past thirty years, Liu Weirong (Samuel), a back-of-house supervisor, walked through The Excelsior’s doors to start work, only to be stopped by security and told there was a meeting. He sensed something was wrong; staff whispered among themselves until management suddenly convened an all-hands meeting that day, saying there would be an important announcement—only to deliver the bad news.

The guest rooms had just been renovated in September, and Wang Huaping (Steve), the concierge, was equally blindsided: “They’d clearly said it wouldn’t be torn down.” Xu Minyi (Alice), a housekeeping supervisor, was 57; she had given thirty years of her life to The Excelsior and had planned to work there until retirement. It took her several months to convince herself to accept reality: “None of us imagined we’d see The Excelsior close with honor.”

A thousand words came down to one: they couldn’t bear to let go.

The public shared the regret. Unlike the five-star Peninsula, which felt out of reach, The Excelsior was only four stars, but its affordable prices and prime location in the heart of a busy district earned it the nickname “celebrities’ canteen.” Many Hongkongers chose it for life’s big moments, forging sentimental ties. Guests were nostalgic, staff were devoted, and regulars from around the world treated the place like home; on return visits they would always ask whether so-and-so was still there, sometimes even bringing small gifts.

Asked what made the hotel special, the three answered in unison: human warmth. For 46 years, veteran staff were everywhere, holding The Excelsior up. Of roughly 500 employees, 29% had worked there for more than ten years, 8% for more than twenty, and the longest tenure reached forty. At the quarterly staff meetings, the line for the long-service awards—ten years and up—always stretched out the door.

![The Excelsior is a modernist building designed by the architect Eric Cumine (file photo)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190408170442pOUQKY9DfEnLhGRZ.jpg-w600)

Leafing through its history, The Excelsior is inseparable from Hong Kong people and from colonial history. The name “Yee Tung,” according to historian Zheng Baoyuan, is thought to mean “Jardine’s East Point.” In 1841, when Hong Kong first opened as a port, the colonial government’s first land auction was won by Jardine Matheson, the oldest British trading house in the Far East; the place was then called East Point. As the firm shifted its focus to business in mainland China and its strategy changed, the East Point site successively became its headquarters, a mint, a sugar refinery, a shipyard, a warehouse, and a container terminal. It was only after the Chinese Communist Party came to power in 1949 and confiscated all the firm’s businesses and assets that Jardine Matheson moved its headquarters back to Hong Kong.

![In 1979, Prince Charles of Britain visited Hong Kong on behalf of Queen Elizabeth II and posed for a photo here (courtesy of The Excelsior)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190408170622YBUE2PpvuonmcMxV.jpg-w600)

![The old Dickens Bar interior at The Excelsior; Leslie Cheung was a regular and often sat in his designated spot (courtesy of The Excelsior)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190408170623HAcLZSPOzMgmr7l3.jpg-w600)

In the late 1960s, the Hong Kong government decided to build the Cross-Harbour Tunnel; seeing the profit potential, Jardine Matheson approved the construction of The Excelsior, which opened in 1973. It once claimed many citywide firsts: at 34 stories it was Hong Kong’s tallest hotel, it had the most rooms of any hotel in the city, and it was the first hotel in Hong Kong to employ female room attendants, among other distinctions. When Prince Charles of Britain visited in 1979, he also stayed at The Excelsior; other visiting notables included the late television mogul Sir Run Run Shaw and American singing legend Tony Bennett.

The hotel’s closure now marked the end of an era, and a chapter in the employees’ lives was closing as well.

At 11:59 p.m. on December 31, 2018, on the Jardine Noonday Gun platform on the Causeway Bay waterfront, Samuel kept his eyes fixed on the countdown screen on the building facade across Victoria Harbour—“3, 2, 1!” Standing beside Samuel were general manager Fan Lijie and his wife. Samuel pulled the cord; with a bang, the gun fired, fireworks burst in the sky at exactly the same moment, and the crowd cheered “Happy New Year.” A waiter passed around champagne, and Samuel toasted amid the celebration.

![The Excelsior's New Year's Eve midnight gun ceremony; Liu Weirong (Samuel) stands at the gun to officiate (file photo)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190408171027KRgxhcDMOrpwdato.jpg-w600)

![The Jardine Noonday Gun on the Causeway Bay waterfront, just outside The Excelsior](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2019040817102765cXhELCNrKZwQJP.jpg-w600)

Looking back, he laughed and said that at that moment he was “too stunned for words; I couldn’t hear anything.”

He was nervous because the Jardine Noonday Gun carries great significance. Owned by Jardine Matheson, its history dates back to the 1840s; it was originally set up to repel pirates and later became a time signal, fired every day at noon. The New Year’s Eve midnight gun ceremony each year was hosted by senior figures in the group, and only on major hotel anniversaries was it entrusted to The Excelsior’s general manager. The hotel happened to be marking its 45th anniversary, and Samuel had just been named 2018 Employee of the Year, so he was chosen to preside over the ceremony as a veteran staff member. In years past Samuel had simply stood below the gun platform holding a tray of champagne and greeting guests, joking that he “looked like an idiot.” He never dreamed he would become the main act, and he was so nervous that he lost sleep for several nights, afraid of making a mistake.

It had taken Samuel thirty years at the hotel to reach that position. In 1989, at the age of 24, he became a bartender at Dicken’s Bar. The bar was named after the great English writer Charles Dickens; the local jazz band Tony Carpio played there for eighteen years, and the bar was hailed as Hong Kong’s first home of jazz. Stars such as Leslie Cheung were also regulars.

![Samuel at a cocktail competition (courtesy of The Excelsior)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190408170113XLsC6gkoV49KqYN0.jpg-w600)

In 1993, Samuel designed a cocktail himself, passed the selection, and represented the hotel in the Beefeater cocktail competition, winning second place. After that he went to Austria to compete in a world championship and represented the hotel several more times. Time flew; he was promoted, moved through several departments, transferred to the banquet department, and met and married his wife at The Excelsior. When he got the call that his son had been born, he was at someone else’s wedding banquet and rushed off in a taxi.

Now the only thing on his mind was worry: “A hundred colleagues means a hundred families. After the demolition, what will happen to me and all the other staff?” Fortunately, the bond between guests and hosts endured, and The Excelsior worked hard to find placements for its staff; Samuel was hired by a sister hotel in the group. After working one job for thirty years, he worried only that he wouldn’t adapt to a new environment.

Alice, the housekeeping supervisor, had the same question. Thirty years had passed in a flash; she had planned to retire there, but life is unpredictable—her joining The Excelsior had been an accident.

Children from poor families grow up fast. With six siblings at home, she finished junior secondary school (Form 3) and started working at fifteen, spending years in the garment industry, paid by the piece. In 1988, when she was 23, The Excelsior was hiring; she had come to keep her friend company while signing up, but both were offered jobs on the spot, and she switched careers.

When the hotel opened it had 1,003 rooms, the most in Hong Kong. At first Alice had to clean seventeen rooms a day, with only half an hour for each; naturally she was “all thumbs” at the start. The routine covered everything from making the bed and cleaning the bathroom to dusting and vacuuming, plus arranging furnishings to standard and cleaning utensils—not a single hair could be left behind. Even when she didn’t know how to deal with foreign guests, she learned to answer with a smile first and use body language. Luckily the hotel offered a three-month English crash course twice a week, and she gradually learned to handle guests’ questions in English.

![Xu Minyi (Alice), a housekeeping supervisor, demonstrates bed-making—she sees housekeeping as a profession](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190408165505OBbVFR4unrIEh1pt.jpg-w600)

Moving up from room attendant to supervisor, Alice learned from patient mentors who were both teachers and friends, and she absorbed the craftsperson’s spirit: “Housekeeping is a profession too.” Bed-making has its own subtle knowledge; the three main rules are neatness, cleanliness, and no odor. “What matters most is that the guest feels comfortable—the moment they enter, the bed looks as neatly pressed as if it has been ironed.”

In one swift, practiced sequence, Alice shook out the tangled duvet and sheets, smoothed the wrinkles with her hand, and tucked each corner into place, all in one go; the whole process took just three minutes.

In the 1990s, a school for the blind visited the hotel, and she had to explain the bed-making process orally, inwardly anxious. Fourteen students gathered around the four-poster bed in the presidential suite. “I was thinking, how do I show them?” At the time the bed was made with three sheets sandwiching a blanket—four layers in all—and she explained the differences between each step layer by layer. In the end the students walked around the bed and felt her finished work, a moment she would never forget.

The friend who had joined with her also stayed at the hotel, and the two often looked back on the past. In her heart Alice had been counting down to the closing day; memories replayed in her mind, and she quietly cried at night. But people must move forward. She laughed and said that as long as her strength held out, she would keep working until retirement: “We’ll see if any hotel will take me in.”

Steve, the concierge, felt some reluctance too, but not as deeply. He planned to take a break, then open a small business with a friend. He spoke crisply and confidently, the archetypal smart Hong Kong guy: after all, a concierge stands on the front lines, dealing every day with the tricky problems posed by guests from every country; there is no routine, only resourcefulness.

![Concierge Steve has honed his skills and knows how to handle every kind of guest](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904081657310Bdg6pQI8bmjlGNU.jpg-w600)

After twenty-five years, there was no outlandish request Steve hadn’t seen. Lost valuables were common: a French tourist had taken a public bus to Stanley, left her wallet and passport behind, and he called the bus depot and accompanied her to retrieve them; she wept with relief. The hardest task was finding tickets for guests during the Hong Kong Sevens every March; even when sold out, he could usually pull strings and reach out to concierges at other hotels to track some down. After the Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macau Bridge opened, a guest asked him to arrange a car to travel back and forth between Hong Kong and Macau; finding a driver took a full two weeks. But there was one thing he would not do: “If a guest says, ‘Find me a woman,’ I refuse, because it’s illegal. You have to follow the hotel rules.” In Hong Kong, sex work itself is legal, but organizing and controlling prostitution is illegal.

The rest of the time, Steve tried to grant every request and deliver every mission. Service is hard; earning a guest’s praise is even harder, yet at The Excelsior it happened often. The reason the three veterans stayed came down to friendly colleagues and warm relations between guests and hosts. Steve reflected, “Regulars treat you like family.” In February, an elderly Filipino regular checked in for the last time; when he left, the stroke-affected old man hugged Steve and wept. He still couldn’t let go after returning home, so in March he came back with his whole family for one more stay.

![At the moment of farewell, staff stand on the lobby staircase calling out, “We’ll meet again if fate allows” (screenshot)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904081703392UbVguG5lFv6W13H.jpg-w600)

At noon on closing day, the “last guest,” Paul Cheeseman from Britain, presided over the gun ceremony. For 27 years, every time he visited Hong Kong he had stayed at The Excelsior, spending 183 nights there. General manager Fan Lijie thanked the staff: “People are what make a hotel attractive.” At the final moment of the farewell, staff gathered in the lobby and called out, “We are Excelsior! Thank you for your support—if fate allows, we’ll meet again!”

Half a century of history came to an end. The Excelsior had begun with the sound of the noonday gun, and now it faded away with it. It closed amid laughter and tears; people went their separate ways onto different paths in life. Only the gun continued to fire at noon. One shot clears the old year, but the bond remains forever in each person’s memory: people may scatter, but the feeling does not.
