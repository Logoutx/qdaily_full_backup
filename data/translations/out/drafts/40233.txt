"In my lifetime, I have witnessed profound changes in society. The most profound of these is the rise of artificial intelligence."

On the morning of April 27, 2017, Stephen Hawking delivered a speech by video at GMIC (the Global Mobile Internet Conference).

He spoke about the threats and potential of artificial intelligence, and called on everyone to stop AI research and development now. His reasoning: people may be unable to control the AI they create, and if that happens, a future in which a large share of humanity has no work is entirely possible:

"We cannot know whether we will be helped infinitely by AI, or ignored and marginalized, or conceivably destroyed by it. Indeed, we are concerned that clever machines will be able to do the jobs humans currently do, and rapidly wipe out millions of jobs."

More important than the question of whether to build a superintelligence, Hawking believed, is using AI to reduce the harm industrialization inflicts on nature. So rather than tirelessly pushing AI technology further, the more important task now is to focus on how to increase AI's social benefits.

"AI research and development is advancing rapidly. Perhaps we should all pause for a moment, and redirect our research from enhancing AI's capabilities to maximizing its social benefits."

To this end, the Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AAAI) has been devoting considerable attention to goal-directed neutral technology. Last October, Hawking established a new research institution in Cambridge, England — the "Leverhulme Centre" — which tackles, through interdisciplinary methods, some of the unresolved questions thrown up by the rapid development of AI research. Hawking also mentioned a report on AI drafted by the European Parliament.

"The report clearly holds that the world stands at the forefront of a new industrial robotics revolution. Its analysis of whether robots should be granted rights as electronic persons — equivalent to the status of a legal person — suggests this may be possible."

At TED, held around the same time in 2017, a dedicated session was also devoted to ["the impact of robots on humanity, and humanity's response"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/40183.html), featuring a chess player who battled a robot, a researcher who studies robots' exam-taking skills, and a computer science professor calling for the Three Laws of Robotics to be rewritten.

AI has become one of the most pressing issues of our time.

Below is the full text of Hawking's speech:

**《让人工智能造福人类及其赖以生存的家园》 (Making AI Benefit Humanity and the Home It Depends On)**

In my lifetime, I have witnessed profound changes in society. The most profound of these — and the one whose impact on humanity grows daily — is the rise of artificial intelligence. Simply put, I believe the rise of powerful AI will be either the best thing or the worst thing ever to happen to human history. I have to say that we still do not know which it will be. But we must do everything we can to ensure that its future development benefits us and our environment. We have no choice. I believe the development of AI is, in itself, a trend fraught with problems, problems that must be solved now and in the future.

AI research and development is advancing rapidly. Perhaps we should all pause for a moment, and redirect our research from enhancing AI's capabilities to maximizing its social benefits. With this in mind, the Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AAAI) established, from 2008 to 2009, a presidential panel on the long-term future of AI, and it has recently devoted considerable attention to goal-directed neutral technology. But our AI systems must work according to our will. Interdisciplinary research is one possible way forward: extending from economics, law, and philosophy to computer security, formal methods, and of course the various branches of AI itself.

Everything produced by civilization is the product of human intelligence, and I believe there is no essential difference between what a biological brain can achieve and what a computer can achieve. It therefore follows that "computers can, in theory, emulate human intelligence, and then surpass it." But we are not certain of this, so we cannot know whether we will be helped infinitely by AI, or ignored and marginalized, or conceivably destroyed by it. Indeed, we are concerned that clever machines will be able to do the jobs humans currently do, and rapidly wipe out millions of jobs.

As AI develops from its primitive forms and proves enormously useful, I also worry about the consequences of creating something that can equal or surpass humans: once AI escapes restraint, it will redesign itself at an ever-accelerating pace. Limited by the long course of biological evolution, humans will be unable to compete and will be superseded. This will cause enormous disruption to our economy. In the future, AI could develop a will of its own — a will in conflict with ours. Although I have always been an optimist about humanity, others believe humans can keep technology under control for a long time, so that we can see AI's potential to solve most of the world's problems. I am not so sure.

In January 2015, I signed, together with technology entrepreneur Elon Musk and many other AI experts, an open letter on artificial intelligence, intended to advocate serious research into AI's impact on society. Before that, Elon Musk had already warned people: superhuman AI could bring immeasurable benefits, but if deployed improperly, it could have the opposite effect for humanity. He and I both serve on the scientific advisory board of the Future of Life Institute, an organization devoted to mitigating existential risks facing humanity, and the aforementioned open letter was drafted by that organization as well. The letter calls for research aimed directly at preventing potential problems while also reaping the potential benefits AI offers us, and it works to make AI researchers pay greater attention to AI safety. Moreover, for policymakers and the general public alike, the letter is substantive, not alarmist. Everyone should know that AI researchers are seriously thinking through these concerns and ethical questions — we consider that very important. AI, for instance, has the potential to eradicate disease and poverty, but researchers must be able to create AI that can be controlled. That open letter, just four paragraphs long and titled *Research Priorities for Robust and Beneficial Artificial Intelligence*, laid out detailed arrangements for research priorities in an accompanying twelve-page document.

Over the past 20 years, AI has focused on the problems arising from building intelligent agents — that is, systems of all kinds that can perceive and act in a given environment. In this context, intelligence is a rational concept related to statistics and economics. Put plainly, it is the ability to make good decisions, plans, and inferences. Building on this work, a great deal of integration and cross-fertilization has been applied across AI, machine learning, statistics, control theory, neuroscience, and other fields. The establishment of shared theoretical frameworks, combined with the supply of data and processing power, has achieved remarkable success across a range of specialized areas: speech recognition, image classification, autonomous driving, machine translation, gait and locomotion, and question-answering systems.

As these fields develop, they form a virtuous cycle from laboratory research to economically valuable technology. Even small performance improvements yield enormous economic benefits, which in turn encourage longer-term, greater investment and research. It is now widely agreed that AI research is progressing steadily and that its impact on society is likely to grow; the potential benefits are immense, given that everything civilization produces is the product of human intelligence. We cannot predict what we might achieve when that intelligence is amplified by AI tools. But, as I have said, eradicating disease and poverty is not entirely impossible, and given AI's enormous potential, it is vitally important to study how to benefit from it while avoiding its risks.

Research on AI is now advancing rapidly. This research can be discussed in terms of the short term and the long term. Some short-term concerns involve autonomous driving — from civilian drones to self-driving cars. For instance, in an emergency, a self-driving car may have to choose between a small risk of a major accident and a high probability of a minor one. Another concern is lethal intelligent autonomous weapons. Should they be banned? If so, how should "autonomy" be precisely defined? If not, how should accountability be assigned for any misuse or malfunction? There are further concerns raised by AI's growing ability to interpret massive amounts of surveillance data — concerns about privacy — and about how to manage the economic impact of AI taking over jobs.

Long-term concerns center mainly on the potential risk of AI systems getting out of control as superintelligence that does not act in accordance with human will emerges — a powerful system that threatens humanity. Could such a misaligned outcome happen? If so, how would it come about? What research should we invest in to better understand and address the possibility of dangerous superintelligence emerging, or an intelligence explosion occurring?

Current tools for controlling AI technology — such as reinforcement learning, with its simple practical functions — are not enough to solve this problem. We therefore need further research to find and validate a reliable solution for keeping this problem in check.

Recent milestones — such as the aforementioned self-driving cars, and AI winning at Go — are signs of trends to come. Enormous investment is pouring into this technology. What we have achieved today will inevitably pale in comparison with what may be achieved decades from now. And we are far from able to predict what we might accomplish once our minds are amplified by AI. Perhaps, aided by the tools of this new technological revolution, we can repair some of the damage industrialization has caused to the natural world. Every aspect of our lives is about to change. In short, the success of AI could be the biggest event in the history of human civilization.

But AI could also be the end of human civilization, unless we learn how to avoid the dangers. I have said that the all-round development of AI could bring about the extinction of humanity — for instance, through the maximized use of intelligent autonomous weapons. Earlier this year, I joined scientists from around the world at a United Nations meeting in supporting its ban on nuclear weapons. We are anxiously awaiting the outcome of the negotiations. At present, the nine nuclear powers control roughly 14,000 nuclear weapons, any one of which could level a city, with radioactive waste contaminating farmland over vast areas. The most terrifying consequence would be triggering a nuclear winter — fire and smoke causing a global mini ice age. This would collapse the global food system and plunge the world into apocalyptic chaos, very likely killing the majority of people. We scientists bear a special responsibility for nuclear weapons, because it was scientists who invented them and discovered that their effects are far more terrible than originally imagined.

At this stage, my discussion of catastrophe may have frightened those of you in the audience. I apologize. But as participants here today, it is important that you recognize your position in shaping the future development of current technologies. I believe we must stand together — calling for support for international treaties, or signing open letters submitted to governments — and that technology leaders and scientists are doing everything in their power to prevent the rise of uncontrollable AI.

Last October, I established a new institution in Cambridge, England, to try to resolve some of the unresolved questions that have emerged amid the rapid development of AI research. The Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence is an interdisciplinary research institute devoted to studying the future of intelligence — something vital to the future of our civilization and our species. We spend a great deal of time studying history, looking deeply into it — mostly the history of stupidity. So it is a welcome change for people to turn instead to studying the future of intelligence. Although we are aware of the potential dangers, I remain optimistic at heart, and I believe the potential rewards of creating intelligence are immense. Perhaps, with the tools of this new technological revolution, we will be able to reduce the harm industrialization has inflicted on the natural world.

Every aspect of our lives will be changed. My colleague at the institute, Huw Price, has acknowledged that the Leverhulme Centre could be established partly because the university created the Centre for the Study of Existential Risk. The latter examines humanity's potential problems more broadly, while the Leverhulme Centre's research scope is relatively narrow.

The latest developments in AI include the European Parliament's call to draft a series of regulations to govern innovation in robotics and AI. Somewhat surprisingly, these involve a form of electronic personhood, to ensure that the most capable and advanced AI holds rights and responsibilities. A European Parliament spokesperson commented that as more and more areas of daily life increasingly come under the influence of robots, we need to ensure that robots serve humanity both now and in the future. The report submitted to members of the European Parliament clearly holds that the world stands at the forefront of a new industrial robotics revolution. Its analysis of whether robots should be granted rights as electronic persons — equivalent to the status of a legal person — suggests this may be possible. The report stresses that at all times, researchers and designers must ensure that every robot design incorporates a kill switch. In Stanley Kubrick's film *2001: A Space Odyssey*, the malfunctioning supercomputer HAL refuses to let the scientists back into the space capsule — but that is science fiction. What we face is fact. Lorna Brazell, a partner at the multinational law firm Osborne Clarke, said in the report: we do not recognize personhood for whales and gorillas, so there is no need to rush to accept personhood for a robot. But the concerns remain.

The report acknowledges that within a few decades, AI may surpass the range of human intelligence, and in doing so challenge the relationship between humans and machines. The report concludes by calling for the establishment of a European agency for robotics and AI, to provide expertise in technical, ethical, and regulatory matters. If members of the European Parliament vote in favor of legislation, the report will be submitted to the European Commission, which will have three months to decide what legislative steps to take.

![The film 2001: A Space Odyssey](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170427112025IQsFrowCDyzL3S2a.jpg-w600)

We should also play a role in ensuring that the next generation has not only the opportunity but also the determination to engage fully with scientific research at an early stage, so that they can continue to realize their potential and help humanity build a better world. That is what I meant when I spoke earlier about the importance of learning and education. We need to move beyond theoretical debates about "how things should be" and take action, to ensure they have the opportunity to participate. We stand at the entrance to a brave new world — an exciting world, full of uncertainty, and you are the pioneers. I wish you well.

Thank you!

**Q&A between Chinese entrepreneurs, scientists, and Hawking**

**Kai-Fu Lee, CEO of Sinovation Ventures:** Internet giants hold enormous quantities of data, and that data gives them all kinds of opportunities to trade user privacy and interests for huge profits. Under the temptation of enormous gains, they cannot regulate themselves. Moreover, this kind of behavior also makes it harder for small companies and entrepreneurs to innovate. You often talk about how to constrain AI, but the harder question is how to constrain humans themselves. How do you think we should constrain these giants?

**Hawking:** As I understand it, many companies use this data only for statistical analysis, but any use involving private information should be prohibited. It would help protect privacy if all information on the internet were encrypted using quantum-based technology, so that internet companies would be unable to crack it within a certain period of time. But the security services would object to this approach.

**Fu Sheng, CEO of Cheetah Mobile:** "Could the soul be a form of existence of the quantum? Or another manifestation in a higher-dimensional space?"

**Hawking:** I believe recent developments in AI — such as computers defeating the human brain in chess and Go — show that there is no essential difference between the human brain and the computer. On this point, my colleague Roger Penrose takes exactly the opposite view. Would anyone say a computer has a soul? For me, the notion of a soul is a Christian concept, tied to the afterlife. I think it is a fairy tale.

**Ya-Qin Zhang, president of Baidu:** "The way humans observe and abstract the world keeps evolving — from early observation and estimation, to Newton's laws and Einstein's equations, to today's data-driven computing and artificial intelligence. What comes next?"

**Hawking:** We need a new quantum theory that integrates gravity with the other forces of nature. Many people claim this is string theory, but I am skeptical of that. The only speculation at present is that space-time has ten dimensions.

**Shoucheng Zhang, professor of physics at Stanford University:** "If you had to tell aliens about humanity's greatest achievement, written on the back of a postcard, what would you write?"

**Hawking:** Telling aliens about beauty, or any art form that might represent the highest artistic achievement, would be pointless, because these are unique to humanity. I would tell them about Gödel's incompleteness theorem and Fermat's Last Theorem. These are things aliens could understand.
