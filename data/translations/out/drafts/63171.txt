As a book column, what follows recommends several books about cities and gentrification. The reading notes are long and will be published in three installments.

**Cities play an important role in our lives; much of the time, they are our lives themselves. Everyone expresses an understanding of the city—in a sense, that is their understanding of life.**

**Think of our recommendations as an index; what we hope they convey is not merely the conclusions these earlier thinkers reached but, as always, something that can advance our own thinking.**

**1**

*Like Shaking Hands with God* is not closely related to cities. It is a literature-related book, a collection of speeches by the novelist Kurt Vonnegut. One of them is a joint promotion by two people—Vonnegut and Lee Stringer—of their own novels.

We know little about this Lee Stringer: a Black man living in New York who felt he should record his own life, and so wrote a novel called *Grand Central Winter*. Vonnegut said: "Your book ought to have political consequences, even though you have no intention of calling for reform. At least in New York, this book (*Grand Central Winter*) deserves to be a bestseller; it shows most of us what we truly know nothing about: what the life of the homeless is like. You do not mean to whine on behalf of those who sleep in the open. But everyone who has read this book will surely say: Good God, something must be done to change this state of affairs."

What is to be done? Stringer asked.

> Suppress the phenomenon? Drive these people away? Make them disappear from our sight? Fill everyone's stomach?
> I don't know what can be done about it, besides figuring out what your relationship to it is.
> I think that is the only thing that can be done.
> It is not about wiping out the phenomenon that violates our sense of things and ourselves. It is only about discovering our relationship to it. Simply this: when you pass someone on the street, what is your relationship to that person?
> I mean, how are we related to one another as human beings? Everything else is empty talk.
>
>
> Kurt Vonnegut, *Like Shaking Hands with God*

"How are we related to one another as human beings." I recognize the importance of this question; in fact, every question explored below and every book recommended should, I believe, revolve around it. It is the premise of all thinking.

"Everything else is empty talk."

Given this premise, most of what we discuss here—especially what ought to be done—concerns cities in the global sense, not only our own city. Nor do we want to avoid our own city; looking farther afield helps broaden our horizons. Of course, only after looking farther afield will we think more about who the new poor are in a country founded in the name of the poor, why there are new poor, why we choose to look away and even try to drive them from our sight—especially once it has grown rich, powerful, and ascendant.

What we want to emphasize is that the one and only premise for all discussion of what a city should be, of gentrification, and of urban renewal is respect for people.

About cities, gentrification, government, "them," the poor, the middle class, and also "us"... whoever this "we" refers to—the reader currently looking at this recommendation, the reader of Vonnegut's book, or the reader of Stringer's book—in any case, our recommendations begin here.

### So What Is a City?

**2**

Sociologist Robert E. Park, in his book *The City*, strips away the notion of the city as a lifeless "artificial construct." The city "does not at first sight look like a natural growth unmarked by human art, and it is therefore hard to think of it as a living organism"; "people are easily deceived by the city's geometric exterior into thinking it is a purely artificial construct that can be taken apart and reassembled like a house built of blocks." In fact, the city arises from human nature.

> Yet the city is actually rooted in the habits and customs of its inhabitants. This means that, while it possesses a physical mechanism, it also retains a moral organism; the two interact, shape, and change each other in certain specific ways. As an objectively visible structure, the city is huge in scale and complex in organization, and this is often our first impression of it. Yet this structure arises from human nature; it is some expression of human nature. On the other hand, although this vast objective mechanism came into being to satisfy the practical needs of the inhabitants, once formed it becomes an external fact of nature imposed upon them, and in turn shapes these inhabitants according to its internal design and interests. The city's construction and traditions are merely different aspects of the same cultural complex; only this cultural complex can determine what distinguishes the city from the countryside and urban life from life in the wild.
>
>
> Robert E. Park, *The City*

**3**

Architect and planner Henry Churchill, from the perspective of his profession, explains what needs a city satisfies. "Gaudy lights, crowds, the tense atmosphere of the free market, the oppressive sense of a luxurious and sinful life in the metropolis are merely the surface of large city centers, not real urban life"—even though these are almost what his professional worth and reward ought to pursue, he still believes this is only the surface of the big city, not real urban life. "Those things may attract people to big cities; they may make the unworldly stare in astonishment and then call their cousins who live in small county towns 'hicks'; they can attract tourists or vagrants. But they are absolutely not what cities depend on for survival."

The true value of a city lies in:

> Broadly speaking, unless it is combined with the goals of social endeavor, planning for urban hardware facilities and economic planning are meaningless.
> The goal of social endeavor is, or ought to be: to make the city a place where children can be raised and educated into healthy, normal people; where people can find work sufficient to support their families, with adequate security; where convenience of life, social interaction, recreation, and cultural enrichment can all be realized. In every respect, this is no utopian ideal; many towns in the past and present have approached this goal.
> In fact, most people's needs are not many, nor do they fantasize about how high these standards are.
> A clean dwelling that meets a family's needs, with its own private space and a little room for children to play or for community activities; a good school; a stable job that provides the family enough for food, housing, clothing, and medical care; within the community, cinemas, bowling alleys, salons; libraries, museums, theaters, or lesser amenities. This is the main substance of everyday urban life.
>
>
> Henry Churchill, *The City Is the People*

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190424040700Vb0eLfaUqhynurCJ.jpg-w600)

**4**

We can use a lyrical passage by E. B. White about New York to underscore the essence of urban life once more.

> But the wonderful thing about New York is that every large geographical unit is made up of countless small neighborhoods. Each neighborhood is self-sufficient. Usually it is no more than two or three blocks long and a few blocks wide. Each neighborhood is a city within a city within the city. Therefore, no matter where you live in New York, within one or two blocks you can find a grocery store, barber shop, newsstand, shoe-shine stand, basement shop selling ice and coal (where you can write what you want to buy on a slip of paper outside the door as you pass), dry cleaner, laundry, deli (beer and sandwiches to go anytime), florist, funeral parlor, movie theater, radio repair shop, stationery store, clothing store, tailor's shop, pharmacy, parking lot, teahouse, bar, hardware store, shoe-repair shop. In most New York neighborhoods, every block or two there is a small commercial street. In the morning on the way to work, before walking two hundred yards, people can take care of five or six errands: buy a newspaper; drop off shoes at the shop to have soles nailed on; buy a pack of cigarettes; order a bottle of whiskey to be delivered after work; leave a note for the invisible man at the coal shop; tell the dry cleaner there is a pair of trousers waiting to be worn. Eight hours later, on the way home, buy a bunch of pussy willows, a Mazda light bulb, have a drink, get a shoe shine—all between the corner where they get off and their own door. These neighborhoods are fully equipped for everything, and people feel a sense of belonging; many New Yorkers spend their whole lives within an area no bigger than a village.
>
>
> E. B. White, *Here Is New York*

**5**

Here the first contradiction appears.

A great deal of the work—in the 1940s New York he depicts—would have been done by first- or second-generation immigrants: Eastern European Jews, Black people, Italians, Irish, and others. If it were Shanghai, and if we still had communities like that, it would very likely be done by people from Anhui, Jiangxi, Northern Jiangsu, Sichuan, and so on.

But city dwellers dislike insecurity; they are wary of newcomers and see them as destroyers of the city's fine landscape, possibly even potential criminals.

Yi-Fu Tuan, in *Topophilia*, emphasizes this need for "distinctiveness" and "purity." Human beings set boundaries for themselves, dividing the inside from the outside into "us" and "them"; the pursuit of purity on one's own side comes from a sense of security and from recognition of a culture and way of life.

> Communities with their own character have clear boundaries that set them apart from the mainstream of urban life. Their distinctiveness is based on economic, social, and cultural factors. In the city's grid, several types of neighborhoods can be recognized at a glance: extremely wealthy communities, extremely poor communities, suburban communities and slums with little contact with the outside world, shantytowns composed of racial minorities or immigrants, and so on.
> Yet the people who live in such places do not recognize the distinctiveness of their own communities to the same degree.
> The very wealthy attach great importance to the boundaries of their domain—"We must keep it pure."
> The middle class living in the suburbs is even more so than the former; they too attach great importance to the integrity of their domain, because compared with the rich, their territory is more easily invaded by "ill-mannered" outsiders.
> People of color in shantytowns are forced to develop a vigilance about home, because as soon as they are not near home, they can feel obvious hostility.
> Whites living in slums (for example, immigrants from Europe concentrated in certain areas) do not care much whether they occupy an area with specific boundaries and specific characteristics.
>
>
> Yi-Fu Tuan, *Topophilia*

In fact, in the next paragraph of the E. B. White passage above, this kind of security is already discussed.

> ...These neighborhoods are fully equipped for everything, and people feel a sense of belonging; many New Yorkers spend their whole lives within an area no bigger than a village. Walk two blocks farther and they feel as if they have entered foreign territory, ill at ease, and must come back. The owners of small shops are especially sensitive to neighborhood boundaries. A female friend of mine recently moved to another apartment three blocks away. The day after the move, she appeared at the grocery store she had patronized for years; when the shopkeeper saw her, he was so moved he almost shed tears. "Now that you've left," he said, "I thought I'd never see you again." To him, three blocks—or about seven hundred and fifty feet—was leaving.
>
>
> E. B. White, *Here Is New York*

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190424040709DHl3kOKf7zwBU0gv.jpg-w600)

### The City's Waste...

**6**

Problems and contradictions seem inevitable, and there are probably no good solutions. Fortunately, Stringer's question is always there: How are we related to one another as human beings? It keeps our thinking on what is probably the right dimension—if we leave aside the concept of "people," what value is there in thinking about these things?

[After all, cities are new](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62048.html), with little history; the major problems cities encounter—inner-city decay, community disorganization, gentrification, and so on—are for most cities encountering them for the first time. After all, as Park said, the city is an organism of human nature, and people are complex and constantly changing; they in turn act upon the city, and each step may produce different new problems.

**7**

Robert E. Park sees the city's processes of organization and disorganization as analogous to the anabolic and catabolic processes of an organism's metabolism. Individuals are integrated into the city; the individual becomes part of the social organism. "The natural way of possessing a culture is to be born into it. When a person is born into a family, that family has already adapted to a social environment—in this case, the modern city"; only thus do concepts such as community and neighborhood become more important. When the members of these communities encounter more economic changes—such as unemployment, poverty, and other problems—differentiation continually occurs in the process of urban development...

> As time goes on, every area of the city begins to take on something closely related to the character and quality of the residents within it. Every part of the city inevitably becomes tinged with the special feelings of its inhabitants. This transforms what was at first a geographically meaningless division into a neighborhood, that is, an area with sentiment, tradition, and its own history.
> Within this neighborhood, history maintains its own continuity.
> The past shapes the present; every neighborhood, under the impetus of its own dynamics, continually unfolds a new life, more or less independently of the larger life outside the neighborhood and the interests connected with it.
>
>
> Robert E. Park, *The City*

**8**

> I once heard William James say that "to go forward is a terrible thing." It is terrible mainly because it breaks the routines of life on which the existing social order depends, thereby destroying the original cultural and economic values—such as the habits of thrift, respect for skill and hard work, and people's hopes, ambitions, and life plans—which constitute the main content by which the social order is maintained.
> Those who have studied the great city will find that our great cities are full of waste, most of it human: the men and women who, for one reason or another, have fallen behind in the rapid advance of industrialization and been cast aside by the industrial organizations they once worked for.
>
>
> Robert E. Park, *The City*

The terrible thing about going forward—is also what most people are unwilling to admit or inadvertently ignore. If the city is an organism, it will inevitably keep producing waste; if the city's subject and product are both "people," then this "waste" will undoubtedly also be people.

The tragedy of the city more or less begins here.

Social division, urban renewal, gentrification, the dispossession of the inner-city poor, capital that is sometimes generous but more often cruel, government policies that are sometimes warm and sometimes cold... all revolve around it.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190424040734HGu4wLkKrREDyvnS.jpg-w600)

### One Principle: The Poor Are Not a Bad Thing

**9**

Because this involves the demographic structure of large cities, let us continue talking about the United States for now.

A city's attitude toward outsiders tilts toward wealthy high-end newcomers from the perspectives of tax revenue, employment growth, and urban prosperity. If this preference comes from civil society, although it is not very politically correct, it seems understandable. It is an instinctive need for a living environment: who wouldn't want neighbors more like themselves?

But if it is the city's leaders, that is another matter. For urban prosperity is no castle in the air; this is common knowledge those in power should possess.

A city's development is determined by people.

High-end populations are no castle in the air either. Has New York attracted high-end outsiders? Of course. But what matters most about New York is its ability to accept immigrants over more than a century. From the Irish to the Italians, to the Jews, to the liberated Black people from the South, to the Puerto Ricans, Koreans, Vietnamese, and of course Chinese today.

The same is true of large Chinese cities. The glory of Shanghai in its first hundred years after opening to foreign trade was likewise the result of accepting immigrants.

> In 2008, 36 percent of New Yorkers were born abroad, and 48 percent of New Yorkers used two languages at home. The corresponding figures for the United States as a whole were 13 percent and 20 percent. Between 1891 and 2009, except for 12 years, the New York Philharmonic relied on foreign-born music directors.
>
>
> Edward Glaeser, *Triumph of the City*

**10**

Immigrants contributed to New York's prosperity.

> With the resumption of large-scale immigration in the late 1960s, people from more parts of the world came in succession. By the 1980s, they had begun to revive local commercial streets in the city. Years of suburban migration, civil unrest, and the city's own fiscal crisis had left many commercial streets without investment.
> In the early twenty-first century, almost half of New York's small businesses were founded by foreigners. In addition, 90 percent of dry cleaners, 84 percent of grocery stores, 69 percent of restaurants, and 63 percent of clothing-store owners were immigrants.
>
>
> Sharon Zukin, Philip Kasinitz, and Xiangming Chen, *Global Cities, Local Streets*

**11**

A poor population is not a bad thing for a city.

> Cities are full of poor people, but it is not cities that make people poorer; rather, cities attract poor people with the prospect of improving their living standards. The poverty rate among people who have just entered a large city is higher than that of the permanent residents, which shows that the wealth of urban residents may increase substantially over time. The poor who come to the city from elsewhere are not fools, nor are they wrong. They flock to the city because the city has advantages they could not find in their original places of residence. The serious problem of urban slums lies not in there being too many people in the city, but in these residents being too detached from the economic center of the large city. A large poor population does indeed constitute a challenge we must face, but hoping that cities can accept millions of rural poor is far better than hoping that potential immigrants will die in agricultural isolation.
> The criterion for evaluating an area should not be the poverty that exists there, but its achievement in helping the poorer population improve their social and economic status. If a city is continuously attracting poorer people to flow in, helping them succeed, watching them move on, and then attracting new poor immigrants, then from the perspective of one of society's most important functions, it is successful. If a place has become the default home of the long-term poor, then it has failed.
>
>
> Edward Glaeser, *Triumph of the City*

**12**

It should be explained that in this article, community, neighborhood, and city are often interchangeable.

Many of the problems we discuss are more closely related to the inner city and differ from the new cities produced by suburbanization. However, it should be noted that Chinese cities, especially first- and second-tier cities, after one or more rounds of urban renovation, have seen large numbers of inner-city residents move out. For example, Shanghai's multiple "large residential communities" outside the Outer Ring Road and Beijing's multiple new towns outside the Fifth Ring Road mostly absorb inner-city populations. This is very different from suburbanization in developed countries; it is basically the wholesale relocation of the inner city.

It is completely different from the suburbanization process Neil Smith describes in *The New Urban Frontier*, in which urban residents expand their own and the city's boundaries, continuing the western frontier expansion. In fact, it is more brutal.

And in the process of suburbanization in Chinese cities, there is also something like the "urban-rural fringe"—more precisely, a Chinese version of the "slum" problem on the periphery of Asian cities. Because of systemic reasons, the political rights of the residents there are effectively deprived: they have neither the possibility of becoming a voting bloc nor the chance to gain periodic respite through elections.

Returning to the question itself. Although all the urban problems we face have an unanswerable side, we cannot ignore the question Vonnegut raised in response to Stringer's description: Good God, something must be done to change this state of affairs.

Much-debated gentrification emerges against this broad backdrop. It manifests differently in different places: urban renewal, urban transformation, greater livability...

### From Inner City to Suburb, and from Suburb Back to Gentrified Inner City: The Continuous Correction of Urban Development

**13**

Gentrification is part of urban development; its manifestation is inner-city revival, so people usually connect its rise with the rise and fall of the inner city. In fact, the rise and fall of the inner city has both social-disorganization causes and causes related to technological change reshaping the city.

> First, the loss of inner-city population is a result of the technological revolution. As early as 1930, Frank Lloyd Wright mentioned this in his Kahn Lectures at Princeton University.
> After giving five lectures on architecture, technology, style, housing, and skyscrapers, his last lecture was "The City." His words astonished the audience. He proposed, "I think the city as we know it today is gone." He listed many technologies—airplane, automobile, telephone, radio—precisely these technologies encouraged people to disperse.
>
>
> Witold Rybczynski, *Makeshift Metropolis*

**14**

Decades later, Robert Sampson believed that human beings had created a new "placeless" world. This new world is the result of the superimposed effects of globalization, innovations in communication technology, and urban diversity, among other factors.

> People have commonly heard this saying: because we can go anywhere, the condition of the place we belong to will not have much effect on our later lives. With the "decentralization" of identity, the neighborhood is seen as obsolete, replaced by global networks with interchangeable qualities. Other so-called factors that dissolve local context include economics, politics, and the state. We unwittingly follow Louis Wirth's formulation, but add some new metaphors: people cynically believe that individuals and places alike are atomized and have lost the communities in which they grew up. For another reason, this perspective, like the individual-choice perspective, leaves no room for neighborhood effects. It sees top-down ("big") processes as an all-encompassing force that overwhelms individuals and neighborhoods.
>
>
> Robert Sampson, *Great American City*

**15**

This process of technological change has not, in a sense, ended. The series of reports we have done in the ["QDaily Anthropology" column](http://www.qdaily.com/special_columns/5.html) on the reorganization of people based on internet communities is, in fact, a vivid illustration of the "dispersion" Frank Lloyd Wright spoke of and the "decentralization" Robert Sampson spoke of.

> Although technology makes it easier for people to obtain images of places, it also reinforces the view that local communities are obsolete. Nineteenth-century railroads and photographs made it easy to travel to distant places, but also deprived local horizons of their immediacy and depth. Today, even the smallest places can be connected through the synchronous exchange of electronic media, yet this destroys the social distance that gives local experience its uniqueness. In short, as markets become increasingly global, place increasingly declines.
>
>
> Sharon Zukin, *Landscapes of Power: From Detroit to Disney World*

**16**

"Decentralization," together with the "deindustrialization" mentioned in articles we have previously recommended, has reshaped the city. With the development of the automobile industry, Frank Lloyd Wright's encouragement for people to "disperse" has to a certain extent become reality. Suburbanization for a considerable period became a city's top priority.

**17**

But in Calvino's writing, this new type of city holds little appeal; he even feels that super-industrialization has returned humanity to something like the agricultural civilization era.

> Compared with the two months I previously spent in New York, my travels in Cleveland and Detroit showed me much more of "America." To some extent, my earlier impression that "America is not American enough" was somewhat corrected.
> What changed first was my own concept of "city." Driving off the highway and looking for the city—where is the city? Driving around for hours, yet unable to find what one imagines as a city center. Yes, there is still a "downtown," a concentration of offices, but residential areas have disappeared; houses are scattered here and there like in our countryside. The middle class generally lives in two-story villas, sparsely situated along the roads of more-or-less identical communities. Here one can hardly move without a car, but there is indeed nowhere to go. Around the residential areas there are no traditional small shops or restaurants; perhaps at some intersection there is a shopping center, the place where people can go to consume and shop.
> We might think this is the result of highly concentrated urban commerce, but it has gone too far. The city has become fragmented, and customs and ways of thinking have changed accordingly; super-industry has returned the world to a state centered on the small family, the whole family gathered around the small stove (now the television set), just like the agricultural civilization of yesterday.
>
>
> Italo Calvino, *An Optimist in America*

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190424040138jsip1t07bwhDWCNo.jpg-w600)

**18**

The problems of suburbanization go beyond this. In less than a generation, the drawbacks have become fully apparent. *Suburban Nation* by Jeff Speck and others summarizes several problems that may cause harm far into the future.

> Unfortunately, this model of racial segregation is self-perpetuating. A child who grows up in such a homogeneous environment finds it hard to feel sympathy for people from another social class and hard to integrate into a diverse society. "The others" are outsiders to the child, seen only on supposedly exaggerated television programs. The more similar and safe people's living environments are, the less they understand different people or things and the more indifferent they are to the world beyond the walls.
> This is in fact a double-edged sword: the poor also know little about the middle class, firmly believing that those people are not of the same kind as themselves, and turn a deaf ear to their suffering.
>
>
> Andres Duany, Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk, and Jeff Speck, *Suburban Nation*

**19**

In China, even though no purely suburbanized city has appeared, this problem of "beggar-thy-neighbor" has already begun to show itself, and even more fiercely. In *Suburban Nation*, the authors call the new generation of middle-class children born in the suburbs "cul-de-sac kids."

We are more familiar with another name for them: "giant babies."

> The wide, safe, green-covered spaces of the suburbs are good for children to play in; that is the upside. But what is bad for children? It is that children in the suburbs lose their autonomy of movement. In the suburbs, various activity places are separated and independent, and the distance between them must be measured by the car's odometer. In such an environment, the range of a child's independent movement will not exceed the boundary of the residential area; even the local baseball field is often beyond a child's independent reach.
> The result is a new phenomenon—the "cul-de-sac kid," children who live like prisoners in an absolutely safe yet utterly unstimulating environment.
> Perhaps before the age of five this situation is acceptable; some people even want it. But what about the next ten or twelve years? Children will constantly depend on adults to drive them everywhere; thus children and teenagers have no opportunity to mature through practice.
> They cannot run out to buy a bucket of milk on a simple errand; they cannot ride a bicycle to the toy store to spend their own pocket money; they cannot suddenly show up in front of their mother at work; they usually cannot even walk to school; even the impromptu baseball games children used to cobble together have become history, because parents, in order to pick up each carpool child at the appointed time, have to arrange things with military precision.
> Children's self-care ability remains at the infant stage; they depend on others for everything.
> They have lost both the ability to create a colorful life and the opportunity to judge and choose for themselves.
> Suburban parents often give children allowance to encourage their sense of independence. "Buy whatever you like!" they say. And the child replies, "Thanks, Mom. When can you drive me to the mall?"
>
>
> Andres Duany, Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk, and Jeff Speck, *Suburban Nation*

**20**

And then there is the car. It has turned a communicative people into enemies fighting each other—if you remember Tocqueville's enthusiastic praise of the nineteenth-century American, you will surely feel despair at the following description.

> In the suburbs, time once spent in the public realm is now wasted in a space that is both private and potentially antisocial: the car.
> An ordinary American, the moment he sits behind the steering wheel, ceases to be a citizen and becomes a driver.
> This means that you can no longer become a friend who knows and is familiar with your neighbor, because your relationship is primarily competitive.
> You fight over road space; if you hesitate while driving, or make the slightest wrong move, your neighbor will immediately protest: honking at you, cutting in front of you, driving ahead of you, even other rude behavior may occur.
> When people defend their various unreasonable, rough, and provocative actions, driving has become as overused an excuse as drinking—"He cut into my lane; it was absolutely unbearable!" Thus the social contract is torn up. The reasons behind this are worth further study.
> It can be said with certainty that it is extremely rare for two pedestrians passing each other to act aggressively.
>
>
> Andres Duany, Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk, and Jeff Speck, *Suburban Nation*

**21**

The suburban middle class begins to miss city life. Reflection unfolds at every level.

> "Civic life requires places where people can meet one another as equals. From political groups to parks, and even other informal gathering places, all urban public spaces are in decline, so human interaction has almost become as specialized a matter as knowledge production." (Christopher Lasch, *The Revolt of the Elites and the Betrayal of Democracy*) In the absence of walkable spaces—public realms such as streets, squares, and parks—people of different ages, races, and beliefs rarely have the chance to meet and talk. Some believe that websites and chat rooms on the Internet can effectively replace public space, but they seriously underestimate the difference between a computer monitor and a real human body.
>
>
> Andres Duany, Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk, and Jeff Speck, *Suburban Nation*

**22**

Even Disney has become a kind of wealth: it satisfies the possibility of social exchange that is lacking in life. If we once again recall Tocqueville on the democratic atmosphere and community identity of American society, we can feel what kind of alienation has occurred in the course of modern urban development to things that once seemed "taken for granted."

> Large numbers of suburban residents like to vacation there. A Disney architect has pointed out that visitors typically spend only 3 percent of their time playing games and watching performances; the vast majority of the rest is spent enjoying a commodity severely lacking in their suburban homes: pleasant and convenient walkable public space, and the social activity fostered by such an atmosphere.
> Social space has now become almost the patented product of Disney and large shopping-mall developers, whereas in the past it was something city builders (whether ancient Greek fishermen or early American observers) considered a matter of course. Only with the rise of modern urban planning did successful public-environment construction gradually decline.
> Among the many reasons for this decline, a very important one is that the rules for building these public places are too simple.
>
>
> Andres Duany, Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk, and Jeff Speck, *Suburban Nation*

**23**

The conclusion is that gentrification is inescapable.
