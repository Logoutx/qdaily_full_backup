Besides the kitchen, Instagram is the second battlefield chefs need to conquer.

If there's a "pretty face" among social platforms, it's Instagram.

The founders, Mike Krieger and Kevin Systrom, probably never imagined that an app originally intended to let people communicate and share their real-life experiences through photos would end up fueling the "looks economy," with fashion, design, food, and just about every other field caught up in it.

Food in particular: it pushed people's pursuit of "beauty" to its absolute peak, and for that very reason, "good-looking" can sometimes make people more forgiving about whether something actually tastes good.

People love sharing everything they've eaten, everywhere, on Instagram. Content tagged #food on the platform exceeds 180 million posts; #foodporn has roughly 95 million; #yummy has 77 million. The term "Foodstagram" was even coined to tease the tribe of food photographers.

So whether or not they're active users, anyone on Instagram is bound to stumble across a steady stream of food photos.

That's how "internet-famous foods" were born. [As one Wall Street Journal report put it](http://www.wsj.com/articles/broccoli-rabe-is-trying-to-be-the-next-kale-1465927540), foods like avocado, kale, and turmeric were made famous by Instagram.

![Avocados and kale made famous on Instagram.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818143749hyPUkC9vgYqtb0xI.jpeg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824143833stDQU9Vfv1Hwg07C.jpg-w600)

Driven by this force, the entire restaurant industry is more or less pandering to Instagram.

[Sonic, the fourth-largest fast-food chain in the US](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/25981.html), designed and made a milkshake specifically for Instagram during this past April's Coachella music festival in California; the chain restaurant Chili's [spends an extra $750,000 a year brushing egg wash on its burgers](http://pix11.com/2015/05/16/chilis-to-spend-750000-a-year-to-make-food-more-instagram-friendly/) — an egg wash — just so the tops glisten; and a "filter restaurant" in Thailand painted its entire space in the five colors of filters, encouraging diners to take photos and upload them to Instagram and other social platforms.

Even chefs who always felt that taking photos of their food was an offense have changed their tune. [Fernando Peire, director of London's The Ivy](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/21370.html), said, "We used to solemnly print 'no mobile phones or cameras' on our menu, but times have changed." Many chefs now treat Instagram as a place to gather customer feedback — even to study and learn recipes — picking up other people's color schemes, cooking techniques, and dish pairings. Which is why there's a saying that besides the kitchen, Instagram is the second battlefield chefs need to conquer.

Everyone is studying Instagram, and when it comes to the exposure and engagement businesses crave, Instagram has no shortage of either. [According to its most recent quarterly figures](http://blog.instagram.com/post/146255204757/160621-news), registered users have passed 500 million, with over 300 million daily active users. In engagement, it leaves Facebook and Twitter far behind, with more than 1 billion interactions — a number even Snapchat, currently more favored by young people, can hardly approach.

No one underestimates Instagram's marketing power, though it carries risks as well. Still, people can hardly resist the lure of a beautiful food photo. Many businesses seem almost obsessive, going to great lengths to make their products look as Instagram-worthy as possible on the platform.

First, feast your eyes on the foods that have been trending on Instagram.

### Foods that vividly demonstrate what "colorful" means

If you ask which color of food is most popular on Instagram, the answer is definitely rainbow.

Starbucks' rainbow drinks took over Instagram not long ago, with combined likes and comments on related posts topping one million. Although Starbucks claimed it knew nothing about it, it quickly rolled out an official menu and published detailed recipes.

![Starbucks' rainbow drinks from its secret menu took over Instagram not long ago. Other rainbow-colored foods had their moment too.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817221955Ypv9xLIZBs1rloXO.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608172220044BlVEhyI9Z5SeLTR.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818140428TkKNCyFWrl4gB70I.jpg-w600)

Unicorn hues and macaron pastels — those soft, dreamy colors — also had their moment on Instagram. Such colors are eye-catching in themselves, and their "everything looks better together" quality has a built-in cross-selling effect. Fluffe Nathan from Sydney posts photos [on her Instagram account](https://www.instagram.com/fluffegram/) of dreamy desserts she makes herself, like cloud ice cream overflowing with girlish whimsy and unicorn-colored pancakes.

![Sydney's [@fluffegram](https://www.instagram.com/fluffegram/) specializes in all kinds of dreamy desserts, like unicorn-colored ice cream and pancakes — recommended for anyone with a soft, girlish heart.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817173758qy4LCWH3i6NVReYM.png-w600)

Ordering a smoothie by calling out its swatch number instead of its name must be a pretty fun experience.

Not just food brands: even fresh, a cosmetics brand built around natural ingredients, promotes its face masks this way. To promote a mask said to contain 50% orange pulp, fresh teamed up with American photographer Brittany Wright ([@wrightkitchen](https://www.instagram.com/wrightkitchen/)), who specializes in playing with color. They cut dozens of oranges into cross-sections and arranged them in a gradient sequence based on their colors.

### Highly creative foods — usually ones with novel shapes

We once reported on a food called the "raindrop cake" that started trending in New York this past April. The person who brought this trendy food to New York was 36-year-old [Darren Wong;](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/25843.html) inspired by Japan's shingen mochi, he used agar to create a delicate, transparent raindrop cake ([@raindropcake](https://www.instagram.com/raindropcake/)), and promoted it on Instagram. A raindrop cake priced at $8 sold 700 pieces on its New York debut day. "This is a light, delicate, refreshing raindrop, prepared specially for your appetite," he wrote on the website introducing the cake.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817172858tAIBb51ZrwPoYciM.jpg-w600)

But capturing the raindrop cake's transparency in a photo isn't easy either. Photographer Tim Ireland not only had to figure out "the difference between fully transparent, translucent, and glossy translucent, and get the lighting right" — he also had to keep the cake from evaporating after sitting in the air too long.

Another trick is to use camera angles to make familiar foods feel unfamiliar, drawing people in with that strangeness. Take this cross-section of an apple pie posted by [@theartofplating](https://www.instagram.com/theartofplating/). It looks like a geometric figure derived from a mathematical theorem or a physics formula.

![An eerily mathematical, formula-like cross-section of an apple pie. From @theartofplating](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817172913Dl9BJrapmSOYyV6t.png-w600)

Oreo has played this trick too. In one photo, a physical Oreo cookie becomes part of the painting — the lens of an astronomical telescope, with a girl looking at the stars through it. Of course, what's even better [on Oreo's Instagram account](https://www.instagram.com/oreo/) is its short videos, where thousands of creative ideas about the cookie come to life.

![Oreo's Instagram account is a collection of all kinds of cookie creativity. From [@oreo](https://www.instagram.com/oreo/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824133944UMSe8WhfXRJ2Oiab.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824134229cyVlHPb4I3NqsgE2.jpg-w600)

### Foods that look meticulously arranged, yet dazzle the eye

The most typical example is the Cube series shot by Dutch photographers Lernert and Sander. They cut foods into uniformly sized little cubes and arranged them in order; apart from the colors, every piece looks copy-pasted — laid out on a computer desktop, they'd tile perfectly.

![From [@lernert_sander](https://www.instagram.com/lernert_sander/); their [personal website](http://lernertandsander.com/) is great fun too.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817174402UbqCsSI7j12zMwgn.jpg-w600)

Another example: this photo of chocolates from [@theartofplating](https://www.instagram.com/theartofplating/), with chocolates of various shapes and colors arranged in an orderly spread across a marble countertop. Isn't it beautiful?

![A flat-lay photo of chocolates from @theartofplating.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817223749nEClyPLV3H1hkzWm.png-w600)

And then there's Michael Zee, the British guy who loves cooking for his partner and showing off their his-and-hers meals. He has now posted more than 1,000 symmetrical breakfasts on Instagram [(@symmetrybreakfast)](https://www.instagram.com/symmetrybreakfast/), with over 620,000 followers — more than many brands' marketing accounts. His most popular photo has drawn over 20,000 likes and nearly 300 comments.

![Michael Zee photographs the daily breakfasts he makes for himself and his partner in symmetrical form. Posting one photo a day, he has kept it up for more than three years.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818141614cMdA7xb9ZkVersKQ.png-w600)

### Foods that look healthy

Instagram has become a showcase for light, healthy eating: from the acai bowls that nearly every European and American blogger has in hand, to detox vegetable juices; from kale salads to quinoa porridge — the #eatclean hashtag has 33 million posts in total.

Fruit, grains, frozen yogurt, and leafy greens have become must-have elements on Instagram's healthy-food checklist. It seems that with them, any plate acquires a healthy, organic soul. And the bowl or plate holding that food must be glass, wood, or white porcelain, so you can see the richness of the ingredients at a glance.

![The acai bowl that nearly every European and American blogger has in hand. Fruit, grains, frozen yogurt, and leafy greens have become the soul of Instagram's healthy-food checklist.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817174937ARkt8QxaSrcVqz21.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817225837d0SGl9hNvmf7AyTH.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817225855zRXJq23keLymSpBU.png-w600)

A Sydney high school student named Georgia Boonen told the media that many of her friends follow nutritionists on Instagram, and the nutritionists post what they eat in daily life: smashed avo, chia pudding, and acai bowls.

"(Eating healthy) has become a kind of pop culture — healthy eating suddenly became the thing on campus. Everyone still loves junk food like fries, pizza, and sodas, but when you're drinking one of those drinks, people think: wow, so cool," Georgia says.

According to [Google Trends data](https://www.google.com/trends/explore?q=turmeric), kale and turmeric — which began popping up frequently on social media last year — are among the stars of healthy meals. Originally, these two foods were nutritious but niche, even unappealing; because they kept appearing on food bloggers' timelines, they became superfoods on Instagram.

### Desserts that merely look "healthy"

Although this may seem to contradict the point above, according to [Adweek's data](http://www.adweek.com/news/advertising-branding/here-s-how-food-brands-can-nail-instagram-165224), desserts — along with chocolate, chicken, vegetarian dishes, and salads — are the foods Instagram users most love to show off.

Not all desserts are equally popular, though. Even though desserts are basically high in sugar and fat, the ones that look fresher and lighter get preferential treatment — like the pistachio cake made by [@acozykitchen](https://www.instagram.com/acozykitchen/), whose muted, slightly grayish pink just seems healthier. Whether it actually is — who knows.

![A pistachio cake made by [@acozykitchen](https://www.instagram.com/acozykitchen/); its slightly grayish pink makes it look healthier than loud, garish foods.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608171746132tNADy81XlUw7jMY.png-w600)

Meanwhile, frozen yogurt and milkshakes are replacing ice cream as the popular summer treats.

Behind most of these refined food photos lies careful design. You may have noticed that many are shot top-down, and quite a few come from professional photographers — [Vice once ran a whole piece on "why is all that Instagram food porn shot from above?"](http://munchies.vice.com/articles/why-all-of-that-instagram-food-porn-is-shot-from-above?utm_source=nextdraft&utm_medium=email). So what have businesses done to attract more customers with beautiful photos?

### Designed and made specifically for Instagram

As mentioned earlier, Sonic, the fourth-largest fast-food chain in the US, rolled out an Instagram-designed milkshake at this past April's Coachella music festival in California. They specifically hired celebrity chef Christine Flynn to do the food styling. From color to shape, every detail was steered toward internet-famous-food territory.

![A milkshake Sonic designed specifically for Instagram. Whether shot top-down or head-on, the cup is square. Such geometric elements not only match Instagram's own aesthetic, they also carry a neat, ritualistic feel.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817223918qa05IPZr6SYW1Ue8.jpg-w600)

[In Sonic's promotional photos](https://www.instagram.com/p/BERorDMR4zQ/?taken-by=sonicdrivein), the milkshake cup is square whether shot top-down or head-on. Such geometric elements not only match Instagram's own aesthetic, they also carry a neat, ritualistic feel. And the color scheme is beautiful too. To highlight the ingredients, each flavor — lavender, brown sugar, butter toffee — gets its own dominant background hue. Background and milkshake combine in harmony, as if picked straight from a color palette.

Before the festival, Sonic had 127,000 followers; by the end of the campaign, the company had gained more than 70,000.

[Chain restaurant Chili's](http://pix11.com/2015/05/16/chilis-to-spend-750000-a-year-to-make-food-more-instagram-friendly/) even brought in a brand consultancy to make sure the Instagram-addicted young crowd would like the food in its restaurants. For this it added a step to its burger grilling — before the burgers go into the oven, brushing an egg wash (a mix of 20% egg and 80% water) onto the surface of the buns — at a cost of $750,000 a year. "But it makes it look great, it glistens, it shines," said the CEO of the restaurant's parent company.

![Brushing a layer of egg wash onto the buns before grilling makes the finished burgers a more appetizing color.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817224007GkTKpEc4VqQbjgCJ.jpg-w600)

On top of that, the restaurant serves fries in stainless-steel trays and cuts steaks into small pieces to make sure the food looks good — in short, everything is about getting people to post it on Instagram.

### Enlisting big food influencers for exposure, turning niche tastes into mass trends

A Wall Street Journal report pointed out that healthy foods known as "superfoods" — avocado, kale, turmeric, acai, and the like — were all made famous on Instagram.

A few years ago, avocado growers wanted to broaden the avocado's appeal beyond salads and guacamole, so they did some field research. A marketing company called Avocados From Mexico, while surveying campuses, noticed some students spreading avocado mash onto toast. That scene gave the marketers an idea: they found some major food influencers and got them to post this way of eating online. After a barrage of photos of healthy breakfasts, the avocado became a breakfast choice for many people, and the US later became the world's biggest avocado consumer.

Turmeric, a spice born of Indian curry, followed a similar path to popularity.

According to [Google Trends](https://www.google.com/trends/explore?q=turmeric), it started heating up in 2015. At the time, a marketer in San Diego named Rachel Kay began running marketing campaigns for products (including turmeric powder and turmeric drinks) from a turmeric supplier, Gaia Herbs, as well as other products.

![Turmeric powder from India used to be used mostly in curries; now many companies turn it into turmeric drinks and other health foods, and even hire PR teams to build buzz for it.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817172649SYtFkvdEc26bJBNm.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817224036l5UCQe7fjYA0JTgz.jpg-w600)

Wellness and fitness bloggers posted the dishes they made with turmeric, and brought out nutritionists' talking points — for instance, that turmeric is anti-inflammatory. An exotic food like this quickly got followers joining in the photo-posting too.

Broccoli rabe (also called rapini) is an old-fashioned food that typically shows up in Italian restaurants and wasn't common in salads. To raise its exposure, the broccoli rabe grower Andy Boy hired a PR team and partnered with Candice Kumai, an influential celebrity chef on Instagram, who was tasked with developing new recipes around the ingredient and endorsing it on her account.

![Broccoli rabe typically appears as a side dish in Italian restaurants and isn't common in other countries. Businesses hired PR teams and Instagram celebrity chefs to endorse it. From @eatbroccolirabe](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608172241131uqYeOKS5bchsCMi.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608172242553XyTbamcCOGZV9Ht.png-w600)

### So where did these Instagram food influencers come from?

Quite a few people became Instagram stars by persistently and single-mindedly posting food photos. Most of them are cooking enthusiasts, and their path to fame is the same as fashion bloggers'.

Take Michael Zee, who posts only breakfast on Instagram: his account [@symmetry breakfast](https://www.instagram.com/symmetrybreakfast/) already has 622,000 followers. [Deliciously Ella](http://www.instagram.com/deliciouslyella/) is another vivid, lively account, now at 927,000 followers. Both have published food-related books because of their popularity.

![Photo from the symmetry breakfast account](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818153124QYX2nevZAIPGoDTq.png-w600)

They're not a rare breed. Many have turned a pure hobby into a career this way — like American photographer Brittany Wright, who loves posting gradient-colored foods and whose [Instagram account](https://www.instagram.com/wrightkitchen/) now has 174,000 followers; or Henry Gargreavs, who switched from modeling and runs an account recommending cafés, [@coffeecupsoftheworld](https://www.instagram.com/coffeecupsoftheworld/), which collects coffee cup designs from around the world.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608181520045mzxb94GyTq2vKwi.png-w600)

"To a large extent, what we choose to eat comes from what our eyes see. Maybe for just a few hundred dollars paid to a food influencer, our restaurant can gain 500,000 customers," says Olivia Yound, brand director of Vaucluse and Osteria Morini. Helen Zhang of LFB Media agrees: "A lot of people decide what to eat tonight based on photos on Instagram, and that's something restaurants can't afford to ignore."

So plenty of businesses cater to these influencers — sometimes treating them to free tastings, sometimes just slipping them cash. [According to the Wall Street Journal](http://www.wsj.com/articles/food-instagrammers-turn-their-accounts-into-professions-1447804336), they get $350 for a single posted photo.

### With no conditions to do so, they create the conditions to make food photograph well

[Israel's Catit restaurant](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/9487.html) offers diners several plates made specifically for Instagram. Unlike ordinary flat plates, their sides curve at nearly a right angle — this provides a solid-color background for photos. Designed by a ceramic artist, the plate even thoughtfully includes a phone rest.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817225030h4zKJOMwTt1690Sf.jpg-w600)

And so that diners chasing that refined-dish look no longer have to move clutter out of the frame when shooting, the restaurant offers another plate called the "360." The name gives it away: it's a rotating plate, letting you find a dish's most beautiful angle. You can't help suspecting the restaurant's values really are "looks matter more than taste" — the cost for an hour (including looking, shooting, and eating) is $155. To boost brand exposure, they also encourage customers to add the corresponding hashtag after taking photos and uploading them to Instagram.

[Thailand's filter restaurant Hashme](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/20390.html) provides a dedicated shooting venue for food-photography lovers. Even its name comes from the English word "hashtag." For the interior design, Thai architecture studio [Onion](http://www.onion.co.th/) pulled off a clever, color-loving design. Five "filter" colors were methodically painted on the walls, ceiling, and furniture, so the whole space feels covered in filters. The restaurant's palette is intense without being tacky, making it a go-with-everything backdrop — with almost no effort, you can shoot beautiful photos of food that looks delicious.

![This filter restaurant painted five colors on the ceilings, walls, tables, and chairs of specific areas.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817225257j4pocHJzIUdxSC8X.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160817225347YFaiCxHsmBXtOlUN.jpg-w600)

That's how Instagram has changed and influenced the restaurant industry. But one notable phenomenon: according to the "Q1 2016 Instagram Study" released by social media analytics company Quintly, Instagram user engagement fell sharply year over year. During 2015, the posting frequency of Instagram photo posts rose 12%, while video posts rose 56%. When you're surrounded by denser and denser streams of information, the attention you pour into any single photo drops.

Which means standing out on Instagram will only get harder.
