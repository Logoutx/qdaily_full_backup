"Every era has its problems. The so-called 'bad times' are never an excuse for complaint or escape. Human beings are born into an imperfect society. So the most interesting and most enjoyable thing a person can do in his lifetime is, through his own efforts, to change society — even if only by a tiny bit. I think this is what everyone should do."

Mr. Sheng Hong gave us this conversation — in a version he reviewed and corrected, he titled it "Striving in an Imperfect Society."

Sheng Hong, 64, is a well-known Chinese economist and director of the Unirule Institute of Economics, a Chinese independent think tank. He was one of the first scholars in China to introduce and study new institutional economics.

On the afternoon of February 12, Mr. Sheng Hong received our interview in his office. Our conversation began with the 40th anniversary of China's reform and opening-up — a field in which he specializes. Since the late 1980s, he has devoted himself to research on the structure, origins, and evolution of institutions; the conflict, fusion, and integration of civilizations; and international political economics. His works include *The Division of Labor and Exchange*, *The Transitional Economics of China*, *In Search of a Stable Form of Reform*, *A Scholar Aspires to the Way*, and *An Economic Interpretation of Confucianism*.

A month later, as we were about to publish this interview, we were encouraged to see Premier Li Keqiang explicitly state in the government work report that "thinking must be further emancipated, reform further deepened, and opening further expanded." At the same time, we could not avoid noticing that the consensus built up over many years around institutional construction, the direction of reform, and the spirit of openness is being eroded.

This interview covers 16 questions, including economic growth, urbanization, the demographic dividend, land system reform, state-owned enterprise (SOE) reform, health care reform, tax rate reform, and more. These are also the topics endlessly debated in the history of reform and opening-up and of great public concern. It can be read as an inquiry into the "consensus" and a reexamination of 40 years of experience.

During the interview, Sheng Hong carried the manner of a Confucian scholar, a style he favors, and he takes Confucius's line "If the Way prevailed under Heaven, I would not be trying to change things" as his own value standard. "Every era has problems. The so-called 'bad times' are never an excuse for complaint or escape. Human beings are born into an imperfect society. So the most interesting and most enjoyable thing a person can do in his lifetime is, through his own efforts, to change society — even if only by a tiny bit. I think this is what everyone should do," Sheng Hong told QDaily.

From the end of 2017 through 2018, we held in-depth exchanges with all kinds of social groups, reviewing 40 years of social change in China — both to mark the 40th anniversary of reform and opening-up and in the hope that our society will be better in the future. The interview with Mr. Sheng Hong is part of this large project.

We will continue publishing in topic form.

### How Should We View the Real GDP Numbers?

**Q: In January, data released by the National Bureau of Statistics said China's GDP grew 6.9% in 2017, accelerating for the first time in seven years. How do you view this figure? Why did this happen?**

**S:** Because China is huge, with different parts, different industries, different regions. One person's feeling is partial; it depends on where you are. For instance, if you work at Alibaba, you might feel growth is especially fast; but if you're in a sunset industry, a traditional industry, you'd feel things are declining. So different people have different feelings. And now people have developed a distrust of official statistics, because recently many provinces have been exposed as having inflated their statistical data. Even if the National Bureau of Statistics says "our system is different from theirs," the country as a whole and the provinces are isomorphic in political structure. By isomorphic, I mean the statistics bureau is not independent. In this sense, we have reason to doubt official statistics. I actually have an article on FTChinese.com — I don't know if you've read it?

**Q: [*China's Two Economies*](http://www.ftchinese.com/story/001075356).**

**S:** Right. I did a bit of analysis. Because there's a new economy and an old economy, the two overlaying and offsetting each other, the final figure comes to roughly 4.x%. Of course that's not necessarily entirely correct — it's just a relatively cautious judgment. Also, we have some other alternative indicators — isn't there the Li Keqiang index? Though even that is not necessarily accurate now. One basis of the Li Keqiang index is the growth rate of electricity generation. Because whether it's industrial growth or commercial growth, electricity is needed. But changes in the industrial structure now make this indicator inaccurate. Another indicator is total logistics volume. All these data are available now. So at least I know that, judging by total logistics volume, from 2014 to 2017 the economy grew about 4% per year on average. Though 2017 was indeed a bit higher, around 6.7%. These are alternative indicators, not very accurate either, but they give us a basis for judgment. And this data, it must be said, carries no suspicion of fabrication by local or central governments. So on average over these years, I think around 4.5% is more realistic.

### 2. Why Isn't the "Mixed-Ownership" SOE Reform Actually Reform?

**Q: In the long run, if China wants its economic growth to accelerate in the future, in what areas should improvements be made? Can you elaborate? I remember that in [*China's Two Economies*](http://www.ftchinese.com/story/001075356) you wrote: "Urbanization is a stable driver of China's medium- to long-term economic growth. Urbanization and the giant-country effect — these two factors will keep the Chinese economy growing at a fairly high rate. The new economy and the old economy will add to or subtract from it. Both government and ordinary people have the responsibility to advance the new economy and reform the old economy. New institutions will eventually replace old ones, but this process will not necessarily be fast."**

**S:** Right. China's advantages are the two points I mentioned: one is the so-called "giant-country effect," which can sustain a long-term advantage in international trade. The other is urbanization, which is fairly stable. But how things actually turn out depends on the development of the new economy and the reform of the old economy. Judging from recent years, reform of the old economy has been very slow — or rather, there has basically been no reform at all. By old economy I mean state-owned enterprises. The current SOE reform measures, in my view, involve almost no reform whatsoever. First, establishing party committees to make corporate decisions — that is definitely wrong. For an economic organization to use the decision-making mechanism of a political organization is definitely wrong. Second, mixed ownership — this is essentially a non-market method; it should be said it is arranged and ordered by the government, not the result of these enterprises' own voluntary negotiations. So in this sense, SOE reform has basically not been reform.

Only in individual places has there been a bit of reform. For example, crude oil imports: the two oil majors' monopoly on imports was broken, and last year roughly ⅓ of crude oil was imported by private companies. This is meaningful, but how meaningful? For instance, these private companies import crude and refine it — their costs are lower — but they cannot set the price themselves. They cannot compete with the "two oil majors" on price. So that doesn't mean much. There may also be more refined oil products exported abroad. Whether this can push forward market-based reform of China's oil or refined oil market remains a question. As long as SOEs go unreformed and run real losses every year, they will drag down economic growth.

### 3. Why Does "Rural Collective Land Entering the Market" Lead to Problems Like Those in Xihongmen Township?

**S:** On the land system, we believe the real reform is bringing rural collective land into the market — truly into the market. By "truly entering the market" I mean entering the land market for urbanization. If land is only subcontracted among villagers, that's market behavior but not very meaningful, because the land's use doesn't change. The most important thing is that farmers have the right to change the use of the land. Mainly it's the suburban land around cities: rural collective land should be able to be sold directly, negotiated directly with developers, or built on by the villagers themselves or in cooperation with others. But although there are pilots for collective land entering the market, there are many restrictions. One restriction is that the land may only be used for industrial purposes. And looking closely, industrial land is generally low-priced land. Local governments' strategy is to price industrial land low but commercial and residential land high. The logic: local governments use low prices to attract corporate investment; once there's investment, people have to live there, there will be more workers, more consumption, more purchases. Then, with the other hand, commercial land and residential land is priced high. The current reform lets rural collectives into the industrial land market without truly obtaining the benefits of urbanization, so it's not very meaningful.

There's another point: the definition of the rural collective. Take Xihongmen township in Beijing's Daxing District — everyone knows the clearing of the so-called "** population" happened there. But one of its identities is: it is Beijing's only pilot site for rural collective land entering the market. Why did this kind of thing happen there? Why did they push so hard? Because Xihongmen township wanted to get its hands on this land. Passing off the township as a rural collective — that is not a genuine rural collective. Rural collectives should be based on the village as the unit, the villagers' small group as the unit. The township government wanted to borrow such a policy to line its own pockets. It seized this right from the villages, then entered the market to sell the land and pocket the money — that's why it was so motivated to drive people away.

Rural collective land entering the market is right in its general direction, but the specific details are very problematic. So how much meaning it has remains to be seen. Reforms like this are tinkering — they can't accomplish much. They're nothing like the reforms of the 1980s and 1990s, which were bold and sweeping. Now there are many interest groups that actually don't want reform, which is why it is like this. But reform can't not happen, so they make a show of reforming. In this sense, because the land system isn't reformed and SOEs aren't reformed, our economy's growth potential cannot be realized.

### 4. If Rural Collectives Had the Right to Dispose of Their Land, What Might Happen?

**Q: You just mentioned two very important aspects for driving China's economic growth: SOE reform and land system reform. Have you calculated what impact these two reforms, if implemented, would have on China's economic growth?**

**S:** I've actually written articles on all of this — last year and the year before. For example, our estimate is that in 2013 the return on net assets of China's industrial state-owned enterprises was -3.8%, and their net assets were then about 52 trillion yuan. If the return on net assets rose to 6.2%, profits would equal 8.7% of GDP. Conversely, that means SOEs drag down China's economic growth by about 8.7%.

Land system reform: for example, if rural collectives in city suburbs had full rights to dispose of their land, they would change the land's use. For instance, instead of farming, they would build houses — villas or whatever — then rent or sell them to city people. And not only the suburbs; all villages with tourism value could do this. If that happened, it would raise GDP growth by at least one percentage point. Let me give a concrete small example: Ergun, at the northernmost tip of Inner Mongolia, has a "Russian village." A family running tourism there can earn 40,000 to 50,000 yuan a year; farming would earn only 4,000 to 5,000. A tenfold gap — a thousand percent growth. There are many such places; as long as one in a thousand rural places is like this, there can be very large growth — the one percent growth I just mentioned. That is possible.

### 5. Why Is It Wrong to Say Growth Will Slow or Stagnate After "Industrialization Is Complete"?

**Q: Some economists believe that after 2012, the slowdown in China's economic growth was a normal phenomenon, because China has completed industrialization and can no longer maintain such a high growth rate. What do you think?**

**S:** Actually, no. It's not that after industrialization there is no new technological stage — the Internet age, for example. Of course the Internet didn't add much growth at first, but in the past couple of years e-retail alone has contributed about 1% to annual growth.

And there's another point I mentioned: urbanization is not complete. Investment in urbanization is much larger than investment in industrialization. Many people think investment means industry. Not so. A Nobel laureate named William Arthur Lewis said that modern economic development consists of two things: industrialization and urbanization. Urbanization is the truly lasting driver of modern economic development. Why? Because urbanization brings enormous demand for investment in urban infrastructure. The volume is immense — about 2.5 trillion yuan in China in 2015. From now on, at least 3 trillion or 4 trillion yuan of investment per year, maybe even more. I have a rough estimate that over the next 20 years, about 117 trillion yuan of urbanization investment will be needed. And then there's housing. What does urbanization mean? Rural people moving into cities. They need places to live, so you have to build housing. Real estate is another huge chunk. Also, when rural people move into cities, their incomes rise above what they had in the countryside, because they have changed industries. Their lifestyles differ from rural ones. In the countryside, many things don't need to be bought; in the city, everything needs buying — even things commonly used in the city — and buying is convenient, because the supermarket is right there in the city, whereas in the countryside you have to travel far. This is the momentum urbanization itself brings. And China's urbanization rate is now about 56%; if the target is 80%, there is still a long way to go. So the "industrialization is complete" argument fails to see the urbanization component and fails to see the Internet economy rising, the new technologies rising.

### 6. Why Should the Government Not Dictate a City's Total Population?

**Q: My next two questions are about urbanization. Since 2017, big cities like Beijing and Shanghai have been carrying out urban renovations and implementing population-control public policies — for example, Beijing's eviction of the ** population, and Shanghai saying it will keep its population within 25 million. How do you feel about this? What do you think of these public policies?**

**S:** The people who make these policies don't understand what a city is. They think a city is a goal set by a government according to its own understanding of the city. Actually, no — a city is the result of the market. A city's most important characteristic is the agglomeration of population. Why do people come to the city? Because once they agglomerate, they obtain what we call "market network externalities." What does that mean? Once we are agglomerated, the possibility of trading with each other arises. Suppose we're all in the countryside, with 300 meters between each person — then there is no possibility of trading at all, because we're too far apart. But if the distance between people is short, the possibility of trading increases. So the meaning of a city is that people agglomerate, and agglomeration brings market network externalities. Once trade happens, it creates a surplus from trade. Trade creates value. With trade, the market can give prices to goods, and price is a signal telling you the degree of market demand for each kind of good. Then we decide whether to produce such products based on that price. First there must be a market; the market brings network externalities and increased trade; through actual trading, prices emerge. Prices tell you that producing such a product is profitable. That brings industries to the area around the city. It's this chain of logic.

So why do people come to cities? Shanghai, for example, has over 24 million people — they come for a reason. They definitely come to make money; without money they certainly wouldn't come. So if the government says "I'm going to stipulate a population number," based on what? It can say it is based on water resources. Well, Shanghai doesn't have that problem, because Shanghai sits at the mouth of the Yangtze. As for Beijing, [didn't Lu Ming immediately produce data last time](http://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/psc/ACEMPRD/EMPLOYEE/CRM/s/WEBLIB_SPE_ISCT.TZ_SETSPE_ISCRIPT.FieldFormula.IScript_viewArtHTML1?TZ_ART_ID=33109) showing that Beijing is not short of water? People make cost-benefit calculations. If resources all have reasonable prices, a person will calculate whether coming to a big city is worth it. Why should the government dictate a city's population size? This is entirely planned-economy thinking. Utterly unreasonable. Among countries around the world, it is rare for a government to dictate a city's population size. For instance, the Tokyo metropolitan area has a population of 57 million.

### 7. Two Million Four Hundred Thousand People May Come to Beijing Every Day Just to See a Doctor — What Can the Government Use to Adjust the Problem of Population Agglomeration?

So Shanghai's population cap of 25 million has little justification. Beijing has such a target too — for example, not exceeding 28 million people. Beijing's target has a more complicated aspect. Because Beijing is the seat of the central government, and a large share of the central government's tax revenue has been invested in Beijing's municipal construction. This means Beijing, as a city, didn't itself create that much wealth, yet resources are disproportionately, excessively poured into Beijing's infrastructure and public services — including health care, education, and so on. Then these better facilities, these better medical and educational resources, attract more outsiders. It is not balanced. It is said that 700,000 people come to Beijing every day to see doctors. Add the people accompanying them, and it's 2.4 million. And the people in Beijing who serve these visitors add another seven or eight million. Every day, like this. Why? Because Beijing's medical resources are too good. If medical resources were distributed more equitably, Beijing would not be flooded with so many people every day. So the problem of Beijing's oversized city is itself a problem of China's political structure. Under this political structure, the government has even more power to allocate resources directly, and it allocates more resources to Beijing. If people could move freely, they would inevitably flow to Beijing. This needs reflection. So if you really want to reduce the population, you should distribute resources fairly and reduce the so-called "high-end" population. Of course, we don't consider them high-end — those people really have no creativity and contribute nothing to society; much of what they do is negative.

Of course, on another point: Beijing can be big — no problem — but under relatively reasonable conditions. We're not saying the bigger Beijing is, the better; that's not what we mean. But you must use fiscal resources very fairly; you cannot put more resources in Beijing. Why does this happen? Very simple: because the decision-makers are Beijingers. That's the logic. If you let the government allocate resources, government officials will inevitably have a bias in allocating them — good resources get allocated close to themselves. Very simple logic. So practices like this are definitely wrong.

### 8. Has the Demographic Dividend Disappeared?

**Q: There's also China's demographic dividend. Do you think China's demographic dividend has disappeared? Why?**

**S:** [Cai Fang could probably answer this one better](http://oa.jjxdt.org/UploadFile/20150323002/2016-08-22/Issue/vgghfzkf.pdf) — he specializes in this question. According to them, it has actually already disappeared. Because the demographic dividend is dynamic: today two people support one person, tomorrow three people support one. That's how it was at the start of the family planning policy. Why? Because the population was large — originally a family might have three children, the couple earned the money, two people supporting three. Later, family planning allowed only two children, and later still only one. If only one child is allowed, then two people support one person. That's the surplus. But look at it now, and it's different. Because this so-called family planning has been in effect for many years, and those only children have grown up facing four elderly people — it becomes this problem. It is actually already negative. For China this is an extremely heavy burden — having to support more people — so the demographic dividend no longer exists.

### 9. What Is Worth Vigilance: Why Is the Northeast's Problem a Problem of SOEs and SOE Culture?

**Q: From an economics perspective, do you have any thoughts on the Northeast's current problems?**

**S:** The Northeast's problem — one problem is the institutional problem everyone criticizes. First, in the Northeast, property rights are not effectively protected. This is the most important problem. We have heard many cases from the Northeast: an enterprise grows, a government official takes a fancy to it, and says to the entrepreneur, "How about giving me half the equity?" The entrepreneur says, "Why should I give you any?" Then the official says, "Oh, you won't give it to me?" — and has him thrown in prison, sentenced to death. I remember a case in Heilongjiang like this. There are especially many such examples. It's the same principle: because administrative power is unconstrained, whatever an official does meets no countervailing force. The official says this entrepreneur is guilty, and administrative power directly controls the judiciary, controls the courts. The court says you're a mafia boss, you're guilty of illegal fundraising — they can always find plenty of charges. And China has no tradition of strictly following judicial procedure. Then you're in real trouble. He can convict you of whatever he wants. Suppose you run an enterprise to make money, and all the other policies look fine — tax exemptions, cheap land — none of it is of any use. Why? Because in the end, if you make money and it gets seized, your calculation comes out negative. Not only negative — you can lose your life too.

Of course, on the other hand, the Northeast has an excessive proportion of state-owned enterprises. On the one hand there is the problem of SOEs themselves — low efficiency, running losses. On the other hand, they bring an SOE culture, which is especially bad. It doesn't want to strive to compete in the market and earn income. Instead it wants — whether with the provincial government or the central government — to bargain for a bigger share of the distribution, to cut a bigger slice of the cake; it doesn't want to bake the cake. This mentality is especially serious. The year before last I saw a news item about some SOE reform in the Northeast. Before any reform was even visible, the central government had first set up a "Northeast Industrial Revitalization Fund." I said, the Northeasterners are really something. Before any reform, they first got the money out of the central government. This culture is especially bad. But in the end, you have to rely on yourself to create wealth. So the Northeast's excessive SOE weight produces an SOE culture that leads people not to strive to compete in the market and earn income, but to ask the government for it — to carve out a bigger piece from existing wealth.

### 10. The Positive Way to Understand the Northeast Economy: Why Is Population Loss Understandable?

**S:** Of course there's another view: since the Northeast is part of the whole country, the Northeast failing to develop doesn't mean Northeasterners aren't getting rich. Because the Northeasterners have all moved inland beyond the pass. Look at the Northeast: growth is slow because people have left. So you can't look at it that way. For instance, a city originally had 10 million people, now it's down to 9 million, growth is 2% — that is still growth; GDP per capita grew. This is a kind of development for Northeasterners — they are actually making money; you can't simply say the Northeast is such-and-such. Because if you look at development, not every place grows every year. Many inland counties grow very slowly. Why? Because people are leaving every year. So if you analyze carefully, you can't say these counties are wrong. Why? Because these counties are inland, with no particular geographic advantages. Why don't people go to the coast? Why not the east? Why not the big cities? So we see many inland counties where the population is simply declining — so you can't entirely say a place isn't developing. How can an inland county compare with Shanghai? No comparison. It doesn't attract me. But are inland county officials necessarily worse than Shanghai officials? Not necessarily.

**Q: In some places, GDP per capita actually rose — possibly even higher than the national average growth rate.**

**S:** Right. Of course, it doesn't mean the central or western regions can't develop — they do develop. Places like Baotou in Inner Mongolia and Yinchuan in Ningxia are developing fast.

### 11. Why Does the Foreign Exchange Problem Come Down to the Safety of Private Entrepreneurs?

**Q: How do you view China's current foreign exchange problem?**

**S:** Because over the past two years large amounts of capital have been flowing out, especially private enterprises investing abroad, while domestic investment growth has been very slow. This is a question of the security of domestic private enterprises' assets — whether property rights can be protected. We have seen many egregious cases: very large asset holdings, but they can be seized by administrative power, and even these people's own freedom and lives cannot be guaranteed. So this may be an especially big problem. Chinese private entrepreneurs feel unsafe, so they have to invest abroad. Over the past two years we have also done some surveys of private entrepreneurs, in Zhejiang and Guangdong, and a considerable proportion of entrepreneurs said they have plans to emigrate overseas. Because no one knows what China's future institutions will be like, whether the basic principles of reform and opening-up can be upheld. Maybe they can't be upheld. Recently someone even wrote on an official Weibo account something about "eliminating private ownership," and someone else said, not now — you have to fatten the pig before slaughtering it. These statements are terrifying. Although the central government is aware of this too.

**Q: Right — some documents and court verdicts have come out recently.**

**S:** Right. Last year the central government issued a document on the equal protection of property rights and made some efforts. Some cases received relatively fair verdicts — for instance, the Kaiqilai case a while back, in which the Kaiqilai company won. Kaiqilai is a private enterprise; across from it was actually a state institution backed by the Shaanxi provincial government, but in the end Kaiqilai won. These are all important signals that the government still wants to protect property rights. But the big question is: is protecting property rights an expedient, or is it a basic principle being followed? Our economy is now slowing; so many private enterprises are investing overseas, and domestic private investment growth is very low — the year before last it grew 2%, extremely low. Yet private enterprises are the main engine of China's economic growth and employment growth. I once gave figures: private enterprises account for more than 90% of GDP growth, and 100% of new employment — and that has been the case for over ten years.

So this problem is fairly serious. If private investment growth comes down, what happens in the future? That is the question. And it must be solved at the root: property rights must be protected in principle — protecting property rights must be made a genuine constitutional principle, not an expedient, not a policy. In the past it was always called policy, and policy can change. This should be a constitutional principle: property rights are protected at all times. This is the most fundamental thing — truly protecting private enterprises, truly restricting those administrative officials who use power to expropriate private entrepreneurs. If you can do that, people's minds will be at ease. These past two years, restrictions on the so-called capital outflow have brought inconveniences, but on the whole it hasn't reached an extreme situation. For example, you can still remit up to $100,000 a year. This limit is still acceptable to ordinary people. If your child is studying overseas, tuition can be paid. But if you want to buy a luxury home, that money is probably restricted. Making big investments is even harder. This approach is definitely not good — it puts the cart before the horse. The root is safety: property safety and personal safety. If you can't deliver that, then people will run.

### 12. How Does a "Good" Institutional Design Like Medical Insurance Distort Prices and Aggravate the Supply Shortage?

**Q: This year is the eighth year since the new health care reform plan formally took effect. Looking back on these eight years of health care reform, what are your reflections? What problems remain? In what areas should future efforts be pushed forward?**

**S:** Last year we produced a complete research report on China's health care system, titled [*Theoretical Research, Effect Evaluation, and Reform Recommendations on China's Health Care System*](http://www.unirule.cloud/xiazai/2017/20170711.pdf). This system has many problems. One problem: the reform led to full coverage by the medical insurance system. In fact, China's medical insurance coverage exceeds 100% — some people probably have overlapping coverage. Over 100% coverage looks like a good thing, but from an economics standpoint it has very serious problems. Once a person has medical insurance, once he gets sick, 75% can be reimbursed — this actually distorts his behavior. Originally every cent had to be carefully budgeted, but now it doesn't need to be — why? Because for every cent spent, 75% is reimbursed. Although the 75% reimbursed is in fact money paid when buying the insurance in advance. Of course many people are in enterprises, where the enterprise withholds it from wages and contributes itself — but that's all your money, just a bit more obscure. Either way it's a sunk cost. So you may buy more, or buy more expensive — a phenomenon occurring in most people at the same time. Our research reached a conclusion: the more you get reimbursed, the more prices rise. You don't benefit at all. For example, with 75% reimbursement, your out-of-pocket share is only 1/4. What happens to the price? The price becomes four times the original. Say it used to be 10 yuan, now it's 40. Do the math: originally, with 100% paid out of pocket, you paid 10 yuan. Now it's different — now 75% is reimbursed, very nice, but the price is 40 yuan. How much did you spend? You still spent 10 yuan. But actually the 30 yuan reimbursed was also spent by you — so you spent more. Why is seeing a doctor expensive? This is the problem.

### 13. Doctors' Low Incomes — Why Did They Ultimately Throw the Entire Pharmaceutical Market's Price System Into Chaos?

**S:** Also, under this kind of system, everyone complains about how hard it is to see a doctor. Originally, to solve the problem of expensive care, the first move was to suppress doctors' incomes — but this is problematic. If doctors earn too little, not many people will want to become doctors; but if you say doctors can take red envelopes, taking red envelopes gets you arrested. So doctors' incomes in China are relatively low. Compared with other countries — for example, in the US a doctor earns 5.7 times the average; in China it's 1.56 times. Because being a doctor is complex work, a technical profession — a doctor's income should be several times the average. But in China it's on the low side. The result: the growth in the number of doctors and nurses in China lags far behind the demand for medical care. From 1980 to 2014, China's demand for medicine and health care, after stripping out price rises and income growth, grew 40-fold, while the number of doctors grew only 1.5-fold. That's why seeing a doctor is hard — queuing. Add to that what I just said: medical resources are distributed unreasonably, concentrated in big cities like Beijing and Shanghai. Why is it hard to see a doctor? Getting to Beijing is hard; queuing in Beijing is even harder. So these problems are all very serious. We haven't taken a positive view of the so-called new health care reform; emphasizing the so-called government role is even more wrong. The stronger the government, the more unreasonable the allocation of medical resources. Definitely so. So reform is needed.

### 14. Can a Market-Based Solution — Lifting the Controls on Doctors' Incomes — Solve the Pharmaceutical Market's Problems?

**Q: Do you have some concrete solutions? Marketization?**

**S:** Of course — we want to emphasize marketization. For instance, for minor illnesses and outpatient care, there is no need for medical insurance, because everyone's income can afford it. Nowadays outpatient care reimbursement is a game; it has none of the original purpose of medical insurance, which was to help when you get a major illness and cannot pay. Helping when a family's finances are about to be bankrupted by a major illness — that's where it has meaning. Minor illnesses don't need it. But now the entire medical insurance system is completely inverted: most people spend their days scheming about how to get reimbursed for minor illnesses — it's a reimbursement game, an enormous waste of resources. So I say minor illnesses don't need insurance, and for major illnesses patients should bear a larger share themselves — say 70%. That is, greatly reduce the insurance reimbursement share: 70% out-of-pocket for major illnesses. If that's still unaffordable — say it exceeds 40% of that year's income — that's beyond what medical insurance can handle. In our proposal, a national fund for catastrophic and serious-illness relief should be established: if medical expenses in a year exceed 40% of income, a government fund should provide relief. We estimate this fund would spend around 200 billion yuan a year — a relatively small figure.

We must also lift the controls on doctors' incomes. Don't meddle with doctors' incomes. Only when doctors' incomes are higher can more people be attracted to the profession. Only with more doctors can there be more supply. The next step is to liberalize drug prices. Why were drug prices originally controlled? Because doctors' incomes were restricted, the overwhelming majority of hospitals used drug sales to subsidize medical services. Doctors' incomes weren't enough, so drug prices were marked up, and a share of the drug price was kicked back to doctors as income. If doctors' incomes were determined by the market, drug prices could be liberalized.

### 15. How to View the 40 Years of Reform and Opening-Up? Interest-Group Monopolies and the "Death Tax Rate" Problem.

**Q: 2018 is the 40th anniversary of China's reform and opening-up. Looking back on these 40 years, what are your biggest reflections?**

**S:** This topic is too big. Simply put, these 40 years can be divided into two parts: the first 20 years and the last 20 years. The first 20 years were basically a process of reform surging forward. "Reform surging forward" means that economically it developed in the direction of marketization, and in thought and culture there was continuous emancipation, a continuous lifting of the suppression that cultural despotism had imposed on the heads of Chinese people. There was also opening to the outside world economically, as well as opening in thought and culture — exchanges with foreign countries in literature, theory, and textbooks, and academic exchanges. For a long time, there were no obstacles. We used to hold international conferences casually — just held them. Now it is probably hard to imagine my holding an international conference here. These were all features of the first 20 years.

But in the last 20 years, as wealth kept pouring forth, interest groups indeed discovered how to cut themselves a bigger slice of the wealth ceaselessly generated by market-oriented reform. And the methods for cutting a bigger slice: the first is monopoly. PetroChina and Sinopec — both groups established in the late 1990s — this was the watershed. They monopolized every aspect of oil, from upstream to downstream, from domestic to foreign — a complete monopoly. Monopoly lets them take a bigger slice: because it is upstream, the monopoly price of oil or refined products amounts to extracting an extra share of wealth from all enterprises and consumers. We have an estimate: the two oil majors' monopoly price for refined oil is 31% higher than the average level of major countries. We have research on all of this — [SOE research](http://unirule.cloud/index.php?c=article&id=4017), [monopoly research](http://unirule.cloud/xiazai/2014/20140822.pdf) — there are reports. We see very clearly that they have grown highly skilled at how to obtain monopoly profits, how to extract a larger share from them. Why are banks so reluctant to open up market competition, so unwilling to let private banks in? This is exactly the reason: monopoly.

Then there is the other piece: the tax rate. Our research, and some articles — I don't know if you've read them?

**Q: "The death tax rate"** (note: first proposed by the scholar Li Weiguang. He believes China taxes too heavily — a 40% tax burden means death for Chinese enterprises, so it can also be called the "death tax rate." A controversy erupted over this at the end of 2016.)

**S:** Right. I also have an article called ["Unchecked Fiscal Spending Is the Main Driver of the Rise in the Macro Tax Rate"](http://www.ftchinese.com/story/001071259). From 1996 to 2015, general public fiscal revenue as a share of GDP rose 12 percentage points. That is obvious. Actually the 1980s–1990s were a low-tax era; afterwards, tax rates kept rising, taking a bigger share from enterprises and residents. This formed a self-aware interest group. They are very politically powerful, so they now confront the rest of society, wanting to take a bigger share and to protect more of their share. So the last 20 years have had this problem — to some degree it is counter-reform. But they also know they can't go too far, can't kill the market. Because if the market is killed, there is no place to take another share from. They want private enterprises to produce; after the production is done, they take out the bigger part through monopoly and taxation. So that is the whole picture of the 40 years.

### 16. Striving in an Imperfect Society — What Should Young People Do?

**Q: Many people feel this era is not so good. In this era, what can each person do?**

**S:** The phrase "this era is not so good" could be said in any era. Some people don't quite understand; they feel "I was unlucky to be born in this era." Actually, every era has its problems. So the problem of "this era is not so good" doesn't exist. Every era has problems — and that is actually our joy. What does that mean? Human beings are born into an imperfect society. So the most interesting and most enjoyable thing a person can do in his lifetime is, through his own efforts, to change society — even if only by a tiny bit. I think this is what everyone should do. Because people now indeed have a misconception: "Why was I born in China and not in the US?" This is a huge misconception. You think the US has no problems? The US is full of problems. Of course the US may now be a bit better than China, but that is because generation after generation of Americans kept striving, improving things bit by bit. Read American history and you can see this. People imagine that America had a few genius founding fathers who wrote a constitution that was then immediately obeyed and enforced, and everyone lived happily ever after. That is simply not the fact. America's today was won by the blood, sweat, and even lives of many generations of Americans.

So wherever you're born is the same — wherever you're born, you should be a person of that place and do that place's work. China indeed has many problems, but China indeed also has many good things and many good traditions. You certainly live in an imperfect society, but it is precisely because you live in an imperfect society that you have things to do. We must strive to change it.

Confucius had a saying: "When the Way prevails under Heaven, show yourself; when it does not, go into hiding." When the Way prevails under Heaven, only then do I come out to work; when the Way does not prevail under Heaven, I live in seclusion. That is what he said to others. What he said to himself was: "If the Way prevailed under Heaven, I would not be trying to change things." What does that mean? Even if the Way prevailed under Heaven, I, Kong Qiu, would not switch — I would stay right here. He lived in the Spring and Autumn period, with rites collapsed and music in ruin, years of unending war — the Way did not prevail under Heaven. But for him: even a place where the Way prevailed under Heaven, I would not go; I stay right here, working hard where the Way does not prevail under Heaven — this is called "I would not switch." That is: I just live in this imperfect, incomplete society, make some efforts, and change this society.

So I think the so-called "this era is not so good" is never an excuse for complaint or escape. Think of it this way — actually, the joy comes from exactly this: you are to strive amid imperfection and incompleteness. It is the same as what Unirule does. Why has Unirule survived to this day? I say it is very simple. We have been through so many tribulations — too many — but every time we accomplish something, we are happy; we have a sense of achievement.
