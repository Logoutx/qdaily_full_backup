Benxi, a resource-exhausted city, is no isolated case.

In Benxi, Liaoning, about 600 meters from the busiest commercial district, there is a park. From morning to night it is occupied by the grandparent generation — people singing, dancing, playing cards, playing mahjong, and hauling calligraphy brushes half as tall as a person to write characters on the pavement. Voices and music hum together into a racket you can hear from far away.

In the early 1980s, the Benxi municipal government rebuilt a run-down neighborhood park, installing slides, a merry-go-round, and a "space shuttle" ride, and named it Children's Park. Nearly 40 years later, the original rides survive only in old photographs. Benxi people now say this is no children's park — it's clearly a grandpas' park, or a seniors' park.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180517184951Yqz2SV4MjnpfrN3d.jpeg-w600)

Benxi is aging fast. In 2016, [a report from the Benxi Municipal Development and Reform Commission showed](http://www.bxdrc.gov.cn/ndrc/content.aspx?i=8d1d0082-b2da-41c8-94ce-27f4a3b3021a) that the number of primary school students per 100,000 people had fallen from 5,200 in 2006 to 3,600 in 2013, while the number of middle school students per 100,000 had dropped from 6,400 to 4,200. [The share of people over 60 had soared from less than 10% to 23.6% in 2016.](http://www.benxi.gov.cn/News.asp?ID=2055949)

The scene at Children's Park is, by and large, a miniature of the whole city. With 1.1 million urban residents still living in the 40-square-kilometer main urban area, Benxi's enormous population base keeps the city looking busy, at least on the surface — sparing it the fate of American Rust Belt cities after heavy industry declined.

But beneath the hustle and bustle, Benxi, where the elderly have become the main population, has turned listless and gray. Buildings left half-finished and abandoned are not unusual. Many street-facing shops have hung up signs saying "closed" or "for transfer." A hotpot-and-barbecue restaurant with a fancy facade has a past-due notice from the water company taped to it. Even in Yongfeng Pedestrian Street, the busiest commercial area, a single turn of a corner reveals a broad expanse of rubble from demolished brick buildings.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180517185101Bt0zKbi8c4To3ajs.jpeg-w600)

For the city's desolation, real estate agent Zhang Xin has the most direct vantage point.

Zhang Xin runs a small realty office in southern Benxi, not far from the Wanda Plaza under construction. Ten years ago, Zhang Xin was a fruit merchant. The fruit business was tough, so he spent 1,500 yuan to get his real estate agent's license. A decade on, he has found that the property business has collapsed too: "Sometimes this storefront doesn't close a single deal in a month; sometimes it closes two or three."

Benxi's housing sales area began to slide sharply in 2013. Five years ago, [Benxi's commodity housing sales area was still 5.271 million square meters](http://www.benxi.gov.cn/News.asp?ID=2024787); by 2016 [only 728,000 square meters remained](http://www.tjcn.org/tjgb/06ln/35173.html). In 2017, [floor space under construction in Benxi was 4.239 million square meters](http://www.benxi.gov.cn/News.asp?ID=2056544), down 25.7% from the previous year.

Unlike real estate agents elsewhere, Zhang Xin's shop covers a wide range of business: besides buying and selling homes, it also does advance payments, payday-advance loans against salary cards, and other work closer to finance. By and large, Benxi's realty offices all cram their signs full of their business scope. "You can't survive doing property alone — there's just not enough volume."

The people coming in to ask are numerous enough. In under twenty minutes, Zhang Xin received two women who looked to be in their fifties. One had come to find a storefront for a friend who wanted to start a business. Zhang Xin recommended a shop near Wanda. "Once Wanda opens and draws people over, this place will get busy." Hard to say whether this was just Zhang Xin's sales pitch. After seeing the client out, Zhang Xin pointed at the row of storefronts outside the window: "Restaurants have been folding these past two years. The ones still hiring are all old establishments. Very few newly opened shops make it now."

The other woman had come to list her own apartment. It was a 30-plus-square-meter unit facing west. The owner had specifically told Zhang Xin this was a school-district apartment and quoted an asking price of 80,000 yuan. Zhang Xin logged all the information, item by item. Closing the notebook and watching the owner walk off into the distance, he grumbled: "Her place — 60,000 yuan, and I could sell it. School-district apartments are cheap now. There are no kids left."

By Zhang Xin's account, Benxi's secondhand home prices have been falling for years. "Mainly there's no inflow of outside people, the migrant population is small, the total population is shrinking, but housing keeps growing."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805171852314KxB7UQaeIHF3dO8.jpeg-w600)

Benxi's population decline has already lasted more than a decade. From 2000 to 2016, Benxi's registered (hukou) population gradually shrank from 1.571 million to 1.499 million. Among all age groups, the 18-to-35 cohort saw the steepest decline, falling from 362,000 in 2010 to 309,000 in 2016.

Benxi has thus fallen into a vicious cycle. The shrinking population means no consumption demand, and the city begins to contract. And as the city contracts, it loses its appeal to young people. [As 24-year-old Liu Yuan put it, "Subconsciously I just feel I should work away from home, that I shouldn't come back."](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/53016.html)

Benxi cannot offer young people what they want. Benxi's commercial districts were all built along the train station, encompassing four shopping malls within a short distance of one another. Apart from Ginzu Shopping Plaza, which has some foreign-trade shops selling clothing wholesale-imported from Korea, the rest — Hualian Department Store, Benxi Commercial Building, and Hualian again — are all much of a muchness. By comparison, Benxi people much prefer going straight to Shenyang to shop, where the brands are more complete and there is more to buy.

Liu Yuan finds Benxi's entertainment options "extremely dull": "Eating, watching movies, shopping, strolling the streets, sightseeing — there's just nothing to do." Because she loves animated films like *Big Hero 6* and *Frozen*, she wants to go to Shanghai Disneyland. "Bowling, archery... if you want to try anything like that, you needn't even think about it in Benxi. Even if Benxi had it, it would definitely be some low-spec version."

It is not that Benxi's businesspeople are unwilling to offer these services — it is that Benxi cannot sustain such a market, especially over the past three or four years.

Tang Lin runs a chuanchuan (skewer) shop on Yongfeng Pedestrian Street. The shop is small: five or six dining tables on the left, and three big freezers full of skewers on the right. Tang Lin works the register herself and also personally cooks side dishes in the kitchen. Working alongside her in the kitchen are her mother and her mother-in-law.

When the shop first opened a year ago, because Benxi had never had chuanchuan before, people came for the novelty and lines often stretched out the door. Recently, even though winter has just passed and people have endured the bitter northeast cold and started wandering the streets again, their novelty-seeking has largely worn off, and business has gone quiet.

Tang Lin has been fairly satisfied with the year's business, so she sold off the Sichuan restaurant she used to run to focus on chuanchuan. But she knows the coming year absolutely won't be as good. Now she is trying every way she can to find more revenue streams for the shop — for example, joining Meituan's food-delivery service, or launching new hotpot-style dishes.

"If it's a food item Benxi doesn't have yet, it should be hot for a while. It depends on your average spend per customer. If it's around ten-plus yuan, it should be fine; anything a bit pricier does poorly," Tang Lin said, lamenting how hard business has become — far worse than seven or eight years ago.

Wang Li estimates that if, seven or eight years ago, opening a snack shop could earn 200,000 yuan a year, that number now might be only 100,000 yuan. "The drop-off has to be that big." She works as a sales assistant at an outdoor-gear store in Benxi, selling clothing bearing logos of brands like Arc'teryx and Under Armour. The sign outside the store reads: "Direct monthly shipments from Shenzhen."

Asked about her daily sales, Wang Li found the question hard to answer. Not because it was inconvenient to disclose, but because the fluctuations were too large to pin down a figure. "Sometimes it's perfectly normal to go many days without a single sale. Customer traffic and spending are both worse than before," she said. "Everything is bad overall. Running a shop is just about maintaining a normal life."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180517185434Py7E6Igrs5U84zTL.jpeg-w600)

"Benxi is a resource-based city. Its original three pillars were steel, coal, and cement. But these past few years have gone very badly, and all the drawbacks of state-owned enterprises have come to the surface. Most industries have withered." Zhu Rui, 62 this year, worked for decades at a local bank, and the various loan projects he dealt with gave him his own understanding of Benxi's predicament in recent years. "Liaoning used to be called the eldest son of the Republic and contributed a lot to the country. Its decline these past few years — my view is that the state-owned economy's share is too heavy. Of course it's a system problem."

On the matter of system problems, Zhu Rui's wife, 61-year-old Wang Xia, may have more say. Five or six years ago, she retired from a state-owned enterprise in Benxi, only to be rehired to keep working. Recently, that enterprise has finally gone through the entire bankruptcy process, and Wang Xia is about to face true retirement.

Wang Xia started working in 1980. Under the job-allocation system of the time, she was originally assigned to an embroidery factory, and later transferred to the electronics industry. "Back then, for a girl to start work in electronics — pretty, white lab coat, slippers. The work was all done wearing little white gloves. How pretty — and it wasn't easy to get in," Wang Xia said. "But later on, after reform and opening-up, it was finished."

In the planned-economy era, talent, markets, and capital were all strictly controlled. Wang Xia recalls that the technicians working in her company had all come from Tsinghua University, which guaranteed product quality.

After reform and opening-up, technical talent ran south to earn more money. Her company consequently suffered a break in its talent pipeline. On top of that, big companies in the south produced the same products at relatively low prices, and the entire enterprise gradually fell into difficulty. "Before, at the very least we still had some profit — at the very least wages weren't owed, and no expenses were owed. Then bit by bit it started owing pension insurance, owing medical insurance."

Wang Xia once had the chance to work at a credit cooperative. That was around the late 1980s and early 1990s. "Back then credit cooperatives were no different from enterprises. They earned 38 yuan a month, and our enterprise earned 38.6 yuan a month, so I didn't switch. Later on, with reform and opening-up, everything came alive, their incomes rose, and our enterprise was done for. I was getting old too, so forget it — I'd just keep at it."

Compared with Wang Xia, Zhu Rui has been far luckier. To this day, China's banking industry remains a monopoly of state-owned enterprises and faces no excessive external competition. Add to that the severe asset bubbles China has seen in recent years, from which banks as financial institutions directly benefited — all of this has made banking work worth its weight in gold.

Because he works at a bank, Zhu Rui's income is now roughly double Wang Xia's. Wang Xia can't help sounding resentful: "My salary used to be a few mao more than his." Still, Wang Xia no longer needs to fret about it. The bankruptcy process will be completed soon, and after that there's no longer any question of wages being paid or not.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805171856158KcXpFP516LwM9kg.jpeg-w600)

System problems have dragged down the efficiency of state-owned enterprises. For a city like Benxi, this matters far more than a single company's bankruptcy and the loss of a few hundred jobs. When a state-owned enterprise becomes a city's pillar industry, it means the entire city runs around that enterprise.

For Benxi, that enterprise is Benxi Iron & Steel Group (BX Steel).

BX Steel's predecessor was the Benxihu Coal and Iron Company, founded locally by the Japanese. After the founding of the People's Republic, the Chinese government, eager to develop heavy industry, restructured it into Benxi Iron & Steel Group. From then on, the group remained Benxi's most important economic lifeline. [In 2007, BX Steel generated 11.06 billion yuan in industrial value-added](http://job.mysteel.com/position-company-m24440-p0.shtml); [that year Benxi's total GDP was 47.56 billion yuan](https://baike.baidu.com/item/2007%E5%B9%B4%E6%9C%AC%E6%BA%AA%E5%B8%82%E6%94%BF%E5%BA%9C%E5%B7%A5%E4%BD%9C%E6%8A%A5%E5%91%8A/13711510), of which industrial value-added above designated size was 19.36 billion yuan.

The more important set of figures: [in 2007, BX Steel's listed subsidiary Benxi Steel Plates paid 2.08 billion yuan in taxes](http://news.lnd.com.cn/htm/2008-04/09/content_130775.htm), while Benxi's general budget revenue that same year was 2.675 billion yuan. Without even accounting for tax rebates, BX Steel was practically the sole pillar keeping Benxi running.

Lu Ming joined BX Steel in 2009. He attended university in Inner Mongolia, where his major was related to steel production. At the start of his senior year, steel companies came to his school to recruit. At first Lu Ming wanted to go to Anshan Iron and Steel Group. Also located in Liaoning Province, Ansteel surpassed Benxi Steel in both efficiency and scale. Ansteel was hard to get into, so Lu Ming settled for second best and chose BX Steel.

BX Steel ended up recruiting more than 40 people from Lu Ming's school, and roughly 200-plus people came to the company from across the country. "It really felt like we'd been conned into coming," Lu Ming said, adding, "Of course that's half joke, half truth."

That was BX Steel's last good time. In the first half of 2008, before the financial crisis arrived, the national — indeed the global — economy was expanding rapidly. Steel, as a basic industry, was also in a booming phase. At the end of that same year, to safeguard economic growth, the state rolled out a 4-trillion-yuan investment plan; the steel industry benefited, capacity expanded further, and a kind of false prosperity took shape.

Chen Juan, 64 this year, originally worked for the Construction General Company under BX Steel. "Jianzong built factory buildings, boilers, and blast furnaces for BX Steel." In her memory, starting in 2009, peripheral companies of BX Steel like the Construction General Company had already begun to be roiled by instability. BX Steel was no longer expanding capacity: "There was no new work, and BX Steel stopped hiring."

Everything came quickly. First, BX Steel halted internal settlement. Originally, the Construction General Company took on projects from BX Steel's main steel-production plants, which paid a fee for the work; suddenly, the Construction General Company was informed that the main plants were no longer willing to pay that fee. On top of that, with the main plants no longer expanding, the Construction General Company had no new work to do.

"Early retirement buyouts are gone, and the heating subsidy hasn't been reimbursed for five years. A one-size-fits-all internal retirement — you just don't go to work anymore, and they give you a little money each year," Chen Juan raised her voice. "A factory of 2,000 people, with only 8 people left behind doing HR and finance work." The enormous Construction General Company collapsed just like that.

![Photo/BX Steel Group official website](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180517190820xsILrSXt74Q3NAoV.jpg-w600)

The consequences of saturated steel capacity soon spread to the main plants. Although Lu Ming's main plant held out several years longer than the Construction General Company, its performance was obviously sliding too. In 2007, Benxi Steel Plates' 2.08 billion yuan in taxes ranked third in Liaoning Province; but by 2013, [Benxi Steel Plates' tax payment had fallen to 823 million yuan](http://ln.qq.com/a/20140330/002742.htm), and its ranking had dropped to 27th. "Once steel prices fall, a ton of steel earns less than a ton of napa cabbage," said Chen Juan.

Starting in 2015, ordinary employees like Lu Ming finally felt the pressure. "When there were profits, they didn't raise our wages. When there were losses, they cut our wages." As performance kept declining, the pay cuts finally reached everyone. "It used to be about 3,500 to 3,600 yuan a month; now it's down to 2,500 to 2,600 — roughly a 1,000-yuan cut per grassroots employee, with managers taking bigger cuts."

Chen Juan said: "BX Steel wages used to be high. If both people in a family worked at BX Steel, we called it a 'double-steel family.' Their income was high, their benefits were high. Now double-steel families are miserable."

Clearly, those affected are not just the people working at BX Steel. In fact, [BX Steel's official figures say 80,000 people work there](http://www.bxsteel.com/a/gongsijieshao/), about 13% of Benxi's 604,000 urban employed population. Counting upstream and downstream suppliers as well, then by Zhu Rui's estimate, a quarter of the entire city's population depends on BX Steel for survival. They draw wages from BX Steel, then spend locally, ultimately propping up Benxi's local economic cycle. That is what it means for a company accounting for 25% of a city's GDP and nearly 80% of its fiscal revenue to belong to a city.

Before long, the impact of BX Steel's pay cuts and layoffs spread across the city. With poor performance in recent years, BX Steel tightened its financial management. Suppliers that had done business with BX Steel had to meet the price-cut demands it proposed. Combined with BX Steel greatly extending payment terms, suppliers' financial pressure kept mounting, eventually causing some companies' funding chains to break.

And for businesspeople like Tang Lin: "BX Steel wages used to be four to five thousand yuan a month; recently it was just over 2,000 yuan. When BX Steel goes down, Benxi's whole economic sector goes down, because it's the thing that drives Benxi's entire economy. It's only just now starting to warm up a bit."

Though the wording differs, businesspeople all subscribe to this view. Zhang Xin said: "Isn't Benxi all about BX Steel? If BX Steel doesn't run its furnaces much, it's finished. These two years BX Steel has run little, so these two years the whole economy has been finished." Wang Li felt: "When the steel business here in Benxi was doing better, things were fine; now that steel prices have dropped, business everywhere here is bad."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805171909512JWzXGwFaBObY9cI.jpeg-w600)

From a macro perspective, the most obvious thing is the collapse of Benxi's public finances. In 2015, the year of BX Steel's pay cuts, [Benxi's public fiscal budget revenue was 5.35 billion yuan](http://www.benxi.gov.cn/News.asp?ID=2041387), down nearly 60% from [12.98 billion yuan in 2014](http://www.benxi.gov.cn/News.asp?ID=2031581). Benxi's fiscal revenue has yet to recover: in 2016 and 2017 the figures were [5.56 billion yuan](https://www.libaiwu.com/23770.htm) and [6.45 billion yuan](http://www.benxi.gov.cn/News.asp?ID=2057023) respectively. A person with knowledge of the matter told QDaily that tax collection has been hard in recent years: "The companies really are in bad shape; many of their financial figures are negative."

For a city with 1.5 million urban and rural residents, this level of fiscal revenue is essentially powerless to keep the city functioning normally. For Chen Juan's generation, already retired, the most direct feeling is that medical-insurance reimbursement quotas have shrunk. At present, Benxi's medical insurance can only cover 75% of what should be reimbursed.

Chen Juan has many complaints about Benxi's medical system. For a stretch of time, her shoulder had been bothering her. At a local Benxi hospital, doctors diagnosed frozen shoulder. After a period of treatment, the pain did not ease. Her son, who works in Shanghai, got her an appointment at a Shanghai hospital, where she was diagnosed with the more serious rotator cuff tear and had minor surgery.

"Benxi's medical standards aren't good; all the good doctors have left," Chen Juan said. She has also already transferred her medical-insurance account to Shanghai; otherwise she couldn't even get reimbursed.

Tight living has become a universal feeling. Wang Li's son is seven this year and about to start primary school. Future education costs worry her. In Benxi, many teachers run after-school tutoring classes, and students must attend. "Every month, food, drink, clothing, and tutoring for the kid — just one child costs five to six thousand a month. Tutoring is especially expensive, but there's nothing you can do; everyone has to get tutored."

Many Benxi people are cutting their spending. Before, going to KTV meant ordering a few bottles of beer, and several people would burn through 1,000 yuan in a night; now you can get by ordering nothing for 100 yuan. Two movie tickets together cost fifty or sixty yuan — that can be saved too; topping up a membership on a video site is good enough.

"Go into a Benxi supermarket and look: it's all people browsing, nobody buying. Only when things go on sale do people all rush to buy," Lu Ming said, his expression serious — not like he was joking.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180517191102tPMd6jzvO4Ik0AxJ.jpeg-w600)

Chen Juan believes Benxi failing to pay pensions is a matter of time. She said flatly that within two years at most, Benxi's pensions will definitely be done for. Every winter she now flies to Shanghai to live with her son. When spring comes, she returns to Benxi. Even though she retired long ago, she still finds work locally in Benxi.

Everyone is looking for a way out. Mr. Tang has been in the used-car business in Benxi for more than 10 years. It started as a hobby, but in recent years he has found more and more people joining the used-car trade. Large numbers of used cars line up along the roads in a long row — a spectacular sight.

Three or four years ago, Benxi loosened its environmental restrictions on used cars. According to public records, [Benxi's used-car import standards in 2014](https://www.che300.com/blog/?p=62) still required the China III emissions standard. [By 2015](https://club.autohome.com.cn/bbs/thread/123e8b3de582fc6a/48172304-1.html), contrary to most other Liaoning cities, which raised China III to China IV, Benxi dropped the China III requirement. In an instant, large numbers of out-of-town used cars flooded into Benxi, giving rise to a huge used-car market.

Mr. Tang bills himself as Benxi's biggest used-car dealer. He sources vehicles from all over the country — as near as Beijing, as far as Shanghai — and handles three or four used-car deals a day. Mr. Tang says the used-car trade is a technical line of work: you have to size up a car accurately and know its price; inexperienced people lose money easily. As to why Benxi has so many used cars, he figures: "You sell one car and earn three to four thousand yuan — isn't that a month's wages?"

Mr. Lü is a Meituan delivery rider. That afternoon, luck was not on his side: for more than half an hour, not a single delivery order popped up on his phone, so he leaned idly against his motorcycle. He attributed it to the off-season — business is only good in the hottest and coldest weather. Mr. Lü has been riding for over a year. Ordinarily he has his own job on a construction site; whenever a shift rotation leaves him free time, he comes out to deliver. As he put it, "It's all wage work anyway — earning one extra paycheck."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805171916239OzUbHgBh4CXSlwJ.jpeg-w600)

Earning a bit more money is the wish of many Benxi people. But with limited local opportunity, they are full of longing for the outside world.

Qi Feng is a nurse at a hospital in Benxi. She has a classmate from nursing school who, after working as a nurse for a while, went to Beijing to do Q&A for a health-services platform's back end. Qi Feng recalls her classmate earned five to six thousand yuan right after arriving in Beijing; now it's over twenty thousand, and she owns an apartment in each of Beijing and Benxi.

Lu Fang is Qi Feng's colleague. Her husband, who works out of town in Chongqing, told her about a young guy he had met at a youth hostel who sells balloons at a tourist spot in Chongqing. "He can sell more than 200 a day, and earns 20 yuan per balloon — do the math on how much he makes in a day."

"Twenty yuan per balloon," she repeated, her tone envious and wistful. "Really, opportunities over there are huge. You make money doing anything."

Lu Fang is considering leaving Benxi. She wants to go over once her husband gets settled in Chongqing — "It's especially nice, especially suitable for living, no great pressure." Failing that, going to Guangzhou would be fine too. "Benxi isn't really suitable for children either. Parents don't pay much attention to well-rounded development — a child's life, or social skills. To put it bluntly, the kids are like little fools: apart from studying they can't do anything. I don't want my child to be like that, so I have such determination to leave, to get out of this place."

This may represent the thinking of many people who leave Benxi, but it is definitely not the only thinking.

For young people like Liu Yuan, leaving Benxi has become a kind of subconscious reflex. Wherever you get into college, you'll very likely stay there. "Basically, in your mind, you just won't want to come back."

The older generation, like Zhu Rui, meanwhile consider renting a place in Sanya to retire. By Zhu Rui's calculation, the couple's pensions together of six to seven thousand yuan would be enough to live on in Sanya. In Benxi, winter brings extra expenses like heating fees on top of that — all told, living in Sanya wouldn't necessarily cost more than Benxi.

Starting in 2012, [the *Benxi Statistical Bulletin on National Economic and Social Development* began disclosing population-migration data](http://www.etmoc.com/gedi/itemlist.asp?id=1690). That year, 5,804 people moved into Benxi and 7,952 moved out. Population loss grew year by year thereafter; [by 2017, Benxi saw 4,113 move in and 10,635 move out, a net loss of 6,522](http://www.tjcn.org/tjgb/06ln/35534_3.html). Compared with registered-population figures that fluctuate due to births and deaths, migration data better reflects the predicament Benxi currently faces.

Should one keep living in Benxi at all? This is a question that those who remain also ponder from time to time.

"Right now we want a child, and my husband and I together make six to seven thousand yuan — it feels pretty tight. If wages get cut further, down to around a thousand-something, then there's really no point," Qi Feng said. "If Benxi's economy gets any worse and our hospital does badly too, we might still have to move away."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180517191432xfk1FGbgPYQXjNhD.jpeg-w600)

To stave off further economic decline, the Benxi municipal government is trying to develop new industries: tourism and pharmaceuticals. But judging from officially published macro data, these two new industries obviously cannot yet fill the gap left by steel's decline.

Benxi once had its moment of glory. In 1992, the State Council approved Benxi as a "larger city." Fellow "larger cities" included Tangshan, Dalian, Anshan, Qingdao, Wuxi, Suzhou, and 18 other cities. Gaining this title let Benxi hold certain local legislative powers; the central government hoped these cities could use local legislation to pilot economic-system reform.

The fates of the "larger cities" diverged. After Chongqing was elevated to a direct-administered municipality, its economic development was remarkable. Wuxi and Suzhou, located along the Yangtze River, attracted township enterprises and foreign-funded factories that became new economic pillars. Benxi, meanwhile, ran into its own troubles.

Although the State Council never officially rated Benxi a resource-exhausted city, Benxi fits the profile: excessive reliance on coal and steel, a single-industry structure, no replacement industries yet formed; coal and iron resources gradually depleting, industrial returns falling; insufficient aggregate economic output, weak local fiscal capacity. The people assigned by the state to this heavy-industry base before reform and opening-up have all grown old, while young people are unwilling to stay. Having lost BX Steel, its economic pillar, Benxi has finally entered its own twilight years.

Benxi is only a microcosm of resource-exhausted cities. [Fuxin, Liaoning](http://news.ifeng.com/a/20170813/51623820_0.shtml), 240 kilometers from Benxi, fell into crisis at the start of the 21st century as its coal mines ran dry, with 129,000 workers laid off — nearly 30% of all employees. Farther south, [Panjin, Liaoning](http://baijiahao.baidu.com/s?id=1586448889609421698&wfr=spider&for=pc), once buoyed by the Liaohe oil field, spent 12 consecutive years ranked last among Liaoning's 14 cities in GDP growth.

To date, the State Council has designated 69 resource-exhausted cities — and that obviously will not include every city that has run into this kind of predicament.
