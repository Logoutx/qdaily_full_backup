All part of the playbook?

Howard Schultz has never let a public opportunity pass without telling the story of his dream, exporting his values of "loving your employees" and "loving your customers." A performance of this scale is hardly a first for China.

This time, however, it was the first time Schultz appeared in China not as Starbucks' CEO, which made the theme of the talk — "A Story About Dreams and Responsibility" — sound more sincere. On December 1 last year, the man who single-handedly built the Starbucks coffee "empire" announced that he would formally step down as Starbucks CEO on April 3 this year, yet the poster still carried the title "Starbucks executive chairman" alongside the conspicuous Starbucks logo.

![The poster outside the Weilun Building of Tsinghua University's School of Economics and Management](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704120716114tWwCVpaPmQANSbr.JPG-w600)

The poster, printed with Schultz's signature smile, stood outside the Weilun Building at Tsinghua's School of Economics and Management and on the large LED screen above the entrance. The school's WeChat official account and official website were also the first channels to announce Schultz's China visit, and of the students we randomly interviewed who came to hear the talk, nearly all had learned about it there.

Even though the audience had no real intention of scanning them, a poster bearing six oversized black QR codes leaned against the portrait poster. Starbucks had also enlisted ifeng.com, Tencent, Sina, Yicai, Wenhui New Media, and Kankan News to livestream the talk; in Starbucks' PR messaging, this was touted as "a synchronized global livestream across six major platforms," and Starbucks China's PR staff had already reposted the news several times within their WeChat Moments. In the hours before Schultz began speaking, Starbucks China's WeChat official account and Weibo both published the announcement as well.

But something unexpectedly went a little wrong. The PR handlers, leading the 29 media outlets invited from across the country, were stopped by the gatekeepers at Tsinghua's southwest gate. One security guard said: "We didn't know about this event. Even if there is one, if the people over at the Weilun Building haven't notified our security office, it's not allowed." The PR staff grew so anxious they quarreled with the guards, and it wasn't until five minutes before the talk began that they were finally let through.

At 7 p.m., the talk formally began. The auditorium, which seats 400, was packed solid; not only were the media seats fully occupied, it was hard to squeeze in even at the entrance. Close to a hundred people stood in the very last row of the auditorium and listened through the entire talk, which ran about an hour and a half.

![More than 500 attendees came, filling every seat in the Weilun Building of Tsinghua University's School of Economics and Management.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170412043531BTuS32xAgC6kFaQ4.JPG-w600)

The Weilun Building where Schultz spoke carries no small significance. It was built in 1997 at the behest of Premier Zhu Rongji. Besides inviting business bigwigs to speak at every graduation ceremony, the School of Economics and Management also periodically invites guests for regular talks. For the past three years' graduation ceremonies, Tsinghua brought in Jack Ma, Facebook's chief operating officer Sheryl Sandberg, and former New York mayor Michael Bloomberg. Last year, IBM's chairman, president, and CEO Ginni Rometty was also invited to speak.

The real purpose of Schultz's talk was, as ever, to promote Starbucks' corporate culture. You could guess as much from the theme.

No surprise, then, that he once again recounted the story from his memoir *Pour Your Heart Into It*: as a boy, Schultz's father lost his job after breaking his ankle. With no medical insurance, social relief, or other safeguards, Schultz's mother had to go out and work as a domestic helper to make ends meet. Having witnessed the unfairness and disrespect his father suffered, he resolved to create a company humane and kind enough — and that company became Starbucks.

He also popularized a word from southern Africa, "Ubuntu" (meaning "I am because of you"), regarded as a traditional African idea of loyalty and connection. After telling the stories of how his first years entering the Chinese market ended in failure yet he never gave up, and of Jews sharing blankets for warmth before being sent to concentration camps during World War II, Schultz unfailingly repeated the phrase "I am because of you."

"Love," "kindness," and "empathy" became the keywords of Schultz's talk. More than once he said that many of Starbucks' decisions were not made with an eye to benefiting profits.

"Over the years, we have kept asking ourselves: what is our core purpose, and what is the meaning of our existence? Is it to open stores and make a profit? Is it to bring returns to our shareholders? Of course that is part of our responsibility. But the responsibility and goal we value more is to achieve a balance between commercial profit and social morality."

If you know even a little about the Schultz and Starbucks story, all of this sounds very familiar. At the annual "Starbucks Partner and Family Forum" in China, and at Starbucks' annual global shareholders' meeting in the United States, Schultz repeats these points every year. It is precisely this that has made Starbucks the commercial company most attentive to social issues.

Schultz is skilled at using events to cleverly amplify the emotional value proposition he pours into the company, and he is good at doing things that look barely related to running the business but touch on public policy and social issues. Whether actively rebuilding in the wake of Hurricane Katrina, or — after U.S. President Donald Trump announced the immigration ban — Schultz's announcement that Starbucks would hire 10,000 refugees as employees, it is all the same. In 2014, former war correspondent Rajiv Chandrasekaran co-wrote with Schultz the patriotism-infused *For Love of Country*, and a project Schultz later worked on with Chandrasekaran, a former *Washington Post* reporter, likewise focused on many social issues, such as race in conflict and the rights of people with disabilities, among others.

The combined online viewership of the six officially partnered livestream channels was claimed to have surpassed 30 million, but what seems rather suspicious is that ifeng.com's livestream, with zero comments, still held more than 3 million viewers online two hours after the talk ended. And on Bilibili, which was not an official partner, the bullet comments were pitifully few — one of them being, "I knew old Huo was here to serve chicken soup."

Judging from the scene, though, quite a few people were indeed inspired by the chicken soup. An alumna attending an executive training program at the School of Economics and Management said Schultz told quite a few down-to-earth life stories, and she praised Starbucks — "just a coffee seller" — for taking a leading position on employee benefits.

Schultz has visited China a fair number of times; he has even joked that he may be the multinational CEO who comes to China most frequently. After listening to the talk, one alumnus of Tsinghua's School of Economics and Management speculated that this trip to Tsinghua was likely just a "stop along the way" of his China visit.

![The Beijing 798 Reserve store](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170412073705OXKtj8hHoryAs7VG.jpg-w600)

In fact, on the very morning of the talk, Schultz appeared together with Belinda Wong, who had just been promoted to president of the China region last October, at a not-yet-officially-opened Starbucks Reserve store in Beijing's 798 Art District. This is Starbucks' high-end product line.

Premiumization, in fact, has been the theme Starbucks has stressed over the past six-plus months, and it is also the core business Schultz is to take charge of after stepping down as CEO. Unlike most of the dimly toned Reserve stores in China, the 798 Reserve store in Beijing uses warm wood elements on the ground floor and large amounts of cement and brass — materials very much in vogue — on its walls and staircases. Upon entering, you could still faintly smell the paint left over from the renovation.

Yesterday morning, Schultz left his signature on a wall on the third floor of the Reserve store, which became a "scenic spot" that Starbucks' partners — as the company calls its employees — take pride in. In fact, such "visits" were staged once in each of four cities: Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Chengdu. At the Partner and Family Forum held in Chengdu last year, he even went out of his way to get Jack Ma to appear in his support.

This year marks the fifth year of the forum, and it returned to Beijing. After more than 100 Starbucks partners and their family members crowded onto the third floor of the 798 Reserve store, the space became extremely cramped, with some partners already standing on the second-floor staircase. In the front row, besides a few senior Starbucks executives, sat a Starbucks partner wearing traditional Mongolian dress, with his mother, likewise in ethnic costume, beside him. They sat together and listened to five Starbucks partners tell stories about their lives with Starbucks.

Among the stories: one about Starbucks providing the best path for career development; one about a partner finding the other half of their life at Starbucks; and several about partners receiving financial aid through Starbucks. The whole venue felt very much like a corporate storytelling session.

But then Belinda Wong announced a policy Schultz called a "milestone": starting June 1 this year, all full-time Starbucks China partners who have worked at the company for two full years and whose parents are under 75 years old will enjoy the "Parent Care Program." In other words, Starbucks will provide full critical-illness insurance for employees' parents. In China, roughly more than 10,000 Starbucks partners meet both conditions at once, out of a total of nearly 40,000 partners in China.

A passage from Schultz laden with "elder-statesman" sentiment was used to portray Starbucks' kindness in terms of "filial piety": "I am deeply grateful to all of you parents for your trust in entrusting your children to us. We promise that we will do our best to help these children develop and grow, so that their parents can feel proud of them."

He also said half-jokingly in his talk: "I admit Starbucks is very bad at marketing. The Starbucks brand image has basically been built through offline stores and word of mouth." This corporate strategy pushes the partners who embody Starbucks culture to the fore. And Starbucks China's expansion plan to "open 5,000 stores in the next five years" also needs to attract more hands. Yumiko... no — Yu Hua, vice president of human resources for Starbucks China, said that in the next five years, the number of Starbucks China partners will double from the current figure, reaching nearly 80,000.

With more than 2,600 stores across 127 Chinese cities, and as it continues to push down into second- and third-tier cities, the premium store has become a differentiated presence for Starbucks. Besides the Beijing 798 Reserve store not yet open to the public, the media were also arranged to visit two other stores yesterday morning regarded as highly symbolic. Built in different periods with different characteristics — one is the Reserve store in Beijing's China World Trade Center, the other is the Starbucks flagship store in the Kerry Centre — they do not all carry Starbucks' classic green, but each comes with its own theme, and without exception they emphasize the work of local artists and designers.

The China World Reserve store is done in the style of a Beijing hutong: a metal relief on one wall uses the wire-inlay craft of making Beijing cloisonné, while another wall is built of the hollow-heart bricks commonly used in Beijing. The Kerry Centre flagship store's facade is covered with wooden molds for making mooncakes, and an entire wall is decorated with the hemp ropes used to transport coffee, running from the ground floor up to the second; every wall even bears the designer's signature. The Beijing 798 Reserve store also displays a piece of Suzhou embroidery by a Suzhou woman worker.

From the end of 2014, when Starbucks' largest coffee flagship store in the world, the "Starbucks Roastery" (Starbucks Roastery), opened in Seattle, it was regarded as the signal that Starbucks had begun to focus on premiumization. This is a higher-order experience distinct from Starbucks' standardization. Consumers can watch the roasting equipment up close while experiencing all kinds of innovative coffees in the heavily industrial-styled space.

This format was quickly brought to New York, and Shanghai — which ranks third worldwide in store count — will see the arrival in May of its own Starbucks Reserve coffee "Roastery," a 2,700-square-meter space capable of demonstrating the coffee-bean roasting process on site, at HKRI Taikoo Hui.

Starbucks' first Reserve store in China, meanwhile, opened in October 2014 in the Shanghai World Financial Center. There are currently more than 60 Starbucks Reserve stores in the Chinese market, along with four flagship stores. Last May, they also opened a new Reserve store at Sinan Mansions in Shanghai.

[In our year-end review of Starbucks earlier](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/35217.html), we analyzed the challenges the company now faces: its performance every fiscal quarter falls short of Wall Street's growth expectations, and profit growth relies on price increases. The results for the first quarter of fiscal 2017 were hardly impressive either — global same-store sales growth of 3% was the lowest since 2009. But the China and Asia-Pacific markets were strong, with revenue up 17.9%, far above the global growth rate of 6.7%, and same-store sales growth of 5% as well.

While placing its hopes on premium stores to drive growth, the China market is, in a sense, Starbucks' biggest bet.

"What were your biggest concerns about entering the Italian market next year, and how do they compare with your concerns when entering China in 1999?" one student asked on the spot.

"Italy has a very good coffee foundation. I waited nearly 50 years before deciding to enter the Italian market. At the time I thought, 'If I can't get in, I'm dead.' But looking back, that anxiety and neuroticism were actually quite healthy. Chinese people didn't used to drink coffee; the China business lost money for its first few years, and people were also fairly worried about national protectionism. The only path to success is to show that you will respect the local culture," Schultz answered. "This year we will launch the Roastery in Shanghai." He offered an analogy: the Roastery is like "the Disney of coffee."

Schultz should know very well that China's coffee market today is nothing like it was 20 years ago.
