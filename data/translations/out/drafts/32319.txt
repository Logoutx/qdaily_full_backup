Why Is It So Hard to Buy a Jet Black iPhone 7?

@@QD_BODY@@
Jet black and every Plus model are nearly impossible to find, and authorized resellers have pushed prices up to 20,000 yuan

At 8 a.m. on September 16, the second day of the Mid-Autumn Festival holiday was just beginning. Most shops along Nanjing East Road pedestrian street in Shanghai were still preparing to open, but at the Henderson Metropolitan shopping center on the east side, two lines — each several hundred people long — had already formed.

One line ran from the first floor of the mall up the motionless escalators to the second floor, where a door leads into the Apple Nanjing East Road store. These were customers who had reserved iPhones online earlier and come to the store for pickup.

The other line formed at the Apple store's street-facing main entrance. Those who had come to the Apple store without reserving online in advance had to wait until exactly 10 a.m., when the store officially opened, to go in and buy an iPhone. A good portion of them wanted the new iPhone in Jet Black, but that day, only the first few dozen in line got to take one home.

### Jet black and every Plus model are nearly impossible to find

![An Apple ad placed inside a Shanghai subway station](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160919003850vX8hpjuKEM7WTr1m.jpg-w600)

Since sales began on September 16, Apple has put up large black outdoor ads everywhere. But getting your hands on that black, glossy iPhone 7 Plus from the ads is just too hard. Unless you placed your order in the first two minutes after pre-orders opened at 3 p.m. on September 9, or you're willing to queue for hours like these people.

Over the next two days, even Apple's official retail stores — which had the deepest stock — had almost nothing on hand. On September 18, QDaily reporters visited four Apple stores in Beijing and Shanghai. Plenty of customers arriving at the stores were asking about Jet Black, but the answer they got at these stores was roughly the same: "Whatever shade of black you want, if you want it in stock you have to grab it on the website at 8 a.m."

Staff at several stores said that since the batch of several thousand iPhone 7 and 7 Plus units that sold out before the September 16 launch, only a few hundred new units had arrived each day. Depending on the store's location, the number of Jet Black units among them ranged from a handful to zero.

When a staffer at the Shanghai Wujiaochang store heard a reporter say the Nanjing East Road store got a few Jet Black units every day, he sighed, "Location really does make a difference." That relatively low-traffic Apple store hadn't received a single Jet Black unit since the 16th.

If you order from the website now, the Jet Black iPhone 7 takes 3–5 weeks to ship to you. The Jet Black iPhone 7 Plus won't arrive until November, and other colors take 2–3 weeks as well.

### With shortages everywhere, Apple authorized resellers can drive the price up to 20,000 yuan

Hard as it is through official channels, everywhere else is even harder. The [Apple flagship store](https://apple.tmall.com/p/rd744050.htm?spm=a1z10.4-b.w5003-5366045755.1.RghUPL&scene=taobao_shop) on Tmall has already taken the iPhone 7 Plus off the shelves. And on the morning of the 18th, the iPhone 7 was in stock only in black (matte), gold, pink, and silver — none in the 32GB version. By noon, black was sold out too.

[JD.com](https://item.jd.com/3133843.html) had only gold and rose gold iPhone 7 units in stock; every 7 Plus was sold out.

![The iPhone 7 Plus is no longer available on JD.com](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609190041038cVmthJ4NKYLpI7v.png-w600)

An Apple authorized store near Dongzhimen in Beijing did have stock. But this store, listed on Apple's official website as an authorized reseller, wasn't selling these iPhones at list price: the Jet Black iPhone 7 Plus 256GB, originally 7,988 yuan, was going for 20,000 yuan per unit; the Jet Black iPhone 7 256GB, originally 6,988 yuan, had been speculated up to 13,888 yuan.

Even quotes for non-black iPhone 7 models ranged from 6,188 to 11,888 yuan — 800 to 5,000 yuan above list price.

"That's the price everywhere now, the supply is just too small," a staffer at this Apple authorized store explained to the reporter, explaining the markup. He said that after the company sold through its September 16 batch, it hadn't been able to get any new models from Apple, and had to source units on its own — 40 units had just arrived on the 18th.

Using the contact information provided on Apple's official website, we contacted roughly 40 Apple authorized stores in Beijing, Changchun, Tianjin, Nanjing, Shanghai, and other cities. Apart from a few that were completely out of stock, all were selling the new iPhones at markups ranging from several hundred to more than ten thousand yuan.

Several staffers willing to explain the iPhone 7 supply situation also acknowledged that since the first batch, Apple had yet to provide any new iPhone 7 units.

The iPhone 7 seems to have returned to the days of the iPhone 4 entering China, or the launch of the iPhone 5s "champagne gold." Apple's own retail stores strictly hold the line on price, but have almost no stock. And nearly everywhere else is selling at a markup.

### Clearly, Apple itself underestimated users' enthusiasm for the iPhone 7

In the week from the start of pre-orders on the 9th to last Friday's market close, Apple's stock rose 11.43%, and the company's market value jumped by more than 60 billion US dollars. That shows iPhone 7 sales are performing far beyond Wall Street's expectations.

It isn't just outsiders — Apple itself also underestimated early sales of the iPhone 7.

You can also see it in the change in purchase limits: Apple is encouraging you to buy more iPhones. This time Apple set the limit at four iPhones per person (two of each model), while last year the limit was two units.

Before the iPhone 7, the iPhone — which accounts for the vast majority of Apple's revenue — saw sales decline 16.3% and 15% respectively in the first two quarters of this year, and Tim Cook felt the trend would continue. In July, [when announcing earnings](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/30146.html), Apple forecast revenue for July–September of between 45.5 and 47.5 billion US dollars, roughly 10% less than [the quarter in which the iPhone 6s launched last year](http://www.statista.com/statistics/263426/apples-global-revenue-since-1st-quarter-2005/).

Based on this forecast, Apple prepared fewer iPhone 7 units. Companies that make components for the iPhone 7 all predicted in their own earnings reports that orders would decline in the third quarter.

Japanese company Alps supplies the camera image-stabilization components for the iPhone. It expects that in the third quarter of this year — the first three months the iPhone 7 is on sale — its revenue from electronic components will fall 6.7% compared with the same period last year.

That already counts as one of the more optimistic companies, because the 4.7-inch iPhone 7 also uses its components, whereas last year only the 5.5-inch version supported optical image stabilization.

Taiwan's ASE holds Apple's chip packaging, testing, and system-in-package orders; it expects this segment's third-quarter revenue to fall 13% year over year. JDI, one of the iPhone's main screen suppliers, forecasts a 20% decline.

The expected level of user enthusiasm determines how many phones Apple prepares.

Apple uses a relatively traditional distribution model. Apart from orders through its own website and several hundred Apple retail stores worldwide, iPhones are first sold to other channels (e-commerce sites, carriers, authorized stores), which then sell them to consumers.

If demand is overestimated, too much product piles up in the channels, causing losses. In April this year, Cook acknowledged at an earnings call that the company had overestimated how popular the iPhone 6s would be in the first two quarters after its launch, and ended up spending more than 2 billion US dollars dealing with iPhones backed up in the sales channels.

This time Apple misjudged user enthusiasm again — only this time, it wasn't an overestimate.

### The allocation of colors has also gone wrong

Jet Black became the most sought-after iPhone partly because it is hard to produce, but also because of how Apple allocated its inventory across colors.

In contrast to the Jet Black shortage, rose gold was amply supplied. Many Apple stores that were sold out of other colors still had rose gold and gold options.

We pre-ordered an iPhone 7 in this color after sales began, and its ship date was recently moved up — from mid-to-late October to late September — something that absolutely will not happen for the two black models right now.

![The hands-on area after Apple's September launch event; the Jet Black iPhone 7 Plus was placed in the most prominent spot](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160919071145iZt5FU2pWgGJLA8n.JPG-w600)

If you bought an iPhone 5s three years ago, you surely remember how the gold version's supply falling short of demand is [almost exactly the same](http://www.businessinsider.com/apple-out-of-gold-iphone-5s-because-asia-likes-gold-2013-9) as now.

Back then, Apple badly underestimated Asian consumers' preference for gold, and it was out of stock within a day of launch. In the first month, the Chinese market even had only the black version in stock for a time.

In the online stores of Hong Kong and Singapore, the gold iPhone 5s also had longer shipping times — first-batch pre-orders weren't due until October.

Pure black is also a classic color across iPhone generations; the iPhone 3G, 3GS, 4, 4s, and 5 all came in black. Only after the iPhone 5's paint chipping did Apple switch to space gray. Now this classic black is back.

![iPhone 3GS — the classic black is back, and the craftsmanship has become more complex](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160919032403m7eawg81SsCJY25I.jpg-w600)

Jet Black is the iPhone 7's most distinctive feature compared with the previous two generations.

And Apple itself is steering this trend: in nearly all of its outward-facing visual imagery — ads and the official website alike — the words "iPhone 7" are inevitably accompanied by a Jet Black iPhone 7 Plus.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160919032430Pvu5lQEoWNfZ1RBX.png-w600)

[Statistics from Slice](https://intelligence.slice.com/iphone-7-first-time-plus-model-pre-ordered/) show that among US consumers, 46% chose "black" (matte) within 48 hours of pre-orders opening, followed by Jet Black (23%); fewer than a third bought a non-black model.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609190225233IuLHXFe0Q1nivmq.jpg-w600)

### It looks about the same as the iPhone 6, but the iPhone 7 is harder to manufacture

Although more people bought black, the Jet Black version is paradoxically the one in shortest supply — which may have to do with its production process.

According to information QDaily obtained from one Apple store, among each day's newly arrived units, perhaps only 1 in 1,000 is the Jet Black version.

That Jet Black you can't get your hands on is also a new thing that consumes more cost. Jony Ive briefly explained its production principle [in a video](http://apple.youku.com/play/XMTcxNzEwNjUzMg==/?spm=0.0.0.0.QKmmrv): Apple takes an aluminum alloy back shell and gives it a "3D rotational polishing," repeatedly washing the metal surface with a special compound, followed by an anodizing step, and finally polishing the surface layer again... in short, it's more complicated. [*Wired*](https://www.wired.com/2016/09/apple-jet-black-iphone-none-more-black/), citing industry insiders, explained that this process may take at least 40 minutes longer than for iPhones in other colors.

The dual camera is also harder to produce.

Photography is the iPhone's biggest technological advance this time around. The iPhone 7 Plus uses a dual camera for the first time, and combined with the new water-resistance feature, it is harder to manufacture.

[Nikkei](http://asia.nikkei.com/Business/Companies/Apple-may-face-iPhone-7-supply-shortage-at-launch-source?page=1) reports that multiple component suppliers were still working on yield-rate problems as late as August this year; if Apple insisted on launching the new phone in September, there might not be enough supply in the initial phase. Now that problem has actually materialized.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160919032505LOiuoqA4z71EvFKp.jpeg-w600)

Production is difficult on one hand; on the other, more people have their eyes on the dual-camera iPhone 7 Plus.

[Slice's statistics](https://intelligence.slice.com/iphone-7-first-time-plus-model-pre-ordered/) show that within 48 hours of pre-orders opening, more consumers ordered the iPhone 7 Plus than the iPhone 7 — the exact opposite of the previous two years.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160919023830gQK08w4xSBasjWcL.jpg-w600)

And finally, Samsung also did its part to drive the iPhone 7 Plus shortage.

This year Apple has a new rival — Samsung's curved-screen phone. The Galaxy S7 Edge even briefly surpassed the iPhone 6s this year to become the best-selling smartphone in the US.

Shortly before the iPhone 7 launch, Samsung released the Note 7, which also uses a curved screen, and sold more than 2 million units in a matter of days.

But then came the news of Note 7s catching fire, followed by Samsung's announcement in early September of a global recall of the Note 7. During the recall, more Note 7s burned. US airlines also began asking passengers not to turn on their Note 7s.

Samsung was the only Android maker that could sell phones in large numbers at the iPhone's price point.

A report from market research firm [MarketStragist](http://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20160906006644/en/Smartphone-Wars-Apple%E2%80%99s-Opportunity-Strike) notes that 33% of Samsung users expressed interest in the iPhone 7. Who knows how many users who had planned to get a Note 7 bought an iPhone 7 Plus instead after the burning incidents.

### Pre-orders are this hot — does it mean the iPhone 7 has reversed the decline that began with the iPhone 6s?

No one can answer that question yet.

There are precedents for products that couldn't be bought at first and later suffered from oversupply. The 2015 Apple Watch also sold out for a time, with supply severely short. But that didn't last long; by its fifth quarter on sale, Apple Watch sales had already fallen noticeably compared with a year earlier.

The same goes for the iPhone that Apple relies on. The gold iPhone 5s was severely out of stock early in its launch, but that year iPhone sales rose only slightly over the iPhone 5.

Predicting user demand [is no easy task](http://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2011-11-03/apples-supply-chain-secret-hoard-lasers), so Apple generally asks its Asian suppliers to keep at least two weeks' worth of inventory on hand, so that it can adjust assembly production at any time based on market feedback.

Apple has already begun adjusting. Taiwanese outlet [Digitimes](http://www.digitimes.com/news/a20160904PD200.html) reports that on the eve of the iPhone 7 launch, Apple temporarily added 10% to its orders with chip manufacturers. After the iPhone 7 launch, Apple placed some additional orders as well.

[Bloomberg](http://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2011-11-03/apples-supply-chain-secret-hoard-lasers), citing supply-chain sources, says Apple makes adjustments after launches in previous years too, and that order volumes typically only stabilize by mid-October — about a month after the new iPhone goes on sale.

Only then will we be able to judge how the iPhone 7 is actually selling.

Still, the iPhone 7's current shortage can at last let Tim Cook — who just turned in [two quarters of declining numbers](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/30146.html) — breathe a sigh of relief. A shortage is far better than unsold inventory.
