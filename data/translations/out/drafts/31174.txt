This interview covers the meaning of 3D technology, ethical fashion, fashion's sense of humor, and democracy.

Last weekend, the Metropolitan Museum of Art's new 2016 exhibition, *Manus x Machina: Fashion in an Age of Technology*, closed in New York.

The exhibition ran from May 5 and presented a selection of haute couture and ready-to-wear pieces from the 20th and 21st centuries, grouping couture and ready-to-wear garments with similar design forms together. Among them were Martin Margiela's Spring/Summer 1997 "Semi-couture" collection, which blurred the boundary between clothing and the human form; Dior's deconstructed designs in its Fall/Winter 2005 haute couture collection that mimic the half-finished state of a tailored garment; Mariano Fortuny; Madame Grès; and Hussein Chalayan's LED dress, among other works.

Overall, the exhibition asked visitors to compare the finished results of different decorative and design techniques, attempting to discuss the boundary between machine production and handcraft.

In addition, the museum published an exhibition catalogue of the same name. In the book's introduction, curator Andrew Bolton traced the origins of the rule that haute couture must be handmade: "The Chambre Syndicale de la Haute Couture, founded in 1910, made the dividing line between hand and machine not only increasingly entrenched within the modern fashion system, but also more institutionalized. This became especially apparent in 1945, when the syndicate introduced explicit rules for the handmade creation of haute couture." And the implication of this historical review seems to be: the rules about handmade haute couture were an artificial measure, born largely of the need to protect French economic interests and industrial production, driven by specific historical factors — but they should not remain stubbornly unchanged as time moves on.

In other words, the exhibition wanted to use contemporary imagery and values to talk with you about what machine production is, what handcraft is, and whether the definitions of the two have changed and crossed over.

With the exhibition nearing its close, the pop-culture news site REDEF interviewed Andrew Bolton. Naturally, the curator talked about the original intent behind preparing the exhibition; more interestingly, he also shared his views on the application of 3D technology in fashion, ethical fashion, fashion's sense of humor, and democracy.

### 1. The exhibition aims to discuss the boundary between machine production and handcraft

"The exhibition aims to discuss the boundary between machine production and handcraft. Traditionally, handcraft has always been associated with luxury, individualism, and originality, while machine production is seen as homogeneous, mass-industrial, and dehumanizing. But the question should not be viewed so simply. I believe that throughout the history of the fashion industry, there have always been machine-made things that took more time and cost more than handmade ones. That is also what the Manus x Machina exhibition aims to discuss."

### 2. Have any audience criticisms been hard for you to digest?

"Actually, what surprised me most was how open-minded the audience was. Many people came to see the show, and there was plenty of dissent as well. Some people, seeing the word 'technology' in an exhibition's title, immediately assume it is an exhibition about wearable technology. Before the show opened, I worried that its content would not match audience expectations. We had done a punk exhibition long ago with a similar problem. What we wanted to do was the influence of punk culture on the high fashion industry, but visitors said they had assumed the exhibition was about 1970s street punk."

"On the whole, audiences were very receptive to this exhibition. They noticed the messages we wanted to convey, such as the focus on 'materiality.' They really focused on the clothes themselves — on what was handmade and what was machine-made."

### 3. It isn't only 3D printing that connects fashion and technology

"The third revolution, or the electronic revolution, has been discussed for a long time. Fashion and technology have been closely linked since the first Industrial Revolution (it isn't something that began with 3D printing technology), although earlier applications were mostly in the textile industry. Fashion is an industry that embraces new technology quickly. Right now, fashion and technology are in a relationship of evolving together, rather than having arrived at some other stage."

### 4. Are consumers more accepting of machine-made products now?

"I think consumers care more about quality now, and they care more about the impact of large-scale machine production, not just the quantity. Previously, many people were suspicious and fearful of technology. And I always wanted to do an exhibition that showed this kind of fear within the realm of fashion — it is also a manifestation of people's fear of technology."

"The way fashion and technology combine is very practical. When 3D technology arrived, none of us were quite sure how it would ultimately affect fashion. But I believe it will eventually have revolutionary significance, like the spinning jenny. This technology could make fashion more democratic — you could have a printer at home and print out a T-shirt you love. That would be the ultimate form of fashion's existence, but with current technology, that step is still a long way off.

### 5. Ethical fashion is a way of balance

In the introduction to Manus x Machina, Andrew Bolton quoted from the *Encyclopédie* compiled by Diderot: "The clothing industry, art, and technology all rest on the same moral foundation." Andrew Bolton then made an analogy: "The clothing industry is as valuable as poetry or medicine."

Now, in fashion, the word "moral" has taken on a new extension, used more often to refer to the impact of production on the environment and on people.

"I think 'morality' in fashion is a responsibility. The fashion industry pollutes heavily, and both designers and factories should be responsible to the environment. Of course, for many designers this is hard to achieve. Take fabrics, for example: for the sake of beauty, environmental protection gets sacrificed, and if you put environmental protection first, the fabric lacks appeal. (Ethical fashion) is a way of balance."

### 6. The current use of 3D printing technology is to replicate nature

Iris van Herpen has several pieces in the Manus x Machina exhibition that use snail shells, coral reefs, and other things from nature, and they look very "organic."

"If you chat with designers like Iris van Herpen and Noa Raviv, they will tell you that the practical application of 3D printing technology should steer it toward more organic forms. That is a more practical way of thinking, but I believe there is also a philosophical component in it. The current use of 3D printing technology is to replicate nature."

### 7. The original intent of the Manus x Machina exhibition was to use aesthetics to challenge consumers' perception of the difference between handcraft and machine production

The Manus x Machina exhibition includes works by some lesser-known designers, such as Noa Raviv and Maiko Takeda — so how did Andrew Bolton come across their work?

"Actually, I discovered these two designers through the magazine Dezeen. Both of them are pioneers in combining handcraft and machine."

"Noa Raviv began using 3D printing technology through computer trial and error. I have always been obsessed with knowing whether, without human intervention, a machine can err on its own. Likewise, without human intervention, can a machine be creative? And some of Noa Raviv's works carry a Platonic romanticism and the aesthetic rules of Greece — symmetry and perfection. And if a machine is used to complete this kind of design, will the machine destroy that style? I am keen to discuss the reconciliation between perfection and imperfection, or rather, to understand the difference between errors made by human hands and errors made deliberately by machines."

"What is interesting about Maiko Takeda is that the aesthetics she creates with machines and high technology are actually all completed by hand. I admire that. And the original intent of the Manus x Machina exhibition was to use aesthetics to challenge consumers' perception of the difference between handcraft and machine production. Sometimes, you think something was made by a machine, but it was actually made by hand. Meanwhile, look at Christopher Kane's work — you would mistake it for handmade, when in fact it was done by machine."

### 8. The reason for choosing Christopher Kane's work is that it is a contemporary portrait of Christian Dior's 1950 design

Some of Christopher Kane's floral dresses were selected for the Manus x Machina exhibition.

"Mainly because I think Christopher Kane's design is a contemporary portrait of Christian Dior's 1950 design. So we placed the two designers' works together. Both designers used flowers, embroidery, and organza in their designs, which could be said to highlight feminine beauty. Looking closely at Christopher Kane's work, the organza and floral designs are a kind of 'wanton sexual provocation.' For me, it recalls the classic microscopic flower paintings of American painter Georgia O'Keeffe. It cleverly links sex and flowers — think of the body of a flower and the body of a woman."

### 9. Some people use design to convey humor, and some use it to express cleverness

"There is a lot of humor in the fashion industry. In fact, many European designers display a distinctive sense of humor and irony, but very few American designers do. Thom Browne is a typical example; Jean Paul Gaultier, John Galliano, and Comme des Garçons's Rei Kawakubo are also in this camp. More and more designers want to inject humor into their designs. On the other hand, some designers prefer their work to show cleverness or intelligence — for example, Martin Margiela and many Belgian designers. But these two camps never intersect. John Galliano and [Alexander McQueen](http://www.alexandermcqueen.com/) fall somewhere in between.

### 10. Fast fashion is dystopian

"The fast fashion business model emphasizes that fashion is by nature disposable or discardable. Fast fashion brands' products cannot reflect designers' talent — they are even their failures. In short, it gives them no room to develop their creativity. I think fast fashion is simply a consumption model — single-use, throw it away when you're done. Fast fashion is dystopian; to some extent it extinguishes the artistry of fashion. That said, I do appreciate the democratization of fashion — (fast fashion) can bring fashion to mass consumers at a relatively low price. I just don't like how this business model kills creativity."

### 11. Is there a way to protect designers' intellectual property, avoid the waste of fast fashion, and yet make fashion more democratic?

"I think culture has a natural tendency to plagiarize. I have never considered 'copying' in fashion a problem. A writer dies, and years later you read his ideas, and you can still apply them in the present day. I hold the same attitude toward haute couture."

"Fashion has increasingly become something that requires a diverse combination. (Building a brand) requires designers, pattern makers, stylists — it is a collaboration, a deep exchange of ideas. In the fashion industry, the idea of relying on a single standout talent is fading."

"The most interesting part of fashion is the shaping of personal style — for example, how people mix fast fashion with vintage fashion, how they do DIY. On the other hand, whether on the runway or on the street, pure originality is becoming rarer and harder to find. Because we simply live in a homogeneous world, and I rather miss the era when originality was everywhere."

### 12. Economic depression gives rise to fashion libertarianism

"The present seems to be an era of smug complacency. Look at the periods in history when fashion creativity flourished — they were usually times of economic depression. For example, the punk culture of 1970s London, which came at the most economically depressed moment in British history, yet was also the era of the most creative personal fashion style. The same was true of New York's East Village in the same period. I believe economic depression gives rise to fashion libertarianism — more diverse expressions and interpretations of personal style. People always need a certain rebellious spirit to achieve 'victory' in a personal sense."

"The same situation exists in other economic systems. In terms of street style, Japan is an interesting example. Cosplay fascinates me — it is a complex way of using fashion to express personal identity. Tokyo is now arguably the world center of personal fashion expression."
