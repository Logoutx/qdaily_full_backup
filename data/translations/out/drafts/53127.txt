Lately I've been thinking, first, about how to make a living, about how to live a better life. And then about how I can write something more meaningful, something that benefits other people.

Scholar Shi Jiepeng has just published a new book of lectures on classical poetry, titled *My Longing Heart — Twenty Lectures on Classical Poetry by Liang Huiwang* (《悠悠我心——梁惠王古诗词二十讲》). The title is taken from *The Book of Songs* and was chosen by his editor. At first the editor gave Shi Jiepeng several titles to choose from, but the one finally settled on was not among them.

Shi Jiepeng let the editor pick the name, knowing that "the editor in charge would feel this name sells better."

Within half a month of going on sale, *My Longing Heart* was reprinted twice and ranked third on Amazon's new book chart. An editor involved said the book's sales were better than expected, and that readers' enthusiasm for poetry was higher than they had imagined.

Shi Jiepeng had earlier inserted song lyrics in his novel *The Chu Tomb* (《楚墓》); friends found them evocative and encouraged him to write on the subject separately. Later the state began promoting the study of classical literature, and poetry courses proliferated online. At the time another friend of his was running online courses and said he wanted to turn Shi Jiepeng's poetry lectures into a full-scale course taught on the web. Shi Jiepeng agreed offhandedly, but the two friends worked eagerly to make it happen.

But the more important background may be that, at that time, Shi Jiepeng had lost his job and had nothing to do.

And so the course took shape. Besides the poetry lectures, Shi Jiepeng also ran a mini-course of eight lectures on the *Records of the Grand Historian* (《史记》) and a course on Chinese characters. After the courses went online, Shi Jiepeng wrote a 10,000-character script for each lecture; this book is the collection of those scripts.

Shi Jiepeng was dismissed by Beijing Normal University in July 2017.

The dismissal notice at the time stated that Shi Jiepeng had long posted erroneous remarks online, causing negative social impact, and that for this Beijing Normal University was dismissing him. The notice contained a more detailed passage stating that Shi Jiepeng "frequently continued to post improper remarks through his personal Weibo account and WeChat official account, remarks inconsistent with mainstream values and unbecoming of a Beijing Normal University faculty member. Shi Jiepeng crossed the ideological management red line, violated political discipline, and brought great harm to the university's reputation."

[This bears some similarity to a recent news story:](https://www.douban.com/people/bonnae1982/status/2162627190/) The Party committee of Zhongnan University of Economics and Law issued a document stating that the university's associate professor Zhai Juhong, while teaching, "violated classroom discipline in class, taught erroneous viewpoints, and made improper remarks, producing a negative impact." The document notes that Zhai Juhong, 49, on April 25, while teaching "Government Structure and Functions" in the course *Principles of Political Science* to the class of 2017 undergraduates in administrative management (first-year students), "deviated from the textbook, the teaching syllabus and the courseware, and erroneously explained the amendment of our country's constitution." Zhai Juhong also "gave an erroneous account of our country's state-owned enterprise property rights system, made unwarranted comments on our country's people's congress system, and gave one-sided introductions to the political systems of other countries or regions, producing a negative impact among students." The Party committee of Zhongnan University of Economics and Law decided to expel Zhai Juhong from the Party, issue a demerit record, transfer Zhai Juhong away from teaching and research posts, and report to the issuing authority to revoke Zhai Juhong's teaching qualification.

And so, Shi Jiepeng was out of a job. Before the dismissal, Shi Jiepeng was an associate professor at the Institute of Ancient Books and Traditional Culture at Beijing Normal University. After graduating from Peking University with a master's degree, he had worked there for 20 years.

![Cover of *My Longing Heart — Twenty Lectures on Classical Poetry by Liang Huiwang*, from Amazon](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180513234002yb4wJq35QG0NDA68.jpg-w600)

Half a month later, Shi Jiepeng's official account recorded his state after losing his job.

"These past days I could only fall asleep by taking estazolam tablets. Many people urged me not to take them and looked up information for me. One look, and it genuinely startled me: liver damage, addiction, increased risk of depression, and hallucinations during sleep... My god, can't keep taking it. After all, the kitty still needs me to take care of it! A friend of mine is a truly good person, but the other day he sent me a line: 'I hear you actually like Lu Xun?' I could dimly picture his half-smiling, exasperated expression on the other side of the screen. I said: 'Let's agree to differ.' He agreed. For someone who's been through the world, not liking Lu Xun is hard. The more you've been through, the more you like him. The more lowlifes you've seen, the more you like him. But next time, you'll still hit a wall."

Five months later, on the last day of 2017, Shi Jiepeng wrote on his official account: "For me, 2017 was a nightmarish year, one I don't want to look back on. If changing the year could change my luck, I hope this ash-like bit of time hurries up and passes — good riddance, don't see it out. I'd love to just piss on it right now, put it out, and step straight into the new year. But what about the new year? Will this world get better? I'm fairly pessimistic, and so, trousers in hand, I stand somewhat at a loss."

After 2018 began, Shi Jiepeng was invited by Japan's Institute of Oriental Culture to be a visiting scholar. He went to Tokyo together with his wife and daughter.

*My Longing Heart* held a book launch on April 21. Shi Jiepeng arrived in Beijing on the evening of April 18. Once the launch ended, he would head straight to the airport and fly back to Tokyo.

At the event, Shi Jiepeng said his favorite poet is actually Li Shangyin. But the book and the course include no lecture on Li Shangyin, because to lecture on Li Shangyin alone would leave no time. And to lecture on Li Shangyin together with others would, Shi Jiepeng felt, be a desecration of Li Shangyin.

Most of the time, Shi Jiepeng says whatever comes to mind. When he explains a problem coherently, you can still hear the teacher's tone. Not the sound of the voice — when he speaks continuously, he slightly raises his pitch and, at the same time, takes a breath. It seems natural and fluent, yet carries a trace of effort. It is a habit formed from accumulated experience. Twenty years of teaching gave him this habit; he could not shake it if he tried.

But compared with before, he has one obvious change: he has added a frequently used term — politically incorrect. Usually the term appears like this: "If I keep talking this way, it'll easily get politically incorrect." In private he will, trustingly, say a few more sentences; even when writing things on his official account he comes across as fairly unguarded. But when he finds himself in a public setting, he chooses to stop right there and not elaborate further.

**Q: QDaily**

**Shi Jiepeng: Author of *My Longing Heart — Twenty Lectures on Classical Poetry by Liang Huiwang*, *The Village Head Xiao Wu* (《亭长小武》), and *The Household Register* (《户口本》)**

**Q: The online shopping page for *My Longing Heart* introduces you this way: "A scholar of classical philology and a historical novelist, Shi Jiepeng, pen name Liang Huiwang, once taught at a prestigious university. Drawing on his deep grounding in ancient paleography, historical phonology and classical poetry, he tries to bridge past and present, reconstruct the living contexts of ancient people, and probe the essence of human life, full of human compassion; with the heart of a child, he says what no one before him has said; he does not trade in grand concepts, only in basic human nature; he does not look to the verdicts of earlier generations, only appraises the achievement of poetry; he does not dwell on subtle words with profound meaning, only appreciates the art itself." Reading this appraisal, what do you think? Anything you would correct?**

Shi Jiepeng: "Once taught at a prestigious university" — that, I think, can be corrected. Why should I cling to a prestigious university? I don't think it's necessary. I feel it's enough to be confident in myself; I don't need to borrow some school to add luster to myself.

And there's "a body of work as tall as the author" (著作等身) — first of all, I don't have a body of work as tall as myself. Besides, that expression has already become a standardized phrase, already rather vulgar. I don't think it's necessary to use it, because it conveys no information. Meaningless. Then the rest — what was it, "tries to bridge past and present" — I also don't think that's necessary. That's the kind of hackneyed phrasing I wouldn't use to describe myself if I were writing it.

The rest I think is all fine — "the heart of a child," "does not trade in grand concepts," those are all quite good.

**Q: You said you really like Liu Zhenyun. Recently his novel *One Sentence Is Ten Thousand Sentences* was adapted into a stage play. What do you think of that novel?**

Shi Jiepeng: The first time I read it, I felt his language was very good. He is very plain, a bit like the character of folk literature, but unlike Zhao Shuli, he has a bit of humor in him. Unlike Mo Yan — there's nothing much to see in Mo Yan's language — Liu Zhenyun's language reaches its peak in *One Sentence Is Ten Thousand Sentences*.

Others may not agree, but personally I feel the greatest glory of his fiction lies in its distinctive language, especially in *One Sentence Is Ten Thousand Sentences*. His later novels resemble *One Sentence*; I imagine that has something to do with what he writes about — things from the Republican era, which have a somewhat more farcical feel. I think it is a bit like Zhao Shuli's novels; only that perspective and that popular mode of expression were his peak. He never wrote a novel that good again.

I have read many of his novels. *Ground Covered with Chicken Feathers* (《一地鸡毛》) is also very good; I read it in high school. He published it in the late 1980s. *Hometown: Yellow Flowers Under Heaven* (《故乡天下黄花》) is quite a failure — he was probably under the influence of magic realism. But he is not a scholar-type author, nor a writer of philosophical speculation; when he forced himself to write with more intellectual weight, he was out of his depth. *Hometown* writes about land reform, and it is not more brilliant than anyone else's. His two later novels are unremarkable, because they are a bit too skit-like — the content is all stuff from the internet.

**Q: I've noticed that you say whatever you think.**

Shi Jiepeng: I say what I have to say directly. Personally I wouldn't vilify anyone either. What is good is good; what I think is bad, I think is bad. I don't want to cater to anyone, don't want to kiss anyone's ass. The thing is, there's a screw loose in my brain — I'll tell you, I feel I don't know where the red line is. I really sometimes don't know. So (to go back to the earlier discussion of what is vulgar), I just said that if I keep going it will get politically incorrect. At most I know the red line is there. It's possible that what I call the red line isn't even the red line anymore — it's already close to a dead line. That's possible too.

**Q: But I still think this is a good way to be.**

Shi Jiepeng: It is good. But society is very sinister; I didn't know that before. Of course I don't deliberately want to touch that red line. I just think one should say things as they are. Why be so hypocritical? But I never imagined it would offend so many people. So having ended up in my current situation, I do feel quite wronged. It's because a wire is missing from my brain.

**Q: There is a poem in this book whose interpretation people dispute. You said one thing: "We have our own brains; we analyze for ourselves." When did you develop this habit — having your own brain and analyzing for yourself?**

Shi Jiepeng: Since... it should be... I figure this has been hidden in my subconscious. Since childhood I have been contrary. When my parents recall me, they say, ah, this kid was disobedient as a child. My wife still holds this against me, because my daughter now has the same personality — she bucks against you; whatever you say, she won't do it. My wife says it's the rotten genes of your family. I deny it. Later she asked my family — my younger sister, my mother — and they said the same thing. It really is my heredity. I think there genuinely is a genetic factor.

So this contrarian streak as a child makes you instinctively disbelieve whatever others say — you resist on a subconscious level. Later I thought: why should I resist? I have to come up with reasons for it. I can't just say it's an instinctive reaction — that would be meaningless. So you think, and then you discover that many people say things that are wrong, many people talk brainlessly.

You have to analyze everything for yourself. My own interpretations differ from other people's — partly nature, partly thinking. Just accepting whatever others say, whatever others preach — that won't do. So whatever I come across, I analyze it myself. Of course this is related to my personal nature. With many poems, my own feeling differs from other people's interpretations — why should I copy theirs?

**Q: What is the earliest thing you can think of where you consciously went against something?**

Shi Jiepeng: Now that I recall, there are some absurd incidents. For instance, as a child I would pass a factory that had a cinema, which often put up posters. One day I saw them putting up posters for two films.

Afternoon tickets were cheap, and I immediately, as if possessed, bought a ticket for the 2 p.m. showing. I was supposed to be in class that afternoon! It never occurred to me at all — I was completely off the social order, off the rails.

After buying the ticket I even asked the teacher for leave, saying I was going to a movie that afternoon. Even now I wonder whether there was something wrong with my brain back then. How could a student buy a movie ticket and tell the teacher?

The teacher was astonished — he said, something like this happens? No, no. I ignored him and went to see it anyway. Afterwards I was terrified — I thought, what do I do if they don't let me attend class tomorrow? They definitely wouldn't let me.

And sure enough they didn't, and I had to bring my parents in. Once the parents came, I certainly got a beating.

So I wonder: is this rebelliousness just being different from others, or being perverse, or what? I can't say. As a kid I was like that. There seem to have been several more incidents like it. Now that I'm grown, I feel I've become somewhat more normal. I do wonder whether what I did then was wrong.

**Q: I thought it would be something involving an especially absurd environment — facing an absurd environment, sensing something was wrong, and rebelling out of nature. I thought that was the reason.**

Shi Jiepeng: Instinctively feeling something was wrong about that kind of environment... For instance, as a kid there were the production teams. My uncle would go swimming in the river and catch fish in it. Then suddenly some militia would arrive, each with a red armband on his arm, aiming rifles at the river and making them all climb out. I found it absolutely terrifying, and I instinctively detested it.

Back then, under that kind of patriotic education, (we knew) the production team's fish could not be caught, but I felt the atmosphere was terrifying, that this was wrong, that living in such a society would be dreadful, and sad. I wonder whether that counts as a kind of rebellion. I think it might be.

Because many people would not feel there was anything wrong with it. But I instinctively felt it was wrong. I felt this society was frightening. Not a good society. And the red armbands were terrifying — there was a very grim, murderous atmosphere.

**Q: When was that?**

Shi Jiepeng: Five or six years old — that was the late Cultural Revolution. I was born in 1971, so it was around 1976. The movie came later, because I was already in elementary school, second or third grade. Second grade — that would make me eight or nine.

**Q: In this book you write: "As a child I walked in the outskirts and saw, amid the wild grass, the terrifying graves one after another, and imagined that the bones in those graves had also lived in this world for decades; if they could write, every one of them would be a history of literature." How old were you? It's surprising that a child seeing graves would think of such things.**

Shi Jiepeng: I figure seven or eight, because as a child my father lived on the outskirts of Nanchang, in the countryside. My mother and I lived in the city. Every winter vacation he would make me go to the countryside on my own, and getting there I had to pass down a small path. Every time I passed that spot I would run like crazy. At the time I would think: the people in these graves also lived once. I didn't use the term "history of literature" then, but I thought that if each of them could describe their lives, their lives must have been very rich.

I am a timid person; even now I am very timid. Sometimes I don't dare watch horror films — after watching one I don't dare sleep at night and have to sleep with the light on. I rarely stay in hotels even now, because I'm scared. I'm afraid. I figure some part of my brain must be broken somewhere. How can anyone be this timid? It's truly incomprehensible. So it causes me a lot of trouble.

I don't even attend academic conferences. Why? Because academic conferences nowadays are different from before — before, two people shared a room; now it's one person per room. Hahahaha. So I usually just make an excuse and don't go.

**Q: I thought you weren't afraid, because I saw on your official account that after you went to Japan, you would visit specialized websites to check which stigmatized properties were near where you lived.**

Shi Jiepeng: I like watching horror films, but I am also afraid. There's a kind of enjoyment, but I truly am afraid. I don't know what's going on. It seems to be human nature — lots of people like watching horror films yet are scared. But probably none of them are as scared as me. I am really so scared I can't sleep. It's been this way since childhood, but I never thought I'd still be like this as an adult.

Sometimes I wonder how ancient people lived — there were no electric lights then, so how did they get through the long nights? Quite strange. I get scared; I'm very afraid of the dark. So when I sleep in a hotel I sleep with the light on. So inconvenient, right? So I take a piece of clothing and cover my head with it. With the light on I can't fall asleep, so I cover my head. Then my mind starts running wild — what if I saw some ghost, how terrifying would that be?

Thinking like this, I feel it might be an illness, a pathological condition. But I don't know what illness. I suspect it is an illness, because it's not quite normal.

![Cover of 《亭长小武》, image from Amazon](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180513234600T7C6mgWXSel2V8nq.jpg-w600)

**Q: You were thinking this way at seven or eight. Because the direction of your thinking relates to literature and history — looking back at it now, would you say this connection is something you only summarized after the fact, or is it a bit "destined" — that thinking that way as a child led you later to do things related to it?**

Shi Jiepeng: Because I am probably still a fairly sentimental person. When I see people who are especially... like beggars — they never expected to end up as beggars when they were born, and their parents didn't want them to suffer like that either. I often put myself in their position and think about their situations; I find them quite pitiable. Actually they too have been through a great deal; their inner lives may be very rich. They just can't express it.

**Q: What kind of child were you at seven or eight, that you would think this way — wondering what they had all been through? Did you yourself go through a lot at that age?**

Shi Jiepeng: I should have been a rather sensitive child. My memories of that time are very rich, very clear. That's why when I wrote *The Household Register* there were so many details. Even my family said, how do you remember so many random things.

I remember my childhood very clearly; it's as a grown-up that I can't remember things clearly. This may relate to my personality — I often like to let my mind wander. They often say I write characters in the air with my hand, but I don't remember that. Anyway, not a very normal person, a bit stubborn. And my younger siblings are not like this; I'm probably quite different from them.

At seven or eight, life was quite bitter for me, quite painful. I was born in '71, when material goods were extremely scarce in China, and you felt the extreme poverty. Others were poor too, but they didn't think so much. Of course this is related to my personality, and to my experiences.

My mother was a vegetable farmer and my father a rice farmer; they quarreled every single day, and watching it, you felt pain. They were so tormented by their bitter lives that they couldn't control their emotions. I think most married couples were like that back then. Material deprivation made one person after another mentally abnormal; I believe most Chinese people then were somewhat abnormal.

Including a story a friend told me later: as a child he suddenly heard pig-slaughter-like howls next door — a neighbor had hung his three children from a roof beam and was beating them wildly, just like in a movie. And this friend himself, having angered his mother, was branded by her with a branding iron.

None of this was normal, but in those days we saw it all the time and nobody thought it abnormal. So I feel Chinese people back then were all a bit pathological. That's just my feeling, mind you.

**Q: I saw you recently wrote about your life in Japan. What did you go to Japan to do?**

Shi Jiepeng: Because a university in Japan invited me to be a visiting scholar. (At the Institute of Oriental Culture, mainly doing research and study in law and history.) I had been to Japan two or three times before as a tourist. I think it is what ancient China yearned for as the rule of the Three Dynasties: lost items are never picked up from the road, streets are clean and tidy, and governance is honest and efficient. Everyone is friendly and courteous to one another. The old are cared for, the young are properly raised. Men have their roles, women have their norms. I think this is precisely what the sages of ancient China longed for.

Moreover, Japan is a country quite close to Chinese culture. There are differences deep down, of course, but looking at the Japanese language you find that a great many of its words come from Chinese. I study historical phonology, and its pronunciations are pronunciations of ancient Chinese, so from the Chinese you know you can roughly tell how a word is pronounced in Japanese — that makes you feel close.

You really want to go to Japan and see why they managed to change. Of course Chinese intellectuals thought the same a hundred years ago. Knowing the reason wouldn't help anyway, but I still want to go and explore. That's about it. Besides, I no longer had a job in China; it makes no difference where you live, so I don't mind.

**Q: Is doing this research itself something you find interesting?**

Shi Jiepeng: I'm interested in it; it's great fun. For a writer, having things he is accomplished at does affect him.

I have always wanted to write a thoroughly modernized historical novel, one where I could make every detail thoroughly modern. It wouldn't read like a historical novel — it would read like modern life. No historical novel in China today is like that; the novels people write are all about kings, generals, ministers, scholars and beauties, telling legendary tales. There is no novel that shows the whole fabric of a society.

But the West has such novels; they can reconstruct the villages of an era. When you write the story, you know exactly where this person lives, what neighbors he has, and what each of them does for a living. China seems to have no novels like that.

For example, the Italian writer Umberto Eco's *The Name of the Rose* — he was a professor of semiotics. *The Name of the Rose* is set in a medieval monastery, and its core is very simple, a detective story. But look at the details he writes: the structure of the monastery, where this person lives, how he enters the library, what he sees inside. You could rebuild the whole thing completely with building blocks.

![Cover of *The Name of the Rose*, image from Amazon](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180514000206GD4xNumfjyeiBQbl.jpg-w600)

Chinese historical novels, including detective novels, have not achieved this. I think this has something to do with Umberto Eco himself being a professor. He has that mode of thinking. But I believe that for a writer to produce something of real weight, he needs exactly this kind of thinking. If you're not a scholar, you cannot form this kind of thinking.

The research I do — research on ancient commentaries — lets me know how ancient people spoke, and how their pronunciation differed from today's. When I look at excavated documents, I know what their lives were like: how much money they spent each day, the life scenes of the time, how their government offices were organized, the entire administrative system — it's all written down in great detail. Slowly it forms something very concrete in your mind.

I think being a scholar still has a very important influence on me, on the kind of writing I pursue.

**Q: When you write on your official account you often use "sarcasm," usually at the end of a passage. What do you think of this sarcasm?**

Shi Jiepeng: Actually it's no longer sarcasm now; it's self-mockery. Because when you find yourself powerless against society as a whole, when there's nothing you can say, you can only mock yourself. Because normally I shouldn't write in this style. I should speak more directly, more truthfully. Now I'm helpless — powerless to do anything for society, I can only be taught a lesson by it. So it can only be a kind of self-mockery. Maybe it's also a form of self-protection.

**Q: Do you think using social networks changes you? I'm not specifically asking about last year's turmoil — it's a genuine question. From your personal experience, does using social networks change a person?**

Shi Jiepeng: Social networks do change you, because they let you make many friends, like-minded friends. In the confined circumstances of life before, you could never have come to know so many people of bright minds and become friends with them. That is a very good thing.

But social networks also put the worst, the most unbearable people right in front of you, leaving you quite despairing of this world. You didn't know before that there were so many rotten people, so much scum. Later, through social networks, you discover that there really are so many scumbags — and they outnumber the noble-minded many times over. You may have known it before, but it was never laid so bloodily before your eyes.

**Q: You wrote a year-end summary on your official account, saying last year was an especially bad year. I didn't expect the dismissal by Beijing Normal University to affect you this much. Since you write books yourself, I thought that without that job you would still have plenty to do, and it wouldn't be too big a blow.**

Shi Jiepeng: The impact was enormous — and not just that, because a lot of hooligans fixate on you. If you publish a book, they will sue you over it; there is no recourse for the wronged. There are many such people online, and they take great pleasure in tearing you down. Their real lives are miserable. I think the whole of society encourages them. Even the program Sina built encourages them. You block them, and they can still send you private messages full of abuse. So I stopped registering for a Sina account altogether.

**Q: Do you think these people affect you more, or does the school's dismissal affect you more?**

Shi Jiepeng: The two reinforce each other. Without this incident, Beijing Normal University would not have dismissed me. Of course, with a different (university) Party secretary, maybe they wouldn't have dismissed me either.

**Q: Listening to you describe this pessimism makes one a bit nervous — pessimism doesn't seem like a good thing.**

Shi Jiepeng: The pessimism I speak of is not ultimate pessimism. I mean a personal sense of powerlessness. A human life is finite; you may not see it, but the future will certainly see it. I think the historical process is certain — but you can't afford to wait a lifetime. Within your own lifetime you may feel you won't see it, and that's where the pessimism lies. But ultimately, of course, I am optimistic.

**Q: Lately, what have you been thinking about?**

Shi Jiepeng: Lately I've been thinking, first, about how to make a living, about how to live a better life. And then about how I can write something more meaningful, something that benefits other people.

**Q: "Benefiting others" is what you call meaningful?**

Shi Jiepeng: It means making people who read your stuff more willing to use their brains. We have our own brains; we analyze for ourselves. Many people don't analyze for themselves — you have to tell them: use your own brain, analyze for yourself. That applies to my own writing too — you're welcome to rebut me.

Nothing is that simple. Take poetry: in real life I meet many people who lack confidence — they feel that the meaning they understand in a poem doesn't match what "he" says, and wonder whether they are too shallow. I tell them this is not shallowness.

It is not the case that a big professor is necessarily right. You can absolutely think about a poem and interpret it for yourself. There is no need to treat him as an authority. Poetry is different from scholarship: it requires no academic training; it is not logical or scientific; it is a matter of aesthetics, something more of the heart. You can think about it with complete freedom.

**Q: What book do you want to write lately?**

Shi Jiepeng: Lately I want to write a novel, a fairly short full-length one. I want to write a rather philosophical novel, something like *The True Story of Ah Q*. A novel that expresses a kind of intellectual's sense of responsibility, that expresses ideas along those lines. A novel that is both lifelike and abstract. I have been collecting material all along. But for now, because of the need to make a living, I am still writing other pieces, so there's no way to start yet.

**Q: Would you mind being more specific? You said it would express an idea similar to *The True Story of Ah Q* — what similar idea?**

Shi Jiepeng: I'll leave that unsaid for now. Any more of that and it gets politically incorrect.

![A still from the film *The True Story of Ah Q*, directed by [Cen Fan](https://movie.douban.com/celebrity/1316280/), image from Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018051400065581YtTdN2SBAC5Hhc.jpg-w600)

**Q: The "modern historical novel" you just mentioned — in a previous interview you said it is an ancient version of *The Household Register*. (*The Household Register* is Shi Jiepeng's autobiographical novel, depicting what he saw and heard growing up in the countryside of Jiangxi.) May I put it this way: comparing your *The Household Register* with the ancient *The Household Register*, which is more significant? Where lies the significance of writing "an ancient *Household Register*" — a novel like that?**

Shi Jiepeng: Because it can reflect a great era through an individual — it shows how utterly insignificant a small person is within a great era. In that way you can express your own values, while at the same time giving readers a kind of emotional edification. Because in the present, you still cannot see the significance of everything happening now within the whole great era; but with things from the ancient past, you can. You can also build a framework of your own. There is more creative freedom.

**Q: "Building a framework of your own" means fiction? That is, you can alter the characteristics of that era?**

Shi Jiepeng: It is fiction, but not altering the era's characteristics — rather, you can express typification more strongly, and be more readable. For example, Yu Hua's novels *To Live* and *Chronicle of a Blood Merchant* have undergone a certain reshaping, which makes them more readable and more typical. But their weakness lies here too: namely, they cater to ordinary readers.

**Q: Does the "typicality" you speak of include writing that era very concretely?**

Shi Jiepeng: It means not writing it in a very vague way. With the earlier *Household Register*, I wrote it rather loosely — that is, I wrote it for those more willing to read, for people with richer inner lives. If I had wanted to be more cunning, I should have made it more story-driven, put it inside a big narrative framework, and made its story more miserable — more miserable means more typical. Because in that era, such things certainly happened.

But in that case — take my reading of *Chronicle of a Blood Merchant*: what dissatisfies me is that I find it deliberately trades on misery. It somewhat loses authenticity, becomes a bit cartoonish. It has its merits and its drawbacks. It panders more to readers, and it can make more money and sell better.

**Q: When you judge whether a novel satisfies you, is "truth" a very important criterion?**

Shi Jiepeng: Yes, authenticity is a very important criterion. A novel is a work of fiction, but its details must still be true. Excepting philosophical novels — actually, many people like Italo Calvino's novels, but I don't. Why? I think he wants to express an idea, and specially crafts a story in order to express that idea; I'm not very satisfied with that. I prefer the classic novels. But Calvino, I think, is different from Kafka — Kafka's details, I feel, are very real.

**Q: For instance, A Yi says that when he publishes a full-length novel — his most recent one — he feels his life has no regrets. For you, first: is writing that very, very important a thing? And second, what kind of work would leave you satisfied and content?**

Shi Jiepeng: I am not satisfied yet. *The Household Register* is the one more people like; it is fragmentary, and some chapters still satisfy me even now when I look back at them. But I have not yet written the one I am most satisfied with. What I most want to write now is, first, a novel like the ancient *Household Register*, and second, a novel like *The True Story of Ah Q*. I do regard it as a very important thing, but I know I cannot rush — I have to keep honing myself.

**Q: If you treated your own brain as equipment to upgrade, what would you add or subtract?**

Shi Jiepeng: Right now I especially want more memory — I'm learning Japanese, and my memory is failing. Nothing else needs upgrading; it's basically adequate. As for the makeup of my knowledge, I would like to add some Western philosophy, theology, and jurisprudence. I have read some. But not enough. And that is hard, because I feel my Western grounding is not strong enough.

**Q: Who pushes this era forward? Or is it some category of people?**

Shi Jiepeng: I think progress now depends on the law, because society as a whole is regulated by law. Including our government, which also says we must govern the country according to law.

I think that now we must let the law uphold this "rule of law" — only then will the country flourish day by day, get better, become civilized and prosperous, and only then will our country become more beautiful. The West is the same — those politicians are basically all trained in law, because everything requires expertise, and governing a country is no different.

**Q: What worries do you have about this society?**

Shi Jiepeng: My worries cannot be laid out on paper. I hope our society becomes more open.
