Population, It's a Problem | A 24-Year-Old from Benxi on Her Life Back Home: "Basically, Nobody Even Considers Coming Back"

The loss of young people has become a shared problem for small Northeastern cities like Benxi.

Liu Yuan is 24. Two years ago, after graduating from Shenyang Normal University, she returned to her hometown of Benxi, Liaoning, to work as a civil servant.

It was around then that she began to realize that of the roughly 200 classmates she had shared classrooms with from primary school through high school, only twenty or thirty at most might still be in Benxi. In fact, she herself had never planned to stay in Benxi before graduating from college — it was a twist of fate that brought her back to this city.

"Deep down, I just felt I should work somewhere else, that I shouldn't come back," Liu Yuan said.

Benxi people are leaving Benxi, and when it comes to the young, it's more like "anyone who can leave, has left." Benxi's registered population has gradually declined from 1.571 million in 2000 to 1.499 million in 2016. Between 2010 and 2016, the population aged 18 to 35 fell from 362,000 to 309,000 — the steepest decline of any age group.

This city, located in central Liaoning about 70 kilometers from the provincial capital of Shenyang, is slowly withering.

Before 1945, the Japanese, drawn by the area's rich coal and iron resources, established the Benxi Lake Coal & Iron Company. After liberation, as China pursued heavy industrialization, the Benxi Lake Coal & Iron Company was reorganized into the Benxi Steel Group, and Benxi Steel gradually became the pillar of the city for the next 70 years.

After reform and opening up, Northeast China's state-owned enterprise system couldn't compete in the market; many companies fell into difficulty and eventually went bankrupt, and Benxi was no exception. On top of that, China's economic growth has slowed this year, reducing demand for steel. With Benxi Steel's profitability slipping, the company cut wages and laid off workers to escape crisis, deepening Benxi's economic malaise.

In 2015, Benxi's fiscal revenue was 5.35 billion yuan, down nearly 60% from the year before. Only with 16.3 billion yuan in transfer payments from the provincial and central governments did the city balance its books, barely keeping local public systems such as healthcare and education running. In 2013, Benxi's per capita disposable income was around 22,500 yuan, ranking eighth among Liaoning's 14 cities.

The people living in this city can all feel the hardship. Ms. Wang works as a sales assistant at a sporting goods store on Yongfeng Commercial Street, Benxi's busiest shopping street. Business has gotten harder and harder in recent years. "A few years ago, if you opened a shop, you could make 200,000 yuan a year; now it's more like 100,000."

Tang Lin runs a chuanchuan skewer shop with her family. Because Benxi never had chuanchuan before, business was decent, but the restaurant industry as a whole has also slumped badly. She pointed at a storefront across the street: "It's been six months, and this shop only just got transferred (sold)."

Even real estate, regarded as a pillar industry, has shown no improvement. Zhang Xin, a real estate agent, closes at most one or two deals a month, and the commissions barely keep the agency's storefront running. Zhang Xin says the economy has been bad these past two years. That phrase is on the lips of nearly every person in Benxi.

Benxi itself can no longer hold this many people. [Data from the Benxi Employment Service Bureau](http://www.chinajob.gov.cn/DataAnalysis/content/2017-01/09/content_1269792.htm) shows the local labor market is oversupplied. In the third quarter of 2016, the number of job openings fell 52% year on year, while the number of job seekers dropped 43%. "Steel and mining companies basically did no hiring this year; real estate, equipment manufacturing, and pharmaceutical companies only hired sporadically, and only at some large firms."

All of this ultimately became the subconscious impulse Liu Yuan described to leave Benxi. "Some people coming back to Benxi — I can imagine that. If a lot of them came back, I'd actually be surprised," Liu Yuan said. "Basically, in your mind, you just don't consider coming back."

Benxi is only a microcosm of the Northeast. In 2010, among five cities in Jilin Province including Changchun and Jilin City, the net migration rates of college graduates aged 24 to 34 ranged from around 6% at the low end to as high as 26%. In 2017, the three provinces of Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang together lost 370,700 people — the only three provincial-level regions in the country with shrinking populations.

Back in Benxi, Liu Yuan now lives a comfortable life. She goes to work at 8:30 and leaves at 5:00. Her leisure activities are "very boring" — "eating out, watching movies, going shopping, strolling the streets, taking in the sights."

Liu Yuan still wrestles with it, of course. Sometimes she envies her classmates who are out there struggling as Beijing drifters, feeling that "when you're young, you should still fight for it." If she really wanted to go out, her parents would support her. But more often, she feels life in Benxi is pretty good — if she gets sick, her family is there to care for her. She's very close to her grandfather, and being by his side makes her happy.

Liu Yuan told us about how she came back to Benxi, her life now, and those classmates who have already left the city. We recorded our conversation with her as an oral account:

### The Subconscious

From childhood to now, the classmates I still keep in touch with — put together, there are definitely 100 of them. Back in primary school, of those 70-some kids, maybe three to five I still have contact with, and not a single one I know has come back. One girl hasn't even graduated yet; the rest are in Shenyang and Dalian, and two of the really pretty ones became flight attendants, in Beijing.

From high school, the classmates I know — my class, that is — six or seven of them are still in Benxi. I can still reach about 40 people. Basically, I'd say the whole class was under 50 people, and fewer than 10 stayed in Benxi. That's the ratio.

If you ask me when I realized nobody stayed in Benxi, that would be after I graduated and came back to work. Actually, some people coming back to Benxi — I can imagine that. If a lot of them came back, I'd actually be surprised.

I've only been graduated for two years; quite a few of my classmates are still in grad school, and some haven't started working yet. For the ones who are working, I look at their WeChat Moments, and basically not many have come back, and when they do come back, I pretty much run into all of them.

Benxi has only one second-tier undergraduate college, and it was only recently upgraded to that status. Everyone goes away to college, and most stay wherever they went to school. Like me — I went to college in Dalian, so I stayed in Dalian. People who went to college in Shenyang basically all stayed in Shenyang.

Deep down I felt I should work somewhere else, that I should be independent and on my own out there. That I shouldn't come back. Even if I hadn't found a job, even if I was still grinding away looking for one, I wanted to go out and give it a shot.

Where this subconscious feeling came from, I don't know. Honestly, not many people came back when I was young either — including among my own siblings, not many came back. Of the four or five of us in my generation, only my sister and I are still in Benxi.

My oldest sister works in Benxi now, but her husband is from Shenyang, so the two of them live in Shenyang — that doesn't really count as being in Benxi anymore. She was always an excellent student; she took part in some competition that gave her bonus points, 20 points or something. Our whole family felt she belonged in Beijing. She had the ability and the brains, so everyone thought it would be such a waste if she came back.

My mom feels sorry about it — her daughter, a top graduate in Beijing, how could she come back to be a civil servant? My uncle and aunt didn't think it was a waste; only my mom did. It's that ordinary-person reaction: "Ah, such a good school, why come back to our little city to work?" She goes on about it all the time — to this day she still likes to bring it up, mentioning it whenever it crosses her mind.

I think it's a pity too. In our eyes, someone from a school that good definitely shouldn't come back.

Because we're a small city — a fifth- or sixth-tier town — everyone wants their kids to go to a big city, to Beijing, Shanghai, or Guangzhou, to fight for their future there. I remember in high school, hearing "wow, he's making seven or eight thousand, even ten thousand a month in Shanghai," and thinking, that's pretty good. Ten thousand plus in Shanghai isn't actually all that much, but hearing it here, where we make three or four thousand a month, ten thousand plus sounded like a fortune — so of course we figured it's much better to develop in a big city. Our parents felt the same: they worked so hard to raise us, so we absolutely had to go out, make our way, and come back only after accomplishing something.

Our high school teachers said it too. Back then, teachers didn't want us to go to college in Shenyang, because if you went to college in Shenyang it was too easy to come back — it was too close. You should go to Beijing, somewhere far; once you've gone far away, maybe you won't come back. Basically, in your mind, you just don't consider coming back.

### Shenyang, Dalian

I think buying an apartment in Shenyang isn't much of a burden, life there is good, it meets your normal needs, and your horizons are broader. It's also quite close to home. I think Shenyang and Dalian are a good choice. For me, if I were out there, I'd go back to Shenyang or Dalian too, because I've almost never had the idea of leaving the province. I never wanted to go home... well, I never wanted to leave the province either. In the end I'd still go back to Shenyang or Dalian.

When I was applying to college, I was set on getting into Dalian. In the end all my friends went to Dalian, and only I went to Shenyang — because of the parallel-admissions system, it just placed me directly in Shenyang.

All my friends went to college in Dalian; a few of us were especially close. One boy is in Dalian. He actually tested quite well but wasn't confident in himself and didn't apply well. These days there's this thing going around at ordinary colleges like ours: you pay some money, come learn this Java software or whatever from us, and once you learn it, we'll introduce you to a job for free — all jobs paying eight thousand or several thousand a month. He went for it, and he really did learn a lot. Now he's just started working at a company in Dalian.

There's another guy who's great too — he can talk with elders and people his own age alike; he's lively but not annoying, really good with people. He went on to grad school, and his major ranks near the top nationally. It's a genuinely hot major now with a wide range of job prospects. He's still in grad school, graduating next year. He could have gone to work after graduating, but he didn't — he went to grad school, and now he's thinking about whether to pursue a PhD.

### Police, Banks, Civil Servants

Then there's one more — he's at the police academy in Dalian, and he's a police officer now.

When the police recruit from the police academies, they rank everyone from first place down. Starting with number one, each person picks their post — say I want Heping District in Shenyang — and then that spot is gone.

Among the people I know who stayed, there are maybe a dozen or so police officers, mostly men, because they went to schools like the China Criminal Police University and the Dalian Police Academy. The boys were all interested in policing, and a lot of people from our school went into the police. Maybe it's because the Dalian Police Academy and the China Criminal Police University are nearby, and the really good ones go to the police academy in Beijing — partly the proximity, partly maybe just wanting to be police. I know of eight or nine from our school alone.

These classmates of mine are all police; they had to be, otherwise they'd have had to give up the profession. They chose to come back more often. Because going to Shenyang or Dalian is no better than coming back. I figure if you're taking the civil service exam anyway, it's better to be home than to go to another city. Apart from how developed a city is, apart from material life, there's no real difference — overall expenses are about the same. You earn a thousand-some yuan more elsewhere, but you have to rent a place and cover other costs. As a civil servant in Benxi, you can actually save some money.

And police really do earn quite a lot now. Their pay keeps going up. It doesn't show yet for them, because they don't have the rank-salary component. Inspectors and the various police ranks earn more level by level. (Editor's note: In 2015, the central government issued the 《Framework Opinions on Several Major Issues Concerning the Comprehensive Deepening of Public Security Reform》, which set the principle that people's police living standards should be "higher than local levels, slightly lower than the military." Depending on rank, rank pay ranges from 2,250 yuan to 560 yuan.)

Besides the police, I'm the only civil servant who stayed; there are two or three others in banks. The rest are said to be without jobs, but I haven't seen any of them sitting at home jobless. Those who want jobs still have to go out to find them — you can't look in Benxi.

Employment in Benxi really is poor. There are very few companies. Honestly, in Benxi right now, apart from civil servants, and banks and insurance — the state-owned kind you can get into through the proper exams — the employment rate really isn't high.

Even in Shenyang or somewhere else, any logistics company would at least hire an accountant or that sort of role. Here in Benxi there are very few young accountants; they're mostly the old-timers around 50. Young people my age, or even thirty, thirty-four or thirty-five, there aren't many.

Like my sister — there really isn't any work in Benxi that suits her. Her mom is thinking about maybe going into business for herself at some point, but there's no concrete plan yet.

Other jobs really are hard to find in Benxi. There aren't many companies — look at job postings, there are almost none, because there's nothing to apply to. Shenyang and Shanghai have private companies, foreign companies, state companies; Benxi has only Benxi Steel. Has Benxi Steel hired anyone in recent years? Nobody even knows what channels they hire through. Their performance isn't great either. When they do hire, it's for technical trades on our side of things — driving big trucks, steelmaking — they rarely hire college graduates for professional roles.

### Beijing Drifters

I also have a few classmates drifting in Beijing — I know of three.

One of them does self-media now, running her own WeChat account. The other two keep posting photography stuff — I'm not quite sure whether they're at an ad agency or what. Judging from their Moments, my guess is they're probably at ad agencies.

When I look at my classmates drifting in Beijing, I almost want to cry — the places they live are so small. Sometimes I see the photos they post and think they look happy. Sometimes the things they post make me feel for them; it really is exhausting.

One girl wrote an article a while back — she just wanted to give it a shot. There was a stretch when she was out of work in Beijing, and in her WeChat posts her parents and relatives all thought she was doing terribly. She posted a picture of plain noodles boiled in water, and everyone thought, that's it, this kid can't even afford a meal. Actually she was just trying to make zhajiang noodles; she wasn't anywhere near as poor as we imagined, and she was perfectly happy.

I'm a very easygoing person, very casual. But a while back I said to my mom, I want to go out too. She asked why. I said, I want to taste that kind of life — working overtime until you're half dead and can't even get a meal. My mom just couldn't understand.

Occasionally I do think that life sounds really great. It's not that I dislike this city — it's that I feel you should fight for something when you're young. Because I can basically see my whole future already. Our work is actually pretty busy, but I always feel that when I'm old I'll just be sipping tea or something, and it would be such a waste not to strive for a while — I don't want to look back when I'm old with regrets. But the price of striving really is too high. If jobs get even scarcer later, I might not even be able to find this job, and I can't bear to give it up.

Then I think again — it really would be too tiring, because my health is poor. My mom works, and she makes me lunch. It's still warming. When she's not home, I just lie around at home; at least we have a dog who keeps me company. I think, if I were lying there all alone, it would feel even worse. Home really is nice, honestly. Occasionally I feel like it'd be a shame never to have fought for it — I do think about it sometimes; last month I thought about it a lot, and all of a sudden wondered whether I should go for it.

### The Exam

When I took the civil service exam, it wasn't because the major was a good fit. I was actually planning to test for Shenyang — I really hadn't thought about coming back.

For my major, in my year only two places were recruiting it, and neither restricted the major, so huge numbers of people applied — several hundred at least. When you register, you usually wait until the last few days to see how many have already signed up. Both of those positions took just one person each, but two to three hundred people had applied.

Then I looked at the Benxi position and saw it took two people, with only a few dozen applicants. It felt like much less competition, so I applied.

By then it was already the first semester of my senior year. Campus recruiting had started and I didn't take part. I applied to two banks, passed the written test for one, and then at the interview — I hadn't slept well and didn't really know what I was doing. It was too early in the morning, I barely got up, and I didn't especially want that bank anyway, so I didn't make it.

I sat there thinking, and I said to my dad, "Dad, I really have no goals. On Weibo they talk about people like salted fish — that's me. I'm the kind of person who only studies with everything I've got when there's no way out. If I had any fallback at all, I wouldn't put in my maximum effort." My dad said, then take the civil service exam. He knew too that his daughter really struggled with studying, that it was tiring, and that grad school might not lead anywhere anyway — so let's enter society early.

Actually, if I had failed the civil service exam, I would also have missed every chance to find a job. Because in my surroundings, my classmates had almost nothing besides grad school, banks, and the civil service exam. My head only held those options; I didn't even know you could send resumes to companies. I had no concept of that at all — I didn't know where people even saw those job postings. That kind of feeling.

So I prepared for the civil service exam. The test was at the end of November, and I only started preparing after the National Day holiday. The prep was signing up for a crash course — seven days — and it finished the day before the exam. My score was actually mediocre: I only got 120 points. I thought it was all over; I was already planning to buy the books for the provincial exam — I hadn't bought them yet — and then the next day the notice came out saying I'd made the interview cut. Then it was another cram course for that, and in the end I passed.

Before all this, I honestly had no intention at all of coming back to Benxi. I felt that working out there was what it meant to grow up and have a job, that you earned more outside. Actually, even now that I've stayed, I sometimes think about leaving, but then I feel it's actually fine. But if there's a chance, my mom actually still feels that if I really want to go and there's an opportunity, I can give it a try.

### Prosperity

It's not that Benxi is bad — it's just not as good as other places.

Of course the big cities — the capital, Shanghai — have better job opportunities and better prospects for advancement. When we went to Beijing for training, the teachers there were just section-level staff in Beijing, but the way they lectured, their depth of knowledge — they really were better. A little section-level clerk like me feels that the prospects for promotion and development there must be better.

The first time I went to Shanghai I was too young; I thought it was wonderful — so prosperous, so big. Coming out at night, everything was lit up. When I went back in high school, I no longer had that feeling of it being especially bright and especially big. But Shanghai really is quite something — the buildings are all so tall. You never see buildings that tall in Benxi. Shenyang doesn't build them that tall either. I think it's nice, so prosperous.

Ask me about Beijing and I can't even say. In high school, our school organized one trip to Beijing — the dean led it, with older students from Tsinghua acting as guides. Last year, another organized training trip took me to Beijing again, out by the airport in the Sixth Ring Road. Since nobody would walk into the city with me and I didn't dare go alone, I didn't go.

I have no real impression of Beijing left; I never saw the prosperous parts, so I don't know how prosperous it is. I watched the flag-raising and thought it was wonderful. But I feel Beijing is nice — big and good, all skyscrapers. Some people like hiking mountains and waters and scenic spots; I like skyscrapers.

In high school, I was sure Beijing and Shanghai must be enormous and very prosperous, with everything — everything you can imagine, they'd definitely have it. I felt that if you couldn't find something in Beijing or Shanghai, you basically couldn't find it anywhere. It seemed pretty great.

Nothing else to say — in Beijing and Shanghai you see lots of foreigners; you see them in Shenyang too, because they have exchange student programs. In Benxi you hardly see any, right? There must be more novel things there than here — it's that feeling of wanting to come into the city from a small place.

When I saw foreigners in college, I still found it odd; after seeing enough, I got used to it. Honestly, foreigners wouldn't come to a small city like Benxi — well, you do see quite a few foreign tourists at ancient towns and such. Around here there's just very little that's new.

In high school I took a trip to Guilin. I went to scenic areas, and one day I stayed in the city proper. It felt quite prosperous, because people in the south stay up very late — it felt like it was still all lit up at that hour, and I thought it was nice. Later, going to Shenyang, I realized it was the same there.

When I first started college in Shenyang, the area around our school was pretty shabby. By the time I graduated it had been built up quite nicely. When I first arrived, apart from a few streets — Zhongjie, Taiyuan Street — the rest felt about the same as home. The prosperous parts are really prosperous; the non-prosperous parts are just like home. That's the feeling.

I think our city lacks a prosperous district, and I find it really strange, because other cities in Liaoning — at the very least, no matter what, they have a bustling area, at minimum a Wanda Plaza holding it up, with something around it. Benxi — I don't know whether it's because there's no Wanda or some other reason — feels like it hasn't changed in twenty-some years, in ten-some years; it's always been this way. At first it felt pretty big, but now that it's always the same, it really feels too... why is there not a single prosperous place?

Every city should have at least one place that's prosperous, that feels like a city center. But Benxi doesn't have one.

### Leaving

We used to have high school reunions; not anymore. Our class monitor — even she can't make it back. We gathered twice during college, over winter and summer breaks. Right — only during New Year, when everyone comes home. Now there's no chance for that anymore.

It's like this: from a small place, you get into college and go out. Are you going to come back? You know, aiming higher means you should go out. A step up — I want to go to Shenyang or Dalian. A step higher still, where do I want to go; even higher, I want to go abroad.

At first it was just the feeling of wanting to get into college; Benxi had a strong study atmosphere. First, if I didn't get into college, I'd lose too much face; second, I wanted to get into a good college. Back then I didn't feel like I was trying to escape this place — it was just wanting to get into a good college. But once you go out, you realize it's better than Benxi, and then you want to stay. And after all, once you're there, you're already familiar with the city.

For Shenyang — first, the facilities all look good; second, employment is better; and I also feel it's close to home. In my view, if I wanted to stay and work in Shenyang, whatever food you want, basically anything you can think of, you'd find — any cuisine, entertainment, tennis, golf — almost everything you can think of, it's all there.

Honestly, I still feel like I'm a student. If I'm not home by nine at night, my dad calls me: "Come home now." I don't feel independent.

I've never thought about leaving Liaoning Province. I'm not even that attached to home, but my grandmother passed away when I was taking the gaokao, and my grandfather has always been so good to me. He's getting old, and we're close, so I want to be nearby — and the pressure isn't too great. When I was in college I felt Shenyang was pretty good too and planned to stay there, which would make coming home easy — but in the end I didn't stay. Now I see my grandfather every day, and that makes me happy.
