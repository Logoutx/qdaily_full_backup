We intend to reopen this interesting and worthwhile topic — why big cities attract people — in a way that's not quite so abstruse.

Uber driver Wang Jun's understanding of the city comes in part from the nearly 2,000 passengers he has met over the past two years in Shanghai and Hangzhou.

This understanding resembles that of most people: riders constantly complain in the car about all the problems of city living — home prices that are too high and still soaring, dense populations that make it harder to find a job, a school place, or medical care. Wang Jun also found that man-made commercial districts and residential zones dictate where most foot traffic and ride requests flow (while in other places you might struggle to get a single order). Even though you can always find someone to carpool with, they rarely talk to one another and lack any real spirit of cooperation when it comes to (sharing) the ride — which can sometimes make them miss a train.

These problems are plain to see, and they encourage people to join every variety of "escape from Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou." The media's endlessly recycled special features of the past few years, along with assorted "China's most livable city" lists, have all ridden this wave. The latter are mostly judged on natural scenery, fresh air, and a relaxed pace of life — which makes it easy to understand why Beijing and Guangzhou ranked dead last and second-to-last. Even architects have begun to hanker for the old days: they helped build these cities, but now they see themselves as accomplices of "urbanization." At last year's Bi-City Biennale of Urbanism/Architecture in Shenzhen and Hong Kong, Liu Heng, an architect and one of the chief curators, said she preferred the old "slow-living" Guangzhou, whose human relationships and way of life now seem precious and hard to come by.

City planning may indeed have had a major impact on both of these things, but people don't always take a pastoral, idyllic view of them. When it comes to the relationships a city produces, optimists see breadth, pessimists shallowness. And as for the pace of life — Wang Aren, a young man just past 30 whom we interviewed, said, "In Beijing, so that I can meet more people, I not only bought a car, I bought an electric scooter too, so I can move fast when there's a traffic jam."

> **"People need to cluster together — clustering is what produces innovation and possibility."**
> 
> 
> Wang Aren said.

How can people's views of the city be so utterly different!

One reason is that when people complain about urbanization, their complaints are usually mixed in with grievances about unreasonable policy. What's worth noting is that policy can now also interfere with our efforts to understand the city itself more deeply. [In a recent Bloomberg report on the reversal of urban population flows](http://hk.bbwc.cn/qetmep.html), the outlet cited an April survey by the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences showing that half of China's rural population currently has no desire to move to a city. Meanwhile, the government is nudging people away from big cities with policies that simplify company registration, cut taxes, and smooth the way for investment.

We'd like to filter out these distractions as much as possible. Put simply, we intend to reopen this interesting and worthwhile topic — why cities attract people — in a way that's not quite so abstruse.

First, the QDaily Research Lab launched a survey at the end of September asking "what standard does a city have to meet today to count as 'livable,'" drawing more than 16,000 participants — probably our most popular survey ever. Clearly people are interested in this topic and believe it still merits discussion.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004043132zQ1uZBCva6IOTVA3.png-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004043259dKuLb2MQXw4sS70B.jpg-w600)

So the other version of the story Wang Jun tells may be more valuable. Were it not for the emergence of a new form of work like Uber — together with the city's ever-rising population density, the demand for rapid movement, and the sharing-style interaction it brings — Wang Jun says that at 37, when he set out to look for a new job, he had nothing to his name and no idea what to do.

When Uber entered China in 2014, it chose Shanghai first. [Its Asia-Pacific head Sam Gellman said in a media interview](http://www.pingwest.com/uber-announce-to-enter-the-chinese-market/):

> "The fundamental reason is that Shanghai's user demand and user numbers determined that Uber should choose Shanghai first."
> 
> 
> 
> Uber Asia-Pacific head Sam Gellman

Wang Jun is a Shanghainese native, and he quickly spotted the new opportunity. He drove in Hangzhou for six months and in Shanghai for a year, timing each stint for when business was best and subsidies highest. At his peak, he earned more than 20,000 yuan a month.

At the same time, the opposite story about "opportunity" may happen to those who return home after years of living in a city: they find they "lack the social network needed to start a successful business back home," [Bloomberg adds](http://hk.bbwc.cn/qetmep.html).

Meanwhile, over the past month we interviewed 13 young people living in (or who have lived in) Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Hangzhou, and Chengdu. Their experiences in cities can't represent the whole picture, but they're well worth hearing. Other stories come from people we've interviewed in various cities in the past, who likewise have their own thoughts on this question.

We believe these views are vivid and persuasive. An entrepreneur who runs a youth space spent 8 years in Wudaokou, the "center of the universe," and came to realize:

> "Beijing — I found that beneath this dirty, murky living environment, there's a surging vitality and culture, interesting things, which is what I crave more at this young stage of my life."
> 
> 
> Cheng Baozhong

A former media editor who often reports from small cities often recalls going for a 6 a.m. run along the beach in Xiamen, but she still believes:

> "Media channels and industry resources are all in Beijing and Shanghai. You have no choice — it's a reality staring you in the face."
> 
> 
> Shi Yuhan

An experimental theater creator found more tolerant venues and audiences in the city, and his story is proving true what Edward Glaeser wrote in *Triumph of the City*:

> "New artistic phenomena almost always make their first splash through theater performances, and only spread electronically much later."
> 
> 
> 
> Edward Glaeser

If the cost of the city isn't too high, it is always happy to provide such theaters.

Several young people looking for a partner expressed a similar view: they believe a future partner is more likely to appear in a city.

These views are usually tied to the density of cities and the accessibility that proximity brings. It often shows up as opportunity and tolerance. It may be a bit hard to grasp, so let us introduce the following young people, whose stories will show you this.

### No one can change the smog — it just isn't that important at this stage

Cheng Baozhong, a second-year graduate student, recently plans to make a video about Wudaokou, wanting to discuss what Wudaokou actually is.

Next to the Wudaokou subway station stands a giant billboard reading "U-CENTER 五道口" (the U stands for "Universe"), so the area is often called the "center of the universe." There's some tongue-in-cheek in this, but not entirely — from the Wudaokou subway station, walk less than 10 minutes north and you see Beijing Language and Culture University, China University of Mining and Technology, and Beijing Forestry University; 500 meters east is China University of Geosciences; head south and on your right is the Chinese Academy of Sciences, on your left Beihang University. A 10-minute walk west gets you to Tsinghua Science Park, home to Google, Microsoft, and Sohu, and less than 2 kilometers further west is the east gate of Peking University. And there are also down-to-earth, shabby little shops, street performers, malatang and barbecue stalls — concentrated enough, and complex enough.

Last year, 73,779 international students came to Wudaokou to begin their new lives in this foreign metropolis. But compared with the number of migrants Beijing takes in every year, that's still a very small figure. [At the end of 2015, Beijing had 8.226 million long-term resident migrants, 37.9% of its permanent population.](http://news.xinhuanet.com/city/2016-01/20/c_128646566.htm)

![The "U-CENTER Wudaokou" sign. Image: lotour](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161003211326UEy7xBK1v4k5QAWG.jpg-w600)

For Cheng Baozhong, Wudaokou is close to being Beijing. Counting from when he started studying at Tsinghua University in 2009, he has spent 8 years in Beijing — 340 days a year in Beijing, 300 of them in Wudaokou. His attachment to the place is much like his attachment to the city: it feels comfortable, yet "hard to see clearly."

When he graduated, he was set on going back to his hometown, feeling only that Beijing had "lots of people, bad air, and no friends." Now, asked again about going home, Cheng Baozhong says he doesn't even dare think about it. Air quality shows no sign of improving, and lines at the Wudaokou subway station are often long — but, "I've found that beneath this dirty, murky living environment, there's a surging vitality and culture, interesting things," Cheng Baozhong says. "These are what I crave more at this young stage of my life."

In 2012, Cheng Baozhong and several classmates founded 706 Youth Space (hereafter "706") in Wudaokou, and Beijing began to get interesting. 706 started with just a few volunteers organizing regular lecture exchanges and sharing sessions, then began running youth cultural-exchange programs, coworking, community apartments, and youth travel services. Now this youth space in Wudaokou hosts six events a weekend, from morning to night, attended by two to three hundred people. Cheng Baozhong and his partners also rented seven apartments in a residential compound in Wudaokou, making it easy for people coming to Beijing for brief internships or jobs to quickly make friends in the 706 community.

Last year, these young people formally registered a company. It suggests similar demand may exist in large numbers — they're all the kind of young people who dislike dinner-table networking and like experimental theater and independent bookstores. "Because our interests and hobbies were relatively compatible, and our education and work levels were fairly matched," they all without exception found Beijing a better choice — even emotionally: five couples have already met through 706 and gotten married, including one same-sex couple.

![Young people practicing sketching at 706](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161003210651WFop5MK7EmklI8vw.jpg-w600)

Wang Aren's thinking is somewhat similar to Cheng Baozhong's. He works at a venture capital firm in Beijing, and because he believes "the value a person generates comes from how many people you collaborate with each day — I need to increase that quantity," he considers complaining about smog, car-plate lotteries, and traffic jams pointless. He also refuses to live in the suburbs: "My work is such that only by meeting more people than my peers in a short time — more entrepreneurs — can I land better projects and connect with the best resources." So that he can meet more people, he not only bought a car but also an electric scooter, to move quickly when stuck in traffic.

> "The value a person generates comes from how many people you collaborate with each day — I need to increase that quantity."
> 
> 
> 
> Wang Aren

Edward made a similar argument in a 2011 article in *The Atlantic*: "Dense cities are more productive than suburbs and offer more high-income jobs." But another of his claims — "height (building tall) is the best way to make housing cheap and living conditions better" — is being challenged. In China's big cities, home prices are becoming unbearable.

Doug, who came to work in China from Washington, D.C., ran into trouble. After graduating in June, he moved to Shanghai for work this year, and the hardest thing was finding a place to live. On his first day Doug looked at 13 apartments; he planned to rent a studio for himself on a budget of 3,000 yuan, but gave up on that idea that very night. In the end he raised his budget to 4,000 yuan and rented a studio near Jing'an Temple — the absolute maximum he could afford. Although he doesn't like living on the first floor: "Right outside the door is where everyone parks their bikes and scooters; it's noisy and chaotic, and every morning someone sets off someone else's car alarm, which annoys me no end."

If there's anything about Shanghai that excites Doug, it's the "atmosphere" he senses in the air — the feeling that everything is changing all the time, that everyone he meets is trying something new:

> "It's like walking into a room full of happy or sad people — that feeling is contagious. You don't need to know them or know what they're going through, but the city can give you that feeling: something is happening here."
> 
> 
> 
> Doug said

In the writings of the Belgian historian Henri Pirenne, this is called "the air of the city." In his book *Medieval Cities*, he recounts the rise of European cities, arguing that cities were the natural byproduct of commerce and economic development reaching a certain level — the burgher class grew, the nobility receded, and in the end —

> "The air of the city makes one free."
> 
> 
> Belgian writer Henri Pirenne

### Working together as equals, job-hopping with ease

Born in 1981, Li Zheng has worked in Beijing for 10 years and changed jobs six times — the longest stint five years, the shortest less than a year. Back in her hometown of Baoding, relatives and friends measure a job by how close it is to home and whether it pays roughly enough, so "every time I explain a job change to them it gets a bit weird: 'Why do you want to change jobs? Isn't the one you have pretty good?'"

When she told them she had recently joined a startup that makes coworking spaces, they said, "So it's interior decorating?" "I said no. He said, is it real estate?"

Since the first coworking space was born in San Francisco, it has become one of the hottest concepts of recent years. [According to Forbes](http://www.forbes.com/sites/falgunidesai/2016/03/10/coworking-spaces-poised-to-enter-new-growth-phase/#2a7f497e4f41), there were a little over 600 such spaces worldwide by 2010; this year the number reached 7,000.

![Dream Plus coworking space](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201610032044069Nql7VkA6OI80HLB.jpg-w600)

Unquestionably, this too is a concept directly tied to the development of cities. The term "coworking" comes from "working together as equals." Compared with mature commercial real estate projects, coworking spaces let people from different backgrounds in a city work in the same space at a relatively cheap unit price. At the same time, they're usually located in the hottest commercial districts, close to subway stations, so renters can quickly find a place to work as they move around the city; they therefore attract many entrepreneurs, freelancers, and artists. In these spaces people don't just work — they also hold art exhibitions, salons, and lectures, gathering creative, active people together to share knowledge and resources, like a community. If you're lucky, these people will be related up and down the industry chain, and cooperation starts right there.

As for Li Zheng, she never expected to join such a company, nor to become a "serial job-hopper." But each of her moves looks perfectly logical: she went to pitch Tencent on behalf of a PR firm and got hired by Tencent; at Tencent she came into contact with an angel investment firm; a friend's referral got her through the interview; at the investment firm she served as a marketing consultant for Dream Plus; and finally she decided to do it herself.

Most importantly, she doesn't worry that anyone in this city will judge her for job-hopping.

![A corner of a coworking space](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161003204445HAjQmpVXfg2TNsJI.jpg-w600)

Wang Aren's job-hopping history is similar to Li Zheng's.

When Wang Aren came to Beijing alone in 2013, he had no bachelor's degree and no work experience. The first month of job hunting was brutal: "This city is too big; you don't know a single person, and you have no idea where to start."

The old way of finding jobs through personal networks no longer worked. Wang Aren read materials on Zhihu and found his first job through Lagou and Boss Zhipin. The average pass rate on these platforms is 30%; Wang Aren's may have been under 10% — but without internet platforms, his odds would have been close to zero. According to Zhihu's 2014 data, Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou were the top three cities among Zhihu users; for Boss Zhipin users, the top three were Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen.

Wang Aren has never once quit without another job lined up in all these years. His two most recent jobs both came through friends he met on urban youth activity platforms like Someet.

In his second year of working he moved into a shared bunk-bed room near Peking University — the kind where strangers cram together — and became a course-crashing student. He was astonished to see that in a philosophy lecture of 40 people, nearly 10 were there crashing the class.

> "An experience like that is impossible to have in a small city. I talk about it now like it's nothing special, but at the time, going to crash a class at Peking University or going to see a play for the first time were both deeply stunning things for me."
> 
> 
> Wang Aren

### What I do has no commercial value, but in the city I can survive

Shi Yuhan is a former editor at the New First-Tier Cities Institute. The "new first-tier cities" she focuses on refer to second- and third-tier cities — beyond Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen — that multinationals have favored in recent years. The reason they're called "new first-tier" is that material living conditions in these cities have basically caught up with the four first-tier cities — and setting aside housing and transportation costs, they can even have higher living costs and greater consumption potential than first-tier cities. Suzhou, Chengdu, Nanjing, Xiamen, and Zhuhai are usually counted among them.

If people value comfort of life, like a relaxed atmosphere, and dislike competition and the maintenance of social relationships, they will of course choose to live in these cities. Shi Yuhan's earlier survey of running showed that Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou have the most running clubs but the least per-capita running mileage. In Kunming and Xiamen, by contrast, there are few running clubs but lots of mileage. Shi Yuhan believes this is because people there don't run for socializing — it's a habit of life.

This young former media editor is leaning toward the latter lifestyle. She yearns for Xiamen and Zhuhai, and doesn't rule out "getting a house in the countryside, growing some flowers and plants." But in fact, Shi Yuhan still chooses to stay in Shanghai, because "media channels and industry resources are all in Beijing and Shanghai. You have no choice — it's a reality staring you in the face."

Zhang Yuan used to work at the Shanghai Dramatic Arts Centre. After a while he felt this theater was too conservative and couldn't directly address modern people's lives, so in 2010 he went alone to Japan to study a dance master's way of living and creating. When he came back, he felt he was "bursting with energy."

Zhang Yuan once planned a piece called "Unanimously Passed" with friends: five of them boarded the train one stop before People's Square, claimed five seats, and when the announcement came that People's Square was the next stop, the five suddenly burst into applause together. Such a performance is very niche — it's neither a play nor a musical, "and nobody knows what you are, so how do you sell tickets?"

Fortunately, a small theater called Xiahe Micang in the southwest corner of Shanghai let Zhang Yuan and his friends rehearse for free. A shabby-looking four-story warehouse, it mainly hosted experimental theater and was open only to amateur troupes. Zhang Yuan rehearsed there for several years until it closed in 2012.

A city has enough audiences to share the risk of experimentation. That rising city costs are hurting this kind of experimentation is an incontestable fact. In Beijing, livehouses are closing one after another; people who make a living from painting and theater gather in Caochangdi, between the Fifth and Sixth Ring Roads. But this isn't necessarily divided along geographic space: in Shanghai, young painters often also rent tiny top-floor spaces in old wooden-structure houses in the city center — cramped quarters.

Just as Zhang Yuan was worrying about his next step, private art museums have been proliferating in Shanghai in recent years. Unlike the big theaters, these new venues accept Zhang Yuan's newfangled creations; they can play the fundamental role of urban venues — "new artistic phenomena almost always make their first splash through theater performances, and only spread electronically much later."

A performance Zhang Yuan once designed featured a bare upper body painted with a big X; traditional theaters accused it of excessive unnecessary nudity, but private art museums let him and other artists do 5-10-day residencies to experiment and create, and he'll soon have his own publication.

![Zhang Yuan's 《鸟林匹克》(Bird Forest Olympics) exhibited at Ming Contemporary Art Museum](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004012956eqN86T3uQMGRkw0m.jpg-w600)

![Zhang Yuan rehearsing at Xiahe Micang](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161004013413fwY4S6gCKuE0GNDi.jpg-w600)

Cities present more latent anxieties and a more plural happiness — which can sometimes be unsettling.

Li Zheng's hometown, Baoding, is only 100 kilometers of highway from Beijing, but to Li Zheng it feels very far away. Most of her classmates back in Baoding have bought a house and two cars, hold steady jobs, and have children — living by-the-book lives in a small city. They don't quite understand this 35-year-old woman who's still single: "You work overtime until 10 or 11 every night — what are you busy with? What exactly are you doing?"

Li Zheng is pretty busy. She bought a home in Beijing and handled every aspect of the renovation herself, feeling that many men don't do it as well as she did. She's also had long relationships, but after one ended she concluded that "the way I fulfill my life's value isn't through marriage, or depending on a man... I think I may be too independent; I can do many things myself — I even feel I could have a child on my own — so why must I tie myself to marriage?" She admires the life of a pair of close friends, both contemporary artists in their fifties who have never married; she thinks their state and philosophy amount to a mockery of the institution of marriage.

A classic theory of the city holds that in dense big cities we more easily find like-minded friends: "the disproportionately large single population makes the city a marriage market where it's easier to find a lifelong partner."

The reality is more complicated. Beyond offering more potential partners, cities present more latent anxieties and a more plural happiness.

This can sometimes be unsettling. Hu Die, 28, came to Shanghai two years ago and is still single. She believes you need at least half a year of living together to really know someone. Hu Die fills her weekends with activities — exhibitions, cycling, skateboarding, lectures, photography, bookstores... — and then she found that in this city the cost of meeting people is low, but the cost of truly knowing a person is high. Everyone is busy with work and has no time to attend to romance: "Apart from arranged dates what options are there? Or swiping around on the dating app 'Tantan' — sometimes I find it rather sad."

But some people know how to use the city's tolerance and vitality. Wang Aren met his boyfriend through the gay social app BlueD, and they've been together for over a year. He now openly says he is gay, but back in his home province of Shanxi he doesn't dare, because he knows the pressures and social norms of small-town life — he has to be very careful not to become a target of public attack.

> Gay people are city animals.
> 
> 
> 
> Wei Wei, associate professor at East China Normal University

Wei Wei is an associate professor at the School of Social Development, East China Normal University. Last year he published a book, *《酷读中国社会》(A Queer Reading of Chinese Society)*, which focuses on the relationship among city, space, and homosexuality.

He believes gay people are city animals who can only enjoy greater freedom in urban space. This is because the city is very heterogeneous, full of all kinds of people: the more heterogeneous a big city, the more it can accommodate marginalized groups, and the easier it is to find one's own kind. Gay districts in America are "ethnic quarters," just like Chinatown, Little Italy, or the Black district; they first clustered in neglected corners of the city center, known as "zones in transition" — a term usually also used for immigrant enclaves.

They may of course wait for the day they become mainstream. Professor Wei knows a gay couple who sold their 100-square-meter apartment in Shanghai's Jiading District and traded it for a 13-square-meter room near Xinhua Road in the city center, because living in the suburbs made it too far to attend exhibitions, parties, and activities. "Gay people don't raise children; without the mundane daily grind, they're more eager to cluster toward the city center."

On weekends now, Wang Aren and his boyfriend go see plays, play badminton, buy books... Buying an apartment isn't in their plans, but they're not moving anywhere else, because "if I leave a place like this, there are too few sources of information and I'd feel fear. I have to live in a big city like this to have enough of a sense of security."

### Everyone swarms in, and hope quickly fizzles. But the next new thing will still appear here first.

Uber driver Wang Jun doesn't look 40 — his skin is dark but full of energy. Two years ago he lost money in business, and he looked for a new opportunity, but it wasn't as easy as he thought.

As we said at the start, the city didn't disappoint him: new job opportunities always appear — possibly jobs with no age limit. He ultimately chose to drive for Uber, because it's a choice that requires little investment and pays decently. And maybe he could even land a boss who hires him as a private driver. Wang Jun became one of the first batch of Shanghai drivers to "eat the crab." The choices weren't limited to one: depending on income level and stability, he could also choose two competing services: Didi and Yidao.

![Image: caranddriver](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20161003211049IFju8fPLtkx5WNAa.jpg-w600)

But Wang Jun is now thinking about finding a new job. After this year's Spring Festival, out-of-town drivers suddenly flooded into Shanghai, with car rental companies getting in on the act and taking a slice of the pie. They rent cars to drivers, who pay them 2,000 yuan a month; then the rental platforms started charging a 5% platform management fee, plus a contribution fund similar to insurance to solve the regulatory problems of private-hire cars.

A week ago, Wang Jun attended a gathering of Uber drivers he knows. Some of them bought cars when business was good and are now trapped by monthly loan payments of 6,500; some returned their cars to the rental companies and quit driving. This might be their last gathering. They don't know Wang Jun's real name — they just call him "6865," his license plate number.

Wang Jun now believes the city changes too fast: everyone swarms in within a short time, and hope can fizzle just as quickly. "Before, you could slack off and take home 7,000-8,000 yuan a month; now you have to work incredibly, incredibly hard to earn just over 4,000." He's a bit at a loss as to what his next job will be.

If he had a special skill, Wang Jun might be able to ride the city's changes more proactively. Shi Yuhan recently quit her job and is preparing to work as a freelance writer for a while. Much like coworking spaces, a considerable number of freelancers are appearing in cities. They believe cities can offer enough job opportunities and upward mobility — enough to pay high living costs — and interesting enough ways of life.

But in a sense, like Wang Jun, no one knows when the next opportunity will appear. The only thing certain is that the next new thing will still appear in the city first.
