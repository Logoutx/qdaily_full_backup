## Good Design Is Often Strange

Of the 14 intriguing observations about good design that Paul Graham offers, "strange" may be the criterion that most demands talent and inspiration. As he himself puts it, "Most of the qualities of good design can be cultivated, but I don't think strangeness can be."

Sometimes this happens as an unconscious act of design. Einstein didn't set out to make relativity strange, and Michelangelo never intended to establish a distinctive style, yet the final result formed naturally. Some outstanding works only reveal how incredible they are when you look back at them.

But he also has another line: "Maybe they only look strange to me because I'm not smart enough. A dog probably thinks a can opener is a miracle too. If I were a genius, I'd probably find (Euler's formula) completely ordinary—it isn't wrong, so what's so strange about it?"

**Aeron Chair**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160801183313xhtqnwK439PvajSV.jpg-w600)

Herman Miller is an office-furniture design brand. You may not have heard of the name, but in offices large and small you've probably seen its signature pieces: a family of ergonomic chairs that includes the Embody, Aeron, Mirra, and SAYL, and that has become the computer-chair of choice for many office workers. The Aeron in particular suits friends who spend more than eight hours a day glued to a chair—its just-right support is said to let even people with herniated lumbar discs sit and work at ease.

The strangeness of the Aeron lies in the butterfly-shaped support structure at the back and the high-tension mesh of the seat back: it can fully bear the weight of a person reclining all the way back—supportive enough without being too hard—and lying on it is so comfortable it feels like someone giving you a push from behind.

Also, when we look at a computer screen our head drifts forward without our noticing; over time the neck gets a bit sore. The Aeron's five-degree forward tilt is a great help at moments like that.

You can imagine that when Herman Miller designed this chair, it must have considered every angle of ergonomics, letting function determine form—and the end result strikes a balance between realizing that function and creating the shape.

**Arnhem Central Station, the Netherlands**

Whatever impression you have of most train stations, whatever picture comes to mind, it probably isn't the look of Arnhem Central in the Netherlands.

It looks more like a large-scale commercial complex. Breaking with traditional building structure, it uses twisted walls and a roof to create a dramatic effect, with the lobby's ceiling, walls, and floor all flowing into one another.

The design is said to have been inspired by the "Klein bottle" proposed by the German mathematician Felix Klein—something like a Möbius strip, a twisted, closed surface with no edges, where the surface never ends. And the completion of this sculptural structure didn't compromise the realization of function; what's more remarkable is that many of the designs and solutions in this avant-garde project were proposed 15 years ago.

**Mini Cooper**

In China, they say, 80% of Beetle owners and over 60% of Mini owners are fashionable women—yet these classic small cars began life as high-quality, low-priced people's cars. The common traits of these people's cars: small size, low cost, low price, a certain level of quality, and stylish looks.

In the design stage, a people's car even had extremely precise standards: the car had to carry four people, or two farmers plus a 50-kilogram bag of potatoes and a basket of eggs; it had to cross plowed fields without breaking a single egg in the basket; and it had to reach a top speed of 65 kilometers per hour while maintaining fuel consumption at a specified level.

The Mini Cooper is a textbook example designed to this standard. The direct impetus for its birth was the fuel shortage triggered by the Suez Crisis. Designer Alec Issigonis adopted an inside-out approach. He mounted the engine transversely, pushed the wheels to the four corners of the body, and placed the gearbox beneath the engine; everything from the headlights and door handles to the radiator grille was minimized, and the unibody shell served no purpose beyond protecting passengers and covering the components—there was no decoration based on the demands of passing fashion. Yet this model, which ignored styling, became a fashion darling and unwittingly created a classic for the ages.

**Axor Starck V Faucet**

French designer Philippe Starck has done it: making even hand-washing an art. The Axor Starck V faucet, launched jointly with the Axor brand of German bathroom-fixture giant Hansgrohe, his long-time collaborator, radically subverts the look of the traditional metal faucet: it strips back the faucet's metal structure and makes clear, transparent crystal glass the main body of the design, and it flips the logic of water flowing downward—the water column rises first and then falls, in a two-stage delivery that lets you admire the beauty of the flowing water, as if watching a natural spring.

The two-piece Axor Starck V design lets you mount the handle on the left or right to suit personal habit; thanks to the water-saving valve design, the upper transparent spout can also be lifted out and even put in the dishwasher for cleaning.

Ponder its design principle carefully and it isn't hard to grasp, yet the faucet's form and the use scenario it ultimately presents are so charming you can hardly bear to turn the water off.

**Nanyang Technological University Teaching Building, Singapore**

Britain's Heatherwick Studio, always fond of the unexpected, is also quite bold in its attempts at university teaching buildings.

Take the teaching building at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore, completed just last year. To avoid repeating the conventional form of corridors linking box after box of classrooms, the building was designed as a cluster of cone-shaped towers around a vast central atrium—a large complex made up of 12 towers that look like "giant parsnips" (very much like white radishes in appearance)—bold imagination with a hint of playfulness.

Of course, this is more than an architectural whimsy: it combines teaching facilities with social spaces, with balconies, gardens, and open-air corridors that encourage as much communication and interaction as possible between teachers and students, guiding students away from the traditional model of passive learning.

The project also gives us a rare opportunity to rethink how traditional university buildings should be designed.

## Good Design Is Suggestive

When we read a story with an open ending, it lingers in the mind; when we appreciate a work of art, the ones that provoke heated debate among viewers across different eras tend to be more vital. That's because the creator leaves plenty of room in these works for the audience to participate, and the audience has more right and more desire to interpret and reshape them.

A suggestive design allows users to employ it as they wish. Users are not confined to fixed rules of use, nor must they obey the arrangements of designers and architects. They are freer; their belongings and their spaces are arranged according to their own ideas. This principle is a bit like the question "why do hackers love Lego": Lego provides users with only basic modules, but users can combine them freely at will, creating rich and abundant worlds.

**Nendo****'s Nest Shelf**

At the 2015 London Design Festival, Japanese design studio Nendo showed a shelf made of carbon fiber. The shelf can easily change capacity: its inner layer is a hidden shelf 4.8 millimeters thick, and if you pull it outward until the whole shelf is fully extended, its width and volume are double those of the original state. "It's as if another shelf of comparable size grows outward from the inside," [Nendo said of its design](http://www.nendo.jp/en/works/nest-shelf/?erelease).

Users can employ this piece of furniture very flexibly: decide how far to extend the shelf according to the actual size of the room, or adjust the size of the compartments to suit your own aesthetic—everything depends on the user's needs. The shelf can approximate a tidy nine-grid, or become one with uneven spaces, but the spaces on the left and right always remain equal, preserving a symmetrical beauty.

**Cat Table 2.0**

"Cat Table 2.0," designed by Ruan Hao, founder of LYCS Architecture, and his team, is typical modular furniture. It is not "a table" in the traditional sense, but a set of wooden cubes of different sizes with holes of varying shapes in the middle. Users can stack the hollow wooden blocks as they like, arranging and combining them into different pieces of furniture, while cats freely rest, burrow, and play on them.

Explaining the design concept, [Ruan Hao said](http://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MjM5NzI0NzMxNA==&mid=205720123&idx=1&sn=dcdd285d4fbf44ec081256b97210cdd8&scene=1&from=groupmessage&isappinstalled=0#rd) that Cat Table 2.0 is an open system defined by the user, with its own rules of composition and thousands upon thousands of possibilities. The paths the cats play along and the form of the Cat Table furniture keep changing with the way users arrange it.

And since it is a shared space for humans and cats, this nature fundamentally encourages users to keep changing its combinations (to give the cats a sense of freshness). Both humans and cats get enjoyment from it—they can interact and communicate without getting in each other's way.

**Table legs designed by Floyd****, the furniture company that sells table legs**

Floyd is a distinctive furniture company that made its name designing and selling table legs. The motivation behind their first product was to address disposable furniture—the kind that's cheap but gets thrown into a landfill when you move.

Founders Kyle Hoff and Alex O'Dell designed a set of simple table legs that clamp onto a tabletop; with them, you can turn any flat surface into a table. The legs can be paired with boards dismantled from old wardrobes, old door panels, and other furniture and materials, putting them back into use. Users not only get the fun of doing their own woodworking and assembly, but end up with a table that has personal character.

As people's lifestyles in cities become more changeable, furniture companies should respond to them flexibly. Floyd is a rethinking of home-consumption culture and urban lifestyles. They later followed up on the original table legs with "legs" suited to other spaces and applicable to other pieces of furniture, letting users create more with them.

**Minecraft**

Minecraft is one of the hottest games in the world right now. Developed in 2009 by the Swedish game company Mojang, it is a sandbox game in which every element is made of blocks; using these blocks as a foundation, players gather resources, craft items, survive and adventure, and explore and transform the world.

Some compare Minecraft to Lego in a virtual world. It gives players extremely high freedom and an improvisational mode of play; players can give full rein to their imagination, creating any space, place, or world, and creating their own ways to play.

The game has no clear built-in instructions or rules. Everything starts from zero: you have no tools, no shelter, but around you there are rivers and hills, forests and deserts; you can explore in every direction, and when you're tired of exploring, you can stop and build a house. The game has no explicit goal either: a player can act as a creator-god transforming nature, or remain an ordinary person doing their utmost to survive in a harsh environment.

Though it hasn't yet penetrated world culture as deeply as Lego, Minecraft is gradually moving in Lego's direction—becoming not just a game or a set of blocks, but a kind of tool, medium, and mode of expression. For example, Minecraft's creative mode lets players use all kinds of block materials without limit, so some people [have spent enormous amounts of time using it as a painting tool](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/11115.html), making paintings, creating graffiti and sculptures; governments in Europe, such as those of the UK and Denmark, provide large amounts of geographic and architectural data to recreate streets and cities in Minecraft, [producing detailed maps](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/6.html); and of course it is also an excellent marketing tool—[a pixelated London mayor Boris Johnson recently appeared in the Minecraft world to promote London's game industry](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/21203.html).
