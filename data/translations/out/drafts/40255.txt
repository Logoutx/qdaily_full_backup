When it comes to defining "livable," what young people ultimately value most is having something to look forward to, as much convenience as possible, finding their own kind of people, and being respected.

So how does a city gild its own face these days?

**First, you can grant yourself an elegant title:**

- Charismatic City, Historic Metropolis, the Jiangnan of the North, the Venice of the East, Garden City... These monikers from city slogans each have their own strengths and emphases, but what they share is that you can't possibly guess which city is being described;
- Campaigning hard for the title of "National Civilized City." The campaign methods include: street stalls mysteriously vanishing citywide during the application period, so citizens can't even buy a breakfast crepe; a sudden surge in street-sprinkling trucks and sanitation workers; and, right on cue, mass-texting everyone messages like "The city is our home; civility depends on us all," so that migrant residents — who hit a wall at every turn trying to get a residence permit or buy an apartment — can feel the long-lost warmth of home;
- Broadcasting data and indicators that nobody quite understands, proclaiming "We are a 'livable city,' a 'top-100 city.'" The various city rankings in China differ in fame and credibility, but every city gets at least one or two titles. Worst case, you can still walk away with "Most Promising City" — the consolation prize of the city world;
- Of dubious authenticity, selling yourself as "the place where a celebrity fell in love, became famous, wrote" — only a step away from saying "our city is where a well-known artist went mad"...

**Then, dress up the city with decorations whose only future seems to be self-indulgence:**

- Regardless of whether conditions allow it, chasing an antique aesthetic — painting perfectly decent street walls grey, replicating the ancient towns that every corner of the country already has more than enough of;
- With no relevant natural endowment to speak of but eager to catch the trend of flower festivals, planting a whole new batch of trees — and indeed successfully drawing crowds on site;
- Making a big show of promoting concepts like "smart city," when all it really amounts to is a row of street lamps with solar panels...

**Next, host some large-scale events — and to look classy enough, you really have to put the citizens through the wringer:**

- Bidding for marathons to build an "international metropolis" or a "vibrant city," only to paralyze the whole city in traffic on race day — while running enthusiasts can't find a decent route or space to run the rest of the year;
- Bidding for "national forums" and "international summits," halting construction for a month; afterward nothing much has changed except that housing prices have, for no apparent reason, crept up another notch;
- Trying to fast-cultivate an artsy, humanistic atmosphere by holding music festivals and art exhibitions — but "humanistic atmosphere" and "fast" are contradictory demands, and the quality of the art events is very much... not art...

**Finally, deliberately or not, build some cityscapes:**

- Every second- and third-tier city is busy building subways; the whole city becomes a giant construction site, and the city center has no room at all for people or cars to pass;
- A contest of bizarre architecture, with forms ranging from egg beaters and bottle openers to syringes and giant underpants — cookware, clothing, even medical instruments, a full set of everything, with a truly touching degree of down-to-earthiness;
- The residential compounds and street-front shops that should actually be down to earth, meanwhile, go "international" with a mash-up of Roman, Greek, Byzantine, and luxurious European styles;
- Public facilities like libraries, stadiums, and parks are often clustered in one corner of the city, and the plaza at their gates automatically turns into a parking lot, welcoming people from afar;
- Who knows where the real good food is, but every place has its "food street," with shop signs gathering "the delicacies of the world" and "the finest flavors on earth"...

All these gilding efforts might make our parents' generation feel "Ah, our county has finally held its head high." But in QDaily Research Lab's previous surveys, most young people find this kind of city beautification behavior inexplicable and embarrassing.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704281552382PVTShiL4zuwOmUk.jpg-w600)

On the question of "choosing which city to live in," young people — because they still have a choice — have always been more careful and cautious. One American survey of 1,000 young people aged 18–35 found:

- Compared with other age groups, they are of course more likely to move across cities, whether for "better opportunities" or to "live differently" — any of these can lead them to relocate to another city;
- 87% still ultimately hope to find a city to settle down in, and 78% of them believe the decision about where to settle should be made before age 35;
- But 53% of young people will first live temporarily in different cities, without considering the question of "settling down and living here" from the start;
- 74% have a timetable in mind when moving to a city — for instance, first thinking "how long do I plan to stay here."

Young people in China are going through similar choices, changes, and plans. As cities decide whom to welcome, young people have their own corresponding sense of what role each city plays in their lives, and they have more of their own ideas about what an ideal city looks like and how to live in one.

Because of this mobility, young people care more about their immediate surroundings, and "the city" becomes more of a topic among them. What kind of city to live in, what counts as a "livable" city — these are endlessly debated. Across all our open calls for responses, topics related to "cities" and "livability" consistently draw the most reactions.

As described above, for all kinds of reasons, cities big and small never stop primping their image in every direction — just as we've listed. Young people have "[had enough of these strenuous city-image promotion tactics](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/1583.html)." These cityscapes and phenomena get mocked mainly because they have nothing to do with residents' actual lives; most of them probably exist for economic gain, image projects, and satisfying certain evaluation metrics.

That said, "livable city" rankings are not a joke either. For example, the *Evaluation Index System for China's Livable Cities* includes "seven first-level indicators — ecological environment, health index, urban safety index, convenience-of-life index, comfort-of-life index, economic affluence, and social civility index, plus urban reputation — and 48 second-level indicators." But do young people really use this standard to assess a city?

Here we've summarized the results of several city-themed open calls from QDaily Research Lab, and we'll try to discuss how young people define a "livable city" in the following aspects. If you have other visions of a beautiful city, please add them in the comments:

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170503154140HhkMgvitzcOnfYjG.jpg-w600)

### The broad-brush "macro reasons" certainly matter, but young people have their own considerations

In QDaily's survey titled ["What standards does a city have to meet today to count as livable?"](http://www.qdaily.com/papers/1231.html), we received 19,700 responses. The four most-agreed options were reasonable yet a bit unexpected: housing prices not being absurdly high, no smog, having work you can do, and a commute of under half an hour.

Caring so much about housing prices might not sound very "young," but excessively high prices do squeeze out some of the people who want to stay in a big city. And if it's just to escape smog and achieve "walking to work," there's no need for young people to stay in first-tier cities either. In other words, the first-tier cities where many young people cluster are, in some sense, not "livable" for them. What brings them to big cities, in the end, is the second-ranked factor: "job opportunities" — having work they can do and want to do, and not being left without options when they want to switch jobs.

As for other factors, young people sometimes take a "settle for the next best thing" approach:

1. Can't afford to buy? Then at least hope your salary (and career prospects) can keep up with the rent (and the rate it rises);

2. Afraid of smog? Then forget Beijing — but Shanghai's air doesn't seem quite that bad, so that could work;

3. The city is huge, but if the subway is convenient, that's tolerable — worst case, just try to live a bit closer to a station;

4. And on the occasional exhausting day when you decide to take a taxi to work, could it at least not be impossible to get one, or gridlocked the moment you step out?

5. And of course, if a city has policies like public rental housing or flexible withdrawal of housing-provident-fund deposits that make life a bit easier for young people — and most people actually know about them — that's an irreplaceable bonus.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170428095805CSB7JedhmzQyaEHs.jpg-w600)

### Grandiose city renovations are no match for eliminating some "small but certain hassles"

In young people's eyes, city life contains quite a few "[small but certain hassles](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/1193.html)" that need fixing, and how comfortable life in a city feels is inseparable from these factors.

6. Extended delivery services: "Having an online-shopping parcel sent home means worrying no one is there to receive it; sending it to the office means worrying it arrives on the weekend" is considered one of the most annoying things in life by most people. Whether there's property management or a community service that can hold parcels becomes very important;

7. The intact-bike rate of shared bikes: the last 1.5 kilometers from the subway station to home never seem to have a suitable mode of transport. Now that shared bikes can finally replace dangerous electric tricycles, please don't let every attempt end in disappointment — running into bikes missing arms and legs;

8. Diversity of dining and shopping options within a kilometer of residential areas: few food-delivery options nearby; cooking for yourself means the wet market seems dirty and the supermarket is too far; wanting to buy daily necessities means the supermarket is far, the convenience store's selection is incomplete, and online shopping can't reach the free-shipping threshold — all hassle-filled problems;

9. Density of "somewhere to sit" public spaces: the main use cases are "lunch and dinner both involve social engagements, going home in between is too much of a hassle, and there's nowhere to go"; and "after a meal with friends you want some entertainment but have to move elsewhere — it's hard to find a place where you can both eat and have a good time";

10. The civility level of renovation work: the neighbor renovating is unavoidable, but please observe some basic rules — don't wake me from sleep so that I want to blow up the building, and renovation companies shouldn't dump debris anywhere or block the hallway;

11. Ease of crossing the road: the road is too wide — the building you're heading into is right in front of you, so why are the footbridge or underpass so far away?

12. Convenience of compound entrances: every time you get home you still have to walk forever or take a long detour inside the compound — could you open one more gate?

13. Cashless-payment penetration: if you're unlucky enough to forget cash and bank cards, getting the cold shoulder when asking strangers for money is understandable — but could you at least complete the whole process, from cardless ATM withdrawal to self-service ticket purchase, with just your phone?

14. The number of "things you absolutely must carry yourself when going out": tissue in public restrooms, water from public drinking fountains or vending machines, umbrellas rentable at malls — are they easy to get? Or must you bring the whole kit with you before you dare step out?

### What does a city's so-called "inclusive and open" bearing actually mean?

Beyond the results from our open calls, we also looked at some younger-skewing criteria from foreign "livable city" rankings:

15. Density of independent bookstores: the ones selling *Five Years of College Entrance Exams, Three Years of Mock Tests* don't count;

16. Green space: whether there's grass doesn't really matter, as long as planting grass doesn't mean people are kept off it;

17. Funding for artists and public events: so people don't get the feeling that "everything around here — restaurants, little shops, entertainment facilities — serves the old or the young, and young people feel abandoned by society," or "can't find anyone to hang out with, and in a city this big it still feels boring";

18. Degree of street heterogeneity: don't make people feel "everywhere looks the same";

19. Diversity of everyday activities: being able to find places to walk, run, and cycle;

20. Public tolerance for views "different from one's own": not just paying lip service to respecting LGBT as political correctness.

### Also, "life atmosphere" really has nothing to do with whether it's an ancient town or has a long history and culture

If we make "life atmosphere" — or "street-market vitality" — a bit more concrete, we think it needs to include:

21. Diversity of commercial venues: besides shopping malls, the city still has traditional markets to wander;

22. The trust index of the fruit stall at your compound gate: that is... occasionally forgetting your money means you can pay next time;

23. Neighbor friendliness index: greeting the neighbor across the hall when you meet, instead of only calling you "1507" when they need something;

24. The "bustling with people" index: compared with bare, empty streets, here's hoping for some street-front shops, so that at least walking alone after dark still feels pretty safe;

25. Late-night diner index: a small restaurant not far from home serving your comforting comfort food, where besides the formulaic "hope you enjoy your meal," you can also exchange a few words with the staff you know.

### What details make up a city's "level of civility"?

These days, how would you actually measure a city's "level of civility"? In our open calls we found that what people care about most are these seemingly trivial things:

26. The detail young people rated as most important: when crossing the road, do people yield to cars, or do cars yield to people?

27. Do electric scooters weave through traffic ignoring traffic lights?

28. Do people voluntarily queue up while waiting for buses and subways?

29. Are public places smoke-free, and is there a reasonably quiet environment?

30. Whether restaurant servers or civil servants, can they treat people politely? And can taxi drivers not make passengers navigate and give directions themselves?

31. Can you pass smoothly through places like train and bus stations without being accosted by scammers?

32. The cleanliness of the streets, and the number of trash bins.

In a QDaily Research Lab poll at the end of 2016 titled "Which discussion topics on social media did you get sick of this year?", "Fleeing Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, and returning to them" received the most agreement and was unfortunately classified as a somewhat tiresome social-media topic.

Still, "Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou" appear frequently on social media, in film and TV, and in marketing campaigns, yet they retain attention. Frankly, no one can deny these super-mega-cities' appeal to young people in terms of job opportunities and cultural diversity; nor can anyone deny the life pressures from housing prices and work pace, or the "small but certain dissatisfactions" brought by traffic, convenience of living, weather, and so on.

Relatedly, "what kind of city counts as livable" has become a topic that never gets old.

The pursuit of material affluence is the primary factor of human nature, followed by the pursuit of natural environment, safety, social and cultural conditions, and public governance. Compared with the endless annual rankings like "Most Happy Cities," "Top Ten Livable Cities," and "Top Ten Quality-of-Life Cities," young people may put more stock in rankings weighted toward the material elements of life itself — for example, the British lifestyle magazine *Monocle* publishes a "Quality of Life Survey" every year. Monocle's score is based on 22 indicators, such as housing prices and cost of living, the number of public libraries, and citizens' freedom — from the price of a home to the price of a coffee or a lunch, as well as the convenience of outdoor activities.

After all, when it comes to the definition of "livable," what young people ultimately care about most is having something to look forward to, as much convenience as possible, finding their own kind of people, and being respected.
