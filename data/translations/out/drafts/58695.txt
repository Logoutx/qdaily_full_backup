In the final analysis, what people fear is that biotechnology will deprive humanity of its humanity — that fundamental quality which, unchanging amid the vicissitudes of the world, sustains us as who we are and determines where we will go in the future. Worse still, biotechnology alters human nature, yet we remain entirely unaware of how much of value we have lost.

**Editor's note: In 1989, Fukuyama put forward the now-famous "end of history" thesis, declaring that in political terms human history had already culminated in liberal democracy. Thirteen years later, in 2002, in *Our Posthuman Future*, Fukuyama reversed his earlier view, arguing that "history will not end unless science ends." In his view, the "end of history" thesis was not secure, and liberal democracy would face many challenges in the future — the most serious of them coming from the biotechnological revolution in modern science.**

**"Biotechnology will deprive humanity of its humanity… yet we remain entirely unaware of how much of value we have lost. Perhaps we will stand on the other side of the great divide between human and posthuman history without realizing that the divide has already formed, because we can no longer see the most fundamental part of human nature," Fukuyama writes in the book.**

### The Road to Designer Babies

The greatest hope of modern genetic engineering is the birth of designer babies.

Specifically, scientists will be able to identify the genes that determine a person's traits — such as intelligence, height, hair color, aggressiveness, or sense of self-esteem — and use that knowledge to shape a baby with better endowments. And the genes being sought may not even come from the human body itself.

Experimentation with transgenic technology on human beings still looks like a remote prospect. There are two possible routes to genetic engineering: somatic-cell gene therapy or germ-line genetic engineering. Somatic-cell gene therapy works by delivering new, already-modified genes through bacteria or other vectors, thereby changing the DNA of the target cells. The problem with this approach is that the human body contains hundreds of billions of somatic cells, and for the therapy to work, millions of somatic cells must be altered. Barring surprises, these modified somatic cells will die along with the person; this treatment produces no inheritance across generations.

Germ-line genetic engineering, by contrast, has long been in routine use in agriculture and has been successfully carried out in many animals. In theory, modifying germ-line genes requires changing only a set of DNA molecules in a fertilized egg, which — through subsequent cell division and differentiation — can grow into a complete human being. Somatic-cell gene therapy changes only the DNA of somatic cells, so it can affect only the person treated, whereas germ-line modifications are heritable. This is especially appealing for treating genetic diseases such as diabetes.

However, before humans use these methods to alter genes, a host of thorny problems lie ahead. The first is the extreme complexity of the problem itself, which in a sense means that genetically engineering high-end human behavior is nearly impossible.

The second obstacle to human genetic engineering is the ethical concern over experimentation on human beings. The National Bioethics Advisory Commission, on the grounds that "experimenting on human beings is extremely dangerous," sought a short-term ban on "human cloning." Before Dolly the sheep was successfully cloned, 270 failed experiments had been conducted. Many failures occurred at the implantation stage, and nearly 30 percent of cloned animals suffered various severe abnormalities. If there is no great likelihood of a baby being born successfully, or if defects produced by the cloning process take time to appear, people will be less eager to make designer babies.

Given the complex causal pathways between genes and the ultimate expression of phenotypes, the harms of cloning are highly likely to be magnified. The law of unintended consequences will be mercilessly confirmed: a gene that predisposes someone to a particular disease may have second- or even third-order effects that go unnoticed when the gene is altered, and these may take years, or even a generation, to manifest.

With so many limits on genetic engineering, does that mean that no matter what meaningful changes it might one day make to human nature, we need no longer discuss it? That claim is premature. Before reaching such a conclusion, we need to carefully consider the following factors.

First, the life sciences are advancing at a striking pace that exceeds human expectations. In the late 1980s, geneticists held a consensus that mammals could not be cloned from the somatic cells of adult individuals — yet the birth of Dolly the sheep in 1997 ended that view. In the mid-1990s, geneticists predicted that the Human Genome Project might be completed between 2010 and 2020, yet new, highly automated sequencing machines finished the project in July 2000. We cannot foresee today what new shortcuts may emerge in the future to shorten the research time for complex tasks.

The multiple functions of genes and the extreme complexity of their interactions do not mean that human genetic engineering will be at a complete standstill until these patterns of action are fully understood. Very often, when a new drug is invented, tested, or approved for market, the manufacturer cannot be entirely certain of its efficacy. In pharmacology, it usually takes years to discover a drug's side effects, and sometimes a drug interacts with other drugs or with the environment in ways that were entirely unanticipated when it was introduced. Genetic engineers can start by solving simple problems, then work their way up step by step toward complexity.

The problem of "experimentation on human beings" is a major obstacle to the rapid development of genetic engineering, but not an insurmountable one. When drugs are tested, animals bear most of the risk first. The acceptability of risk in human experimentation depends on the benefits the project can deliver: take Huntington's disease, which carries a 50 percent chance of leaving a person demented or dead, and whose faulty allele will also be passed to offspring. Such a disease can be treated differently — it is entirely unlike increasing muscle tone or chest circumference. People will not stop seeking gene therapy simply because unforeseen or long-term side effects may occur, as long as it is effective in its early stages of treatment.

As for whether eugenic or non-eugenic genetic engineering will spread widely enough to alter human nature, that is a completely open question. Clearly, for any genetic-engineering technique to have a significant effect on an entire population, it must be very useful, quite safe, and inexpensive. Designer babies will be quite expensive at first and will be an option only for the rich. Whether designer babies become ever cheaper and therefore popular depends on the pace of technological progress — one can compare, for example, the falling price curve of preimplantation genetic diagnosis.

No one knows whether genetic engineering will one day become as cheap and ubiquitous as ultrasound and abortion. That depends to a large extent on the benefits it can deliver. The concern most widespread among bioethicists today is that this technology will be accessible only to the rich. Suppose that in the future biotechnology could use a fairly safe and effective genetic technique to produce children of higher intelligence — then that danger would increase greatly. In that scenario, developed, democratic welfare states would re-enter the eugenics game — this time not to prevent the birth of low-intelligence babies, but to use genetic techniques to raise the intelligence of the naturally disadvantaged and their descendants. At that point, the state would require that this technology be kept cheap and accessible to everyone. Only then would an effect on all of humanity truly become possible.

Even if genetic engineering never becomes a reality, the first three stages of biotechnology's development — a more intimate understanding of genetic causal chains, advances in neuropharmacology, and the extension of lifespan — will still have a profound influence on twenty-first-century politics. **These developments will be enormously controversial, because they challenge the equality people hold dear and the capacity to make moral choices; they give society new means of controlling citizens' behavior; they will change our traditional understanding of human character and identity; they will overturn existing social structures, deeply altering the distribution of intelligence, wealth, and political processes; and they will reshape the nature of global politics.**

Second, even if genetic engineering capable of affecting the entire human race takes twenty-five, fifty, or even a hundred years, it would still be by far the most consequential development in biotechnology to date. **That is because human nature is the foundation of justice, morality, and the good life — and all of these will be transformed beyond recognition by the widespread application of this technology.**

### Why We Should Worry

Faced with the possible paths of humanity's future, we need to ask ourselves: why do we worry about biotechnology?

Eugenics is the specter hanging over all of genetics — it means deliberately breeding only humans with preferred hereditary traits. The word eugenics was coined by Francis Galton, Charles Darwin's nephew. At the end of the nineteenth and the beginning of the twentieth century, state-sponsored eugenics programs enjoyed broad support — not only among right-wing radicals and social Darwinists, but also among Fabian socialists Beatrice and Sidney Webb, George Bernard Shaw, communists J. B. S. Haldane and J. D. Bernal, and even the feminist and birth-control advocate Margaret Sanger.

The United States and a number of other Western countries went so far as to pass eugenics laws, allowing the state to forcibly sterilize the "feeble-minded" while encouraging people of superior quality to have as many children as possible. In the words of Judge Oliver Wendell Holmes: "We need people who are healthy, of good character, emotionally stable, compassionate, and intelligent; we have no need of fools, dullards, paupers, and criminals."

After Hitler's eugenic policies — the extermination of entire peoples and medical experimentation on the "inferior" — were exposed, the eugenics movement was banned in the United States. Since then, continental Europe has been inoculated against any revival of eugenics, and genetic research of any kind fell out of favor. Opposition to eugenics was not universal, however: in the progressive, social-democratic Scandinavian countries, eugenics statutes were not repealed until the 1960s. In Asia, apart from Japan's forced medical experiments on civilians during the Pacific War (the infamous Unit 731), other Asian countries had no strong objection to the concept of eugenics.

The second flaw of past eugenic thinking was that it was state-sponsored and coercive. The Nazi Party carried this policy to a horrifying extreme, slaughtering the innocent and experimenting on "inferior" people. Even in the United States, those deemed feeble-minded or "moronic" (a technical term then used for the mentally deficient) were very likely to be brought before courts for adjudication and ordered to be forcibly sterilized.

Genetic engineering has put eugenics back on the table exactly as it was; what is clear is that any methods a future eugenics adopts will differ greatly from the historical path, at least in the developed countries of the West. This is because neither of the two errors above will apply again — a future eugenics will be kinder and gentler, gradually shedding the terrifying associations attached to the concept in the past.

![Poster for the film Blade Runner 2049, from Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181126220029y6pYiCT8XWGq0Aol.jpg-w600)

The first obstacle (that eugenics was technically infeasible) existed only under the scientific and technological conditions of the early twentieth century, such as forced sterilization. Advances in biological testing already allow doctors to detect recessive genes carried by the mother before a couple has a child, and may in the future extend further to screening embryos that have inherited recessive genes from both parents and carry a high rate of deformity. Obtaining this kind of information is already possible for certain populations: for example, Ashkenazi Jews are far more likely than others to carry the Tay-Sachs gene; as a result, two carriers of this gene may decide not to marry or not to have children. In the future, germ-line engineering will offer the possibility of eliminating such recessive genes, so that the offspring of specific carriers will be spared. If such treatments become easy to obtain and inexpensive, then the prospect of eliminating a particular gene from humanity at large becomes conceivable.

The second obstacle to eugenics (that it was state-driven) will cease to be a major factor in the future, because almost no modern society wants to return to the era of eugenics competition. In fact, since World War II, all Western countries have marched a long way toward protecting individual rights, and the right of individuals to make their own reproductive decisions ranks high among human rights. The view that the state has legitimate concerns in collective matters such as the health of its citizens' gene pool is no longer socially accepted — it is instead regarded as an outdated form of racism and arrogant elitism.

The kinder, gentler eugenics already taking shape will become a personal choice for the two parties to reproduction, rather than state coercion imposed on citizens. One commentator put it this way: "The old eugenics required the continual selection of appropriate genes for reproduction and the weeding out of inappropriate ones. The new eugenics, in principle, will allow all inappropriate genes to be raised to the highest genetic standard."

Prospective parents are already able to make this kind of choice: when amniocentesis reveals a high likelihood that the baby has Down syndrome, they can decide to induce an abortion. In the future, the new eugenics will lead to more induced abortions and discarded embryos — which is why abortion opponents are so fiercely opposed to biotechnology. But the eugenics of the future will impose no coercive measures on would-be parents, nor set limits on their reproductive rights. On the contrary, their reproductive choices will be greatly broadened, because a range of other problems — such as infertility and congenital malformation — will no longer be a worry. More likely still, future reproductive technology will be safe and effective enough that no embryos will be discarded or harmed.

When it comes to future genetic engineering, I personally prefer to drop the already overburdened word "eugenics" and replace it with "breeding." In the future, we will very likely breed humans the way we breed animals — only with more scientific methods and more effective techniques — deciding through genetic selection what we pass on to the next generation. "Breeding" no longer needs to carry the connotation of state endorsement; more fittingly, it expresses the ever more "dehumanizing" potential of genetic engineering.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181126220145UoB4NIm9dMGODhRQ.jpg-w600)

Thus, no argument against human genetic engineering needs to lose its focus by dint of association with state advocacy or expectations of government coercion. That said, opponents of breeding a new kind of human still need to spell out exactly what harm is done when individual parents freely choose to restructure their children's genes.

We can further assume that while parents will not deliberately harm their children, they will try to maximize their own happiness. Libertarian writer Virginia Postrel writes: "People want to advance genetic technology because they want to use it for their own purposes; they hope it can help themselves and their descendants preserve their humanity… In a dynamic system where individual choice and responsibility are decentralized, people need trust no authority but themselves."

According to economic theory, social harm becomes an aggregated effect only when individual choices lead to "negative externalities" — that is, when the costs of harm are borne by third parties who had no part in the transaction. For example, a company may profit by dumping toxic waste into a local river, but at the expense of community members living nearby. A similar effect has already shown up in Bt transgenic corn: it produces a toxin that kills the European corn borer, a local pest, but in doing so also inadvertently kills monarch butterflies. The question to consider is whether a situation could arise in which individual choices in biotechnology produce negative externalities, so that society as a whole is made to suffer.

In genetic modification, the child — who is never asked for consent but is the very party involved — is clearly a potential third party who could be harmed. Existing family law assumes a shared interest between parents and child, and therefore gives parents considerable latitude in raising and educating their offspring. Libertarians stress that since most parents only want the best for their children, this implies a kind of tacit consent on the child's part — the child being the direct beneficiary of higher intelligence, better looks, and more desirable genetic traits. Yet there remain many possibilities in which a reproductive-technology choice benefits the parents while harming the child.

### What Is in the Child's Best Interest?

Parents can easily make wrong decisions on this question, because they usually consult and rely on the advice of scientists and doctors according to their own agendas. The impulse to master human nature out of sheer ambition, or to define what humans can become on the basis of purely ideological assumptions, is all too common.

The journalist John Colapinto, in his book *As Nature Made Him*, recounts a heartbreaking story. Its protagonist, David Reimer, suffered a double misfortune: in a botched accident his penis was burned, and afterward he came under the supervision of John Money, the famous gender expert at Johns Hopkins University. John Money stood at the opposite extreme of the "nature versus nurture" debate: he insisted that, on the evidence of a lifetime of work, so-called gender identity is not naturally formed but constructed after birth. David Reimer gave Money an opportunity to prove his theory. David was one of identical twins, and could therefore be compared with his genetically identical twin brother. After the burning accident, Money castrated David and raised him as a girl, naming her Brenda.

Brenda's life was a private hell, because she knew — contrary to what her parents and Money told her — that she was actually a boy, not a girl. At first, she insisted on urinating standing up rather than sitting down. Later:

> Joining the Girl Scouts made Brenda's life simply miserable. "I still remember weaving daisy chains and thinking like a girl. If that's the most exciting thing in the Girl Scouts, count me out," David recalls. "I kept thinking about all the fun things my brother did in the Cub Scouts." At Christmas and on birthdays, Brenda would receive dolls as gifts, but she refused to play with them. "What can you do with a doll?" David, today, asks in a tone full of frustration. "Look at it? Dress it? Then undress it? Comb its hair? That's so boring! If you have a little car, you can drive it somewhere — a real sense of achievement. I needed cars."

The effort to create a new gender identity inflicted such severe emotional torment that once she reached puberty, Brenda ended the supervisory relationship with Money and completed a sex change through penile reconstruction surgery; David Reimer, by all accounts, is now married and living happily.

Today, the differences between the sexes are well understood to begin before birth: when the brain of a human male (as of other animals) is bathed in testosterone in the womb, it undergoes a process of "masculinization." Yet the most instructive part of this story is that for nearly fifteen years, Money asserted in his academic papers that he had successfully converted Brenda's gender identity to that of a girl — when in fact the opposite was true. Money's reputation spread far and wide because of this research. His deceptive findings were echoed by feminist Kate Millett's book *Sexual Politics*, noted by *Time* magazine, saluted by *The New York Times*, and compiled into countless textbooks, one of which cited it as proof that "children can easily be raised as the opposite sex" and that whether innate sex differences exist in humans at all "remains unclear, and can be controlled through cultural learning."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181126220244kN0TQ371vxulMCbJ.jpg-w600)

David Reimer's case serves as a useful reminder of how biotechnology may be used in the future. David's parents acted out of love: devastated by their son's maiming, they agreed to the "horrifying" treatment and lived with deep guilt for many years afterward. John Money, by contrast, was driven by a mix of scientific vanity, academic ambition, and the desire to create an ideologically pointed case, which led him to ignore contrary evidence and act entirely against his patient's personal interest.

Cultural norms can also lead parents to make choices that harm their children's interests. We touched on one example earlier: in Asia, people use sonograms to determine the sex of their offspring and choose whether to abort. In the cultures of many Asian countries, having a son brings clear advantages in social standing and old-age support. But this plainly harms the female babies who die before they are ever born. The skewed sex ratio also makes it harder for men as a whole to find matching partners, and reduces their bargaining capital against women in the marriage market. If poorly raised men may bring more dangerous violence and terror to society, then society as a whole will suffer as a result.

On individual traits such as intelligence, such negative externalities may be even more pronounced — and enhancing intelligence is regarded as the most prominent target of future genetic improvement. Where reproduction and IQ are highly correlated, a society with a higher average IQ may be wealthier. But the IQ enhancement many parents pursue will prove illusory in many respects, because the advantage of higher intelligence is relative rather than absolute.

For example, people want to raise children of higher intelligence so they can squeeze into Harvard — but competition for admission to Harvard is zero-sum: this means that if my child, made smarter through gene therapy, gets into Harvard, he or she may take the place of your child. My decision to have a designer baby leaves you to bear the consequences (or rather, your child bears them), but overall, it is unclear who ends up better off. A genetic "arms race" of this kind will place a particular burden on those who, for religious or other reasons, do not wish to genetically modify their children; if everyone around them is doing it, their decision to hold out becomes ever harder to stick to, for fear of blocking their child's prospects.

Although people worry about unintended consequences and unforeseen costs, the deep-seated anxiety about biotechnology hidden in people's hearts is not utilitarian at all. In the final analysis, what people fear is that biotechnology will deprive humanity of its humanity — that fundamental quality which, unchanging amid the vicissitudes of the world, sustains us as who we are and determines where we will go in the future. Worse still, biotechnology alters human nature, yet we remain entirely unaware of how much of value we have lost. Perhaps we will stand on the other side of the great divide between human and posthuman history without realizing that the divide has already formed, because we can no longer see the most fundamental part of human nature.
