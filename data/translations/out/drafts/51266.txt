"Where Great Writers Gather: Treasures from the British Library" runs at the Shanghai Library from March 15 to April 15.

"Reader, I went to Shanghai…"

Modeling itself on the British writer Charlotte Brontë's line "Reader, I married him," which closes the final chapter of *Jane Eyre*, the British Library's curatorial team wrote this sentence in a similar tone in the exhibition introduction for ["Where Great Writers Gather: Treasures from the British Library"](http://www.britishlibrary.cn/zh-cn/events/where-great-writers-gather/).

On March 15, the exhibition officially opened at the Shanghai Library. In the Charlotte Brontë section, visitors can see the original manuscript of *Jane Eyre*. The British Library curatorial team deliberately turned the pages to the final chapter; search through Charlotte's neat, elegant round hand and you can spot the famous line mentioned above.

The remaining four manuscripts are: poetic drafts of T. S. Eliot's *Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats* along with his letters discussing the behavior of cats; a draft sonnet by Percy Bysshe Shelley dedicated to Lord Byron; five original pages of Charles Dickens's *The Pickwick Papers*; and D. H. Lawrence's letters to his agent discussing plans to publish *The Rainbow* secretly after the novel was banned.

Exhibited alongside these manuscripts are translations of the five British writers' works by dozens of Chinese translators, as well as the different editions that Chinese readers bought and read.

The curatorial teams of the British Library and the Shanghai Library hope that visitors can learn—through these most original manuscripts—about the different writers' methods of creation, the journey a book takes from the writer's desk to its readership, and the distinct personalities of each writer, while also learning about the history of how British literature has circulated in the Chinese-speaking world.

The reason for choosing these five writers and their corresponding five manuscripts was to complement the Shanghai Library's existing collection and to ensure diversity among the manuscripts—novels as well as letters and poems.

"The selection of exhibits is closely integrated with the Shanghai Library's existing collection. For example, our library has a limited edition of Lawrence's books with his signature, which we think is excellent, so naturally we chose Lawrence as one of the featured authors… Dickens's manuscript impressed me deeply, because the Shanghai Library happens to hold books from Dickens's own library, which gives it enormous significance—through this collection, the ties between the British writer and Shanghai can be connected," said Huang Xiangong, director of the Shanghai Library's Historical Documents Center, in an interview.

To highlight these "ties," the exhibition also adds several special items.

One is the original manuscript of an inscription the British playwright George Bernard Shaw wrote to the Shanghai dramatist Huang Zuolin. The letter was written on July 10, 1937, three days after the Marco Polo Bridge Incident broke out. Its content is ardent. Opening with the brief exclamation "Arise, China!", Shaw wrote:

"The future of the Eastern world is yours, if you have the perseverance and the courage to grasp it. The gospel of that future will be Chinese drama. Do not use my plays; create your own."

This letter is exhibited together with a translation of it handwritten by Huang Zuolin himself, placed in an exquisite photo album Shaw had custom-made for Huang. The album's cover is decorated with Moroccan goatskin, with the character "Xiao" (Shaw) printed in its center. At the time, Shaw told Huang: "When you go back someday and have a satisfactory production, paste the stills here." Yet out of his treasure for this gift, Huang never pasted in a single photo.

![The original manuscript of the inscription British playwright George Bernard Shaw wrote to Shanghai dramatist Huang Zuolin, along with the album Shaw gave Huang. At the bottom of the display, the curators placed a mirror so visitors can see the album's exquisite cover clearly. (Photo by reporter)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803200047165XgImdhiYrt3p7Dw.jpg-w600)

There is also *The Voyage Talks*(《谈瀛小录》), published in May 1872 in *Shen Bao*—the earliest translated fiction in China. The story was translated from the Lilliput section of the British writer Jonathan Swift's *Gulliver's Travels*. And there is *The Second China War Campaign*(《中国第二次战役纪行》) from Dickens's private library, with Dickens's bookplate pasted on the inside front cover, depicting a lion holding a Maltese cross in its paw.

This exhibition is part of the British Library's three-year cultural exchange program "The British Library in China: Sharing Knowledge and Culture." Previously, the British Library collaborated with the National Library of China and the Muxin Art Museum to hold two manuscript exhibitions. And after the Shanghai Library exhibition ends, they will go on to exhibit in Hong Kong.

If you happen to have the chance to come to Shanghai before April 15, do visit the exhibition at the Shanghai Library. Otherwise, you can learn more about the exhibition and the five British writers through the text below and the [Chinese-language website launched by the British Library](http://www.britishlibrary.cn/zh-cn/).

### Shelley Beyond "Ode to the West Wind"

Among Shelley's poems, the line Chinese readers know best is the last line of "Ode to the West Wind": "If winter comes, can spring be far behind?"

However, the poetic draft exhibited this time is not the original text of "Ode to the West Wind," but the sonnet Shelley wrote to Byron, "Sonnet to Byron" (its original title on the manuscript is "To ——"). Alexandra Ault, a curator at the British Library, describes the piece as: "a poem by one Romantic poet to a more famous Romantic poet."

The poem was written on an extremely small sheet of paper with its edges trimmed. The paper's surface bears the creases left from having been folded into a square, showing that someone once folded it up and tucked it into a notebook or carried it in a pocket. The manuscript was originally kept by Shelley's wife, Mary Shelley—[whose other identity is the author of *Frankenstein*](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/49896.html). After copying out the poem, she gave the manuscript to her friend Charlotte Murchison.

Interestingly, at a Sotheby's auction on December 16, 1931, the manuscript ended up in the hands of the Austrian novelist Stefan Zweig. In 1986, Zweig's trustees donated the manuscript, along with the rest of Zweig's collection, to the British Library—making this exhibit possible.

![Manuscript of "Sonnet to Byron," by Percy Bysshe Shelley, c. 1821–1822. © British Library Board](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803200049233FRnufEM5SCoINQB.jpg-w600)

In 1908, Lu Xun published an essay on Shelley, *On the Power of Mara Poetry*(《摩罗诗力说》), in *Henan*, a magazine for Chinese students in Japan. At the time he used the pen name Lingfei. The piece was written entirely in classical Chinese; its title, rendered literally in modern Chinese, would be something like "On the power of devil-like poetry." Having learned that the editors of *Henan* favored long articles, and in order to earn more in fees, he deliberately wrote the piece at great length. It became one of the most important treatises of that era.

The earliest circulation of Shelley's poetry in China relied on the translations of Su Manshu and Guo Moruo. The two translators' styles could hardly be more different.

Su Manshu liked to translate poems using classical Chinese verse forms. He once rendered Shelley's short poem "Winter" into a five-character old-style poem, which reads more like a work by an ancient Chinese poet. Guo Moruo, by contrast, used vernacular Chinese. He translated "Ode to the West Wind," but his free-verse rendering broke the three-stress terza rima of the original, destroying the poem's rigorous sense of rhythm.

### The Publishing History of Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre*

Before the semi-autobiographical novel *Jane Eyre* was officially published in 1847, it was rejected by quite a few publishers. To make the novel easier for publishers and readers to accept—and to prevent people who knew her from matching the story's characters and events to reality—Charlotte Brontë initially did not use her real name, instead choosing the male pen name Currer Bell.

In August 1847, the then-unknown publishing house Smith, Elder & Co. received the manuscript. The publisher George Smith loved the story and published it two months later.

The novel's original manuscript is very thick, comprising three volumes; what is exhibited this time is the third volume. At the time, it was sent to the Stewart and Murray printing house, where typesetters split the manuscript into different chapters, picked out movable type, and printed the novel. So look closely: on the left side of the displayed manuscript you can find fingerprints stained with ink, and on the right side a faint line of penciled handwriting—"Swindell"—presumably the name of the worker responsible for printing that page.

"Brontë's writing career began with this manuscript. The publishing house that published this work was not famous before, but became very famous after publishing *Jane Eyre*. Through this manuscript we can see that the author was not fighting alone, not writing shut away in a small room, but interacting with the publisher; we can see, in physical form, the process from the manuscript's creation to its wide circulation," Alexandra Ault explained.

![The ending of *Jane Eyre*. Fair copy of the manuscript of *Jane Eyre*, volume three, by Charlotte Brontë, 1847. © British Library Board](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320005030Q6FZnk5c7ED9gilf.jpg-w600)

The translation history of *Jane Eyre* in China also had its twists and turns.

It was first translated by Zhou Shoujuan and included under the title *The Second Dawn*(《重光记》) in *Heartstrings*(《心弦》), published by Shanghai's Dadong Book Company in July 1925. Zhou Shoujuan was a writer of the Mandarin Duck and Butterfly School, skilled at writing romance fiction. *Heartstrings* was the fourth volume in the romance series "Our Lovers," collecting his abridged or excerpted translations of Western love stories.

Although Zhou Shoujuan considered *Jane Eyre* "a truly great romance novel," his definition of "great" remained confined to the understanding of the Mandarin Duck and Butterfly School—that is, it was merely a love story, with no other meaning. His translation style was also thoroughly characteristic of that school: Charlotte Brontë, for instance, was rendered as "Jialü Bailangdie," while Jane Eyre was called "Yanhen Yi'er."

It was not until September 1936, when the complete translation *Jane Eyre: An Autobiography*(《简爱自传》) by Li Jiye—retranslated from the original for this new edition—was published by Shanghai's Life Bookstore, that Chinese readers gained a richer understanding of Jane Eyre and the British society she lived in. The book included Charlotte Brontë's preface to the second edition, which mentioned the reproaches and slanders the British establishment of the day heaped on *Jane Eyre*, as well as Charlotte's forceful rebuttal of them.

### Charles Dickens, Who Wrote at Furious Speed

Charles Dickens's manuscripts, covered in crossings-out and corrections and written in a scrawling hand, were thoroughly disliked by printing-house workers. The exhibited manuscript of *The Pickwick Papers* is proof.

He wrote extremely fast, revising as he went, partly because the novel was originally published in serialized installments. Between 1836 and 1837, the series of adventures experienced by Pickwick and the fictional club he founded was published monthly in 19 installments. Only after the serialization concluded in 1837 were all the stories collected and published as a single novel.

"The interesting thing about this manuscript is that people can not only see the story Dickens abridged, but also the judgments of those who handled the manuscript—such as the printers… From the manuscript being printed in serialized installments, until the serialization was complete and only then bound into a single-volume novel, people can thus see the entire process of this work, from manuscript to serial parts to novel," Alexandra Ault said in an interview.

![Part of the manuscript of chapter 19 of *The Pickwick Papers*, by Charles Dickens, 1836–1837. © Mark Charles Dickens, Head of the Dickens Family. Some rights reserved](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018032001001551pd0T97zufNsoGJ.jpg-w600)

Like Charlotte Brontë's *Jane Eyre*, the earliest Chinese translation of this novel, *A Laughable History of Travels*(《旅行笑史》), also passed through the hands of Mandarin Duck and Butterfly School writers; it was jointly translated by three translators—Li Changkuan, Chen Xiaodie, and his father Chen Xu (a famous Mandarin Duck and Butterfly School writer)—and published by Zhonghua Book Company in 1918. However, this translation was very thin, because they translated only the first nineteen chapters of the original and, on the grounds of irrelevance to the main storyline, subjectively deleted a great deal of content.

That said, they did not omit the comical, amusing parts, and often embellished them to make them even more exaggerated than the original. This kind of approach—emphasizing plot and entertainment—was not uncommon in other early translations of Dickens and can be regarded as a typical "popular literary taste."

The famous translator Lin Shu translated many of Dickens's major works. However, Lin himself knew no foreign languages; each of his translations was a collaboration with his partner Wei Yi, who would first render the text orally into Chinese, after which Lin polished and revised the translation. Lin Shu thought highly of Dickens, considering him superior to the other two writers he had translated—Walter Scott and Alexandre Dumas fils—and even placed him on the same level as China's Zuo, Ma, Ban, and Han (Zuo Qiuming, Sima Qian, Ban Gu, and Han Yu).

### D. H. Lawrence's Struggle

On November 29, 1915, D. H. Lawrence wrote a letter to his London agent James Brand Pinker. In it, he wrote that he was discussing with friends the idea of privately printing and publishing *The Rainbow* by subscription.

About two weeks earlier, the novel—which follows three generations of a British family from 1840 to 1905—had been put on trial for its explicit sexual content, banned from distribution in Britain, and booksellers were ordered to destroy their stock.

![Autograph letter from D. H. Lawrence to his London publishing agent James Brand Pinker, dated November 29, 1915, preserved in an album of letters from Lawrence to Pinker. Reproduced by permission of Paper Lion Ltd. and the Estate of Frieda Lawrence Ravagli. © The Estate of Frieda Lawrence Ravagli](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803200051443lLzjPXaCOmYTguK.jpg-w600)

After *The Rainbow*, his other, more famous novel, *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, suffered the same fate for similar reasons. It was not until 1960 that this novel (first published in 1928) could be legally published in Britain.

*Lady Chatterley's Lover* was first translated by the renowned translator Rao Shuyi, who published it under his own name in 1936. The print run was small—only 2,000 copies—but it sold out immediately at the time, and numerous pirated editions appeared.

Rao Shuyi's complete translation was of high quality. He was extremely faithful to the original and stated that he translated the novel not to entertain readers, but because he deeply appreciated D. H. Lawrence's intellectual significance and literary value. He pointed out that Lawrence's ideal was to dispel worldly society's mystical views on questions of sex—"from the point of our body's most needed, most deeply needed origin, through the touch of great tenderness, to engender new morality, a new society, a new life."

### The Cat-Loving T. S. Eliot

The Eliot who wrote *The Waste Land* and the one who created *Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats* seem almost like two different people. The former is utterly serious; the latter is comical and endearing. Probably, it's because he truly loved children and cats.

This playfulness comes through vividly in one of the exhibited letters. The day he wrote to his good friend Polly Tandy happened to be February 13, 1940—the eve of Valentine's Day. So on the letterhead he used for writing reviews for the literary criticism magazine *Criterion*, he penciled several hearts, and a man struck by Cupid's arrow.

![Letter from T. S. Eliot to Polly Tandy. © Faber & Faber Ltd. and the Estate of T. S. Eliot](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803200052073XA0Bdpb9rZCOqxS.jpg-w600)

In this letter, he also shared a fragment of a poem about Grizabella, a glamour cat who had fallen on hard times. He wrote: "It's a sad story, and even a somewhat ugly one." Fearing the poem would be too "sentimental" for young readers, he left "Grizabella" out when the poetry collection *Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats* was finally published in 1939.

However, years after Eliot's death, his wife Valerie showed fragments of the poem to the musical-theater writer Andrew Lloyd Webber. In 1981, Webber created the musical *Cats*, in which Grizabella became a major character.

These exhibited letters exchanged with the family of the marine biologist and broadcaster Geoffrey Tandy contain many unusual phrases Eliot rarely used. He once offered advice on how to keep a cat: "When a cat adopts you… there's nothing to be done." And he himself had kept quite a few cats.

*Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats* was originally written for the children of Eliot's friends and for his own godchildren; more than a dozen named cats, each with a distinct personality, appear in it. Before publication, the collection's primary readers were the children of Eliot's publisher Geoffrey Faber.

Although the work that brought Eliot enormous prestige was *The Waste Land*, *Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats* was his best-selling work.

![Autograph letter from T. S. Eliot to Alison Tandy. © Faber & Faber Ltd. and the Estate of T. S. Eliot](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320005226AnY9e8O7GfLNBZoq.jpg-w600)

![Draft of the poem "Old Deuteronomy," by T. S. Eliot, tucked inside a letter from Eliot to Alison Tandy, dated November 15, 1938. © Faber & Faber Ltd. and the Estate of T. S. Eliot](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320005226s2JorCjYfgDNFlzp.jpg-w600)

The spread of Eliot's works in China is closely tied to the outstanding translations of the translator Zhao Luorui. In 1935, while pursuing a master's degree at the Institute of Foreign Literature at Tsinghua University, Zhao Luorui trial-translated the first section of *The Waste Land*; the following year, at the invitation of Dai Wangshu, she translated the complete *Waste Land* together with the poet's own notes, and in 1937 published the first complete Chinese translation of *The Waste Land*.

She and Eliot met once, in July 1946 at the Harvard Club. To thank her for her translation, Eliot gave her an autographed copy of his poetry collection, inscribing on the title page the words "Signed for Zhao Luorui."
