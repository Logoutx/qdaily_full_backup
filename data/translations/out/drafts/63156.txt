What Exactly Is “I-Photography,” Freed From Ideology and the Duty to Document Reality?

@@QD_BODY@@
It no longer takes the precise description of events as its creative standard, but rather a certain emotional atmosphere within vivid experience.

On March 23, 2019, Japanese photography critic Kotaro Iizawa gave a lecture titled *The Private Nature of Photography* 《摄影的私性》 at Hengshan Héjí in Shanghai.

In the lecture, he used the Japanese photographer Nobuyoshi Araki’s 1971 photobook *Sentimental Journey* as an example, leading the audience through all 108 photographs in the album to explain how Araki created a “narrative I-photography” out of documentary photos from his honeymoon trip.

In *Sentimental Journey*, the scenes are real, but time is disrupted. Araki uses a sequence of imagery—life—death—rebirth—to tell a story with “a certain universal emotional resonance.” Kotaro Iizawa believes Araki “wanted to shoot this photobook in the form of an I-novel,” and the characteristic of the I-novel is that the reader cannot be sure the “I” in the book is the “I” doing the writing.

![*Sentimental Journey*. The handwritten title “Sentimental Journey” appears at the top; below it is written “Limited to 1,000 copies,” and in the middle is their wedding photo.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904221354452zBsrKIkUiRNAdP0.jpg-w600)

This is related to Japanese cultural traditions. In *Notes on the History of Japanese Photography 2* 《日本摄影史笔记 2》, Iizawa writes that “the birth of shashin [private photography] might also be seen as a return to the deep connection between Japanese photography and haiku.”

Shoji Yamagishi, then editor-in-chief of *Camera Mainichi*, described this shift in perspective in his March 1975 column as “a move from issues of public opinion to extremely personal expression,” and that “this approach at least returns to the tradition of the I-novel in Japanese literature, that is, to seek trust and truth in the object called the ‘I.’”

“Like Japanese tanka and haiku, they also sing of and sigh over things around oneself. In fact, this may be a sensitivity deeply rooted in Japanese culture; the I-novel is the same, and from it, within the realm of photography, a branch developed.” Iizawa told QDaily.

The emergence of this “private” tendency in photography was also connected to Japanese society at the time. After World War II, Japan’s political, social, and cultural structures were shaken to the core. University campuses and streets saw continuous demonstrations, and society was filled with a restless atmosphere born of antiwar sentiment. Against the backdrop of the social movements of the 1960s, a new “private” form of photography appeared in Japan.

But this was not a photographic form that began with a clearly articulated concept; rather, starting in the 1970s, photographers such as Nobuyoshi Araki, Daido Moriyama, Takuma Nakahira, and Masahisa Fukase each pioneered their own practices, after which critics gradually unified them under the term “I-photography.” In fact, this photographic form was not clearly called “I-photography” until the late 1980s.

The photography magazine *Provoke*, born in November 1968, was a starting point for the development of “I-photography.” The magazine declared, with a style of “crude, blurry, out of focus,” that photography could be freed from ideology and from being a tool for transporting reality. Takuma Nakahira, Koji Taki, Yutaka Takanashi, and others who founded *Provoke* put forward the slogan of capturing “fragments of the real world that cannot be grasped by language,” no longer taking precise description of events as the creative standard, but rather a certain emotional atmosphere within vivid experience.

![*(Provoke No. 2).* Tokyo, 1969, from [moma.org](https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2013/01/25/for-the-sake-of-thought-provoke-1968-1970/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422154040Yj1cQu3gAftKX9C6.jpg-w600)

Photographer and image critic Takuma Nakahira published *For a Language to Come* in 1970. Consistent with *Provoke*’s resistance to the mainstream and to traditional aesthetics, the photographs capture the breath of the moment through Nakahira’s personal “bodily touch,” which at the time was “a violent breath buried deep in the dark night.”

*Provoke* had a print run of 1,000 copies at the time and sold only 500. Iizawa said in the interview: “Now everyone thinks it was an amazing magazine, highly regarded around the world. But its influence at the time was just 500 copies, so I hope people don’t deify it too much.”

The magazine published only three issues. By March 1970, the book *First, Abandon the Seemingly Plausible World* 《先抛弃似是而非的世界》, made up of photographs and essays, marked the substantive end of *Provoke*’s activities. But society began to recognize that, beyond documentary and reportage photography, there existed a “private” tendency in photography that “explores oneself and one’s surroundings, the relationship between oneself and others.” Under this tendency, photographers such as Nobuyoshi Araki and Masahisa Fukase each carried out their own photographic practices.

![Top: Takuma Nakahira, *Shinjuku Station Underground Mall*, 1969; bottom: Takuma Nakahira, *Ueno*, 1969. Both photographs are included in *For a Language to Come*.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904221542016Wvu8lXOqFCYg93K.jpg-w600)

### 1

Born in 1954, Kotaro Iizawa is a Japanese photography critic. He graduated from the Photography Department of Nihon University College of Art and holds a doctorate from the Graduate School of Art, University of Tsukuba. Having “realized he lacked the talent and desire to be a creator of photographic art,” he turned to the study of photography. His major works include *Welcome to the Museum of Photography* 《欢迎来到摄影美术馆》, *On I-Photography* 《私摄影论》, *The Era of "Women’s Photography"* 《“女子摄影”时代》, and *Reflections on Photography* 《写真的思考》.

The period of his graduate studies coincided with the 1970s, when “I-photography” was active; this is the main reason he has continued to research it. From the 1980s onward, Iizawa introduced and critiqued the work of well-known photographers represented by Nobuyoshi Araki, and in 2000 he published the Japanese edition, *Shishashin-ron* 《私写真论》 (“shashin” is the Japanese word for photography).

![The Chinese edition of *On I-Photography* 《私摄影论》, published in October 2018](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904221432266sMrwDRFxlkOy0gN.png-w600)

In Iizawa’s view, in the broad sense, any photograph with a photographic subject (that is, a person taking the photo) can be called “I-photography.” And when the person taking the photo consciously uses photography to explore the self and the relationship between “I” and the surrounding environment and others, that is the narrow sense of “I-photography” that *On I-Photography* wants to focus on.

Therefore, an ordinary family’s photo album belongs to “I-photography” in the broad sense. And as with the Japanese photographer Shoji Ueda, who “consciously and spontaneously wanted to photograph family members” and through photography “explored the ties between himself and his family, his clan,” that is “I-photography” in the narrow sense.

![Shoji Ueda, *Dad, Mom and their children*, 1949, 20.4 x 27.8 cm, gelatin silver print © Shoji Ueda Office, courtesy: Shoji Ueda Office and Three Shadows Photography Art Centre via [Three Shadows](https://www.threeshadows.cn/cn/exhibitions/70/works/image1124/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422143415sJyS8eWzVF4utY62.jpg-w600)

![Shoji Ueda, *Scenery of the dune with my wife*, 1950, 22.0 x 28.0 cm, gelatin silver print © Shoji Ueda Office, courtesy: Shoji Ueda Office and Three Shadows Photography Art Centre via Three Shadows](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422143258aoGQki4z6VBOn3Wx.jpg-w600)

If we turn the question around and ask, “Is there any photography in the world that is not ‘I-photography’?” Iizawa thinks a bank surveillance camera might not be, because there is no one behind the monitor (no photographic subject); apart from that, almost everything is “I-photography” in the broad sense. He cites the artist Xu Bing’s work *Dragonfly Eyes* as an example. This is a “feature-length film” that uses a large amount of surveillance footage from the internet, edited and re-dubbed to tell a story. Iizawa finds this a very interesting work: “As a kind of ‘anti-photography’ device… there is no one operating it, yet it has a kind of autonomy.”

But he is not trying to propose “the one correct answer”; rather, he wants to “positively grasp the polysemy and breadth of meaning attached to the term ‘I-photography’” and express it. To define “I-photography” and clarify its possibilities, “the first thing that must be done is to investigate and study the creative acts of several photographers.” In *On I-Photography*, he selects four photographers—Takuma Nakahira, Masahisa Fukase, Nobuyoshi Araki, and Shigeo Gocho—and through their lives, creative backgrounds, contemporary criticism, the photographers’ own interpretations, and the attitudes of those around them, attempts to show the possibilities of “I-photography.”

These four are photographers Iizawa has long followed and critiqued; they occupy very solid positions in the history of Japanese photographic expression, “sometimes even to the extent of causing history’s direction to shift dramatically.”

Born in 1934, Masahisa Fukase’s creative career underwent a very clear stylistic transformation. In Iizawa’s view, he was a photographer possessed by “inexplicable impulses”; “no other photographer could so devotedly, almost self-destructively, cling to photography as a means of expression.”

Iizawa sees Fukase’s creative process from the *Pig Slaughter* 《杀猪》 solo exhibition and the photobooks *Game*, *Ravens*, *Family*, and *Memories of My Father* 《父亲的记忆》 as a process in which “something was disappearing bit by bit from his photographic world.” If this disappearing thing is viewed in concrete terms, it was his wife Yoko, the deep loneliness inside him, and his hometown and family. For Fukase, photography “was a lamp illuminating the darkness, the plumb bob at the end of the rope measuring the depth of the well,” and he was using photography to search for the self (the “plumb bob”). In the series *Private Scenes 1992* 《私景 1992》, created before Fukase fell down the stairs while drunk and lost consciousness in 1992, he put his own face or parts of his body in the frame, stating, “Everything captured can be said to be a reflection of myself, a ‘private scene.’”

These works of Fukase are seen by Iizawa as “a certain unique, thorough attempt to explore I-photography”: “If one did not take that kind of risk, one probably could not see the true face of that tiresome yet utterly bewitching monster.”

### 2

Among “I-photography” photographers, Nobuyoshi Araki is probably the best known to the general public. His practice in the field of “I-photography” has a “unique breadth,” and he is also the only one of the four photographers in the book who is still actively shooting. Because Araki has produced a huge volume of work, the photobooks and dialogue collections published before 2000 already number nearly 200. Even analyzing Araki solely from the dimension of “I-photography” is no easy task. In the book, Iizawa chooses two aspects to discuss his “I-photography” practice—Araki photographing his own wife, and Araki placing his own image into his photographic works.

Born in 1940 in a working-class district of Tokyo, Araki joined the photography department of the advertising agency Dentsu after graduation. He met his wife Yoko at Dentsu; the two married in 1971. Araki’s *Sentimental Journey*, which announced the birth of Japanese “I-photography,” was shot during their honeymoon trip.

This is not a photobook that simply depicts what the newlyweds saw. In the preface to *Sentimental Journey*, in a handwritten epistolary “I-Novel Manifesto” on the left side, Araki wrote: “I am not saying that because I photographed my own honeymoon trip, these are real photographs… I have always felt that the I-novel is the most fitting description of my photography. Moreover, I believe the I-novel is what is closest to photography. I have simply arranged the photographs according to the honeymoon itinerary as it was.”

This is also the greatest characteristic of Araki’s “I-photography”—the indistinguishable confusion between private life and story. In his later work, he even directly mixed real dates with false dates by adjusting the numbered dial, as in the 1980 publication *Nobuyoshi Araki’s Fake Diary* 《荒木经惟的伪日记》, where photos dated “April 1, April Fool’s Day” and “August 15, Japan’s WWII surrender anniversary” are surprisingly numerous.

In *Sentimental Journey*, Araki removed the joy of the honeymoon trip and instead selected many images with a kind of “expression immersed in sorrow.” He even inserted a section depicting “death” in the middle of the photobook, choosing images such as a half-eaten apple like a skull, a bench like a stone coffin, and a boat drifting downstream, punctuating it with imagery of death (the other world).

And in this album, very private sexual images also appear many times. This is one reason some people one-sidedly misread “I-photography” as merely nude or sexual photography. For “I-photography,” “whether sexual images appear is judged solely by whether they are necessary to the story.”

Iizawa sees the sexual images in *Sentimental Journey* as “a ritual that brings Yoko back from the world of the dead to the world of the living.” The famous photo of Yoko curled up and lying in a boat is interpreted as “Yoko as the dead.” Therefore, “the ‘sex’ in Araki’s photographs does not exist simply to represent ‘sex’; it is there because it is necessary to the story.”

![At the lecture, Iizawa said: “This is a honeymoon trip, so Yoko should be happy, but in the photos she shows no sign of happiness at all. There is not a single photo of her smiling in this photobook. But in the color photos from the honeymoon trip that were later made public, Yoko is smiling very happily.”](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422143653AwD2pEvJLUi1bynR.jpg-w600)

![“Actually, he was photographing a stone bench, but it looks especially like a coffin. Like this, there is an enormous amount of imagery of death.”](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422143653XM4Gu8J7cjOD9QUI.jpg-w600)

![At the lecture, Iizawa said: “Yoko sleeping in the boat. Judging by the progression of the story, this is Yoko as the dead, and her pose is also consistent with that of a fetus floating in amniotic fluid in its mother’s womb, but in fact she was a 23-year-old young bride.”](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904221436534mnYR0FhlZAiuOIL.jpg-w600)

Inserting sexual images within such a narrative flow—I think this also demonstrates Araki’s unique narrative ability.”](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422143658Dy6Fiuebh2gClRWV.jpg-w600)

Yoko was the first and finest critic of Araki’s photographic act. She did not mind that images of herself appeared so frequently in Araki’s photos; rather, she “observed the self shown in the photos and her relationship with Nobuyoshi Araki and repeatedly thought about them.” Regarding Araki’s photo of her hand holding a summer orange, Yoko felt “the scene of him across from me also eating a summer orange and pressing the shutter directly with his hand still smelling of summer orange… He is not photographing me, but the dense emotion floating between him and me.”

Iizawa believes that in such a relationship of “photographing and being photographed,” neither side holds the upper hand, nor is there a one-sided relationship of one giving and the other receiving.

However, even Yoko had a conflicted psychology between “not wanting to be photographed anymore” and “feeling lonely if I were no longer photographed.” In *My Love Life*, she wrote: “The latter feeling always seems like a kind of heartless abuse. The relationship between man and woman and the relationship between photographing and being photographed overlap so deeply, experienced as this dual profound bond, that I begin to worry whether I can no longer be satisfied in the originally simple relationship.”

Yoko, the wife of another “I-photography” photographer, Masahisa Fukase, also voiced a similar complaint (more like an accusation): “For ten years, while living with me, he merely gazed at me through the lens. The me he photographed was, without doubt, nothing more than himself.”

The photographers’ wives raised the question of whether “I-photography” is a form of creative desire tinged with “selfishness.” Iizawa believes that I-photography is indeed a “selfish” act, but this needs to be understood from both negative and positive sides.

He told QDaily: “Negatively speaking, like the artistic creative acts of artists, it is indeed a bother and a disturbance to the people around them. But positively, because of the artist’s selfish act, artistic breakthroughs can indeed be achieved, which is also a good thing… But you can’t use that to negate their artistic creation. If you were to criticize such selfish artistic creation, there would be no art left.”

### 3

Another special feature of Nobuyoshi Araki’s “I-photography” is that he always appears in his own photographic works. *Sentimental Journey: Winter Journey* includes Araki in the wedding-ceremony commemorative photo, Araki wearing a paper mask in the hospital room, Araki holding Yoko’s memorial portrait at the funeral, and Araki carrying the urn home on the Odakyu Line. He does not hesitate to include such photos in his photobooks, and we who view the photos accept this behavior without hesitation.

If we strictly regard “the person who presses the shutter” as the photographer, it would be inappropriate to see such photos as Araki’s works. Then why do “we” accept them? Iizawa believes that the “Arakis” who appear in the photographs are in fact a symbol of Araki. And his trademark black-rimmed glasses and mustache “are probably meant to make himself thoroughly symbolized.”

![Included in Nobuyoshi Araki’s *Sentimental Journey: Winter Journey*; the person in the photo is Nobuyoshi Araki.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904221557509Z3DtW4qC5vwThaX.png-w600)

This tendency is even stronger in *Nobuyoshi Araki’s Fake Diary*: out of the album’s 187 photographs, 19 show Araki himself. By the 1990s, Araki had chosen other forms of alter egos to enter his works—models of dinosaurs, lizards, and crocodiles called “Komodo.” Komodo sometimes looks down at the streets from a hotel balcony, sometimes is placed beside Beethoven’s grave, and sometimes is stuffed between a voluptuous woman’s legs. In the *A’s Paradise* 《A 的乐园》 series, Komodo even attains the status of protagonist. “As long as I photograph them, that place directly transforms into ‘A’s Paradise,’ because ‘what is being photographed is me.’” Araki splits himself, like the Invisible Man, effortlessly permeating “the human world, the era, the subject,” completing a process of assimilation.

“Object and symbol, Nobuyoshi Araki and ‘Araki’—he deliberately swaps their positions within the photograph, making the boundary between them ambiguous. It is through this photographic act that he continually poses the question: Does a true, unique ‘I’ really exist? There is no ‘real face’ behind the mask. If you try to forcibly tear off the mask, you will only tear away the flesh deeply embedded beneath it. On the contrary, whatever mask one wears, while it is worn, it becomes living skin with blood flowing through it, gradually sticking to the ‘I.’”

![Both photographs are included in Nobuyoshi Araki’s *ARAKI IN WIEN: Tokyo Paradise*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422155811rT49xSYg5ndVeOhl.jpg-w600)

Araki thus walked a path of exploring the self, then making himself disappear through symbolization and alter egos. “The ideal state of I-photography is, while exploring oneself, to make oneself disappear.” Iizawa explains this disappearance as the concept of “nothingness” in Eastern philosophy. He believes: “Works like that—first pursuing the self, then trying to erase the self—are far more interesting than things that are ‘nothing’ from the start.”

But this ideal state is hard to reach. “The more you ask about the self, the more you search for the self, the less you know who you are.” And the four photographers in the book all failed to reach this most ideal state; in the later stages of their I-photography practice, “Takuma Nakahira lost his memory, Masahisa Fukase was engaged in almost suicidal behavior, and Shigeo Gocho died very young.”

And Araki, who continued to “dream of erasing the self,” was also publicly accused last year by KaoRi, a model he had photographed for 16 years. KaoRi published a long article titled [“Is This Kind of Knowledge Really Correct?”](https://www.douban.com/note/667224718/), in which she mentioned being “forced to pose nude,” being compelled to shoot nude photos in front of many onlookers, and Araki using explicit titles such as “KaoRi’s Sex Diary” without consulting her. These acts caused KaoRi mental stress and led to her being “followed, having people break into her home, having her photobooks stolen, her garbage rummaged through, and her mailbox stuffed with uncancelled creepy postcards.” Under the influence of the American #MeToo movement, KaoRi “finally felt there was no need to continue dedicating herself to the fiction he had constructed.”

![From [realkyoto.jp](http://realkyoto.jp/article/izawa-kotaro/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422142018RH9wg3K72WdSEAfO.png-w600)

Iizawa wrote an [article](http://realkyoto.jp/en/article/araki_iizawa/) in 2018 stating his views on this incident. While reading KaoRi’s article, Iizawa’s strongest feeling was that the times had changed. In his QDaily interview, he also mentioned the transformation of the relationship between Araki and his models under changing times.

“In the early days, Araki had a very good relationship with the models around him, because the models wanted to express themselves, and at that time Araki also needed their expression, so their relationship was harmonious. In the 1990s, young girls would seek Araki out on their own, hoping he would photograph them, including nude photos; at that time he was very popular. Now the biggest problem with KaoRi is that after 2000, what the models needed and what Araki wanted had already diverged; they were no longer in agreement, which is why this problem arose.”

KaoRi, who began working with Araki in 2001, was asked to participate in the shoots. “When Araki was creating in the 1990s, the relationship between Araki and the models was more like a collaborative work, making a piece together… Now Araki’s approach may not be a good one; he forcibly demands that models do such things, which is not as good as the earlier collaborative work. In that there are actually two subjects—the model and the photographer—colliding. But the state between the early Araki and Fukase and their wives was one of collaborative work; that state was relatively ideal.”

### 4

The Chinese edition of *On I-Photography* 《私摄影论》 came out in October 2018. In the 18 years since the Japanese edition was published, the meaning of photography has changed considerably with the changes in media and equipment. Photography moved from material silver halide to digital sensors, and in the past decade to mobile phones. Although Iizawa’s summary of I-photography as “uncontrollable, irreversible, open” has not changed much, “because of certain internet characteristics, it has become more diffuse and easier to spread.”

The internet amplifies the speed of image circulation. Iizawa also mentioned the role of the internet in his [post](http://realkyoto.jp/en/article/araki_iizawa/) about the KaoRi incident. “Another thing that has become prominent in the digital age is that the choice to be nude is more dangerous than before. Even a one-time adventure can lead to unexpected trouble because of online diffusion.”

After photography’s display media evolved through photobooks and exhibitions, today everyone can hold a small screen in their hand. In the current online environment, everyone can disseminate their own “I-photography” (in the broad sense), and everyone can shape their own image on social media. Iizawa believes this expression is similar to how, decades ago, Nobuyoshi Araki “symbolized” himself. He said in the interview: “When people in the digital age shoot I-photography, they are symbolizing themselves. For example, on social media, those ‘I’s are not really ‘I’s; they are just symbolized ‘I’s.”

But *On I-Photography*’s discussion stops at 2000; Iizawa himself does not use social media often, and he hopes younger generations will research “I-photography” in the internet age, the mobile-phone age.

![Shigeo Gocho, *SELF AND OTHERS*. This photobook contains 60 photographs in total; the image shows the cover and the first five.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190422155945EjHSxfZhDVwikzvB.jpg-w600)

Because of this 18-year gap, Iizawa feels that, viewed today, *On I-Photography* exists as a classic, and publishing the Chinese edition of *On I-Photography* at this time is very significant. In recent years, Iizawa has often been invited to China to give lectures on “I-photography,” and last year the Three Shadows Photography Art Centre in Beijing also held a Shoji Ueda retrospective. He feels that “the current state of photography in China is on a parallel line with Japan’s [photography] at that time.”

Under the changes of social transformation, people have begun to explore the self more, and the convenience of photography has given more people the possibility of exploring through photography. In professor Gu Zheng of the School of Journalism at Fudan University’s [*“Photography Is Your Private Life; Private Life Is in Your Photography” — On Chinese I-Photography*](https://www.douban.com/group/topic/2764143/?author=1) 《“摄影，就是你的私生活；私生活，就在你的摄影中!” ——中国私摄影论》, published in *Art Today* 《今日美术》 in 2006, he attributes the social conditions for the emergence of Chinese I-photography to “the transformation of Chinese society into a mass consumer society after the mid-1990s.” Amid changes in social ideology and values, a new generation of Chinese youth who grew up alongside mass media began to express their desire for self-expression primarily through photography.

“Japan was the same at the time. At first everyone pursued realism and social reportage, and after going through an evolution, especially starting in the 1990s, we began to search for the self. I think such a journey—this evolution previously occurred in Japan, and it is currently happening in China.”

“Although before this, Chinese photographers each carried out some attempts at self-exploration through photography, I think after this, this theme will become stronger and more concrete in China.”

(Interview translation: Tang Shi, commissioning editor of *On I-Photography*)
