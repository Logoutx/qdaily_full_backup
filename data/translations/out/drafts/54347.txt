Class rigidity and class mobility touch on a great many things; this piece starts with education.

Both Guo Yuhua and Yan Fei of Tsinghua University mentioned a long-running observation by Jin Jun, a teacher in the university's own sociology department. Every year he teaches a course called "Introduction to Sociology" to first-year students. In that course he surveys the students' family backgrounds — their parents' jobs, household income, and so on. "He found that the great majority of Tsinghua students come from middle-class families, while the number of students from truly humble or impoverished families really has been on a declining trend over the past stretch of time," Yan Fei said.

This conclusion corroborates the view of Xiong Xuan'ang, the top scorer in Beijing's 2017 college entrance exam. [In an interview](http://www.thepaper.cn/baidu.jsp?contid=1716409), he said: "I think children from rural areas are finding it harder and harder to get into good schools. I grew up in an ordinary family, but we never worried about food or clothing; my parents are both intellectuals, and in a big city like Beijing I enjoy educational advantages that children from other places or the countryside simply cannot have — that kind of advantage lets me take something of a shortcut in my studies. In recent years, it has also become rare to see a top gaokao scorer raised in the countryside; most are people from well-off families who are themselves outstanding."

Our discussion began with the weakening of class mobility, and several scholars from university backgrounds turned their attention to the problem of fewer and fewer rural students in universities. Lei Yi gave an exact turning point: 1997.

The question of equality in university education is a product of how resources are distributed and monopolized; behind it lies the problem of the urban-rural dual structure, and beyond that lies the question of how to understand urbanization — which, of course, also includes counter-urbanization.

Class rigidity, or blocked class mobility, arises from all of this.

We can see that, on the one hand, because of differences in governing philosophy, differences in how cities are understood may shape thinking about urbanization for a considerable time to come; on the other hand, the "talent wars" that have spread across the country and continue to this day also highlight the complexity of the problems in the process of urbanization: apart from the few super-first-tier cities like Beijing and Shanghai, the shortage of young people has become a constraint on the future development of the vast majority of Chinese cities... and possibly more than that. As Chen Yuanyuan, a professor at Shanghai University of Finance and Economics, put it in another of our articles: although cities are currently competing for high-end talent, within five to ten years they will come to realize the importance of low-skilled labor — "they will pay the price for this," she said. Once the economic drawbacks become apparent, the competition among cities may in the end come down to "grab whoever you can get."

Perhaps only then, under the pressure of urgent aging-related social problems, will the distribution of educational and urban resources truly begin to reverse.

Class rigidity and class mobility touch on a great many things; this piece starts with education.

### 1. Why has the share of rural students in universities declined since 1997?

**Lei Yi, Research Fellow, Institute of Modern History, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences**

> They go to university, to graduate school; some of them hold down three jobs at once, work as tutors, and still have to support their younger brothers or whoever. Isn't that quality? Studying hard in the midst of poverty, and still helping to solve the family's difficulties — is that not quality? Is only a piano grade certificate "quality"?

First of all, I think that compared with having no exams at all, the restoration of the exam system did allow some people from the lower strata, or the very bottom, to test their way up. Of course there are differences of wealth and class — between remote areas and cities, even within a single big city, and between key schools and non-key schools. Even today, the moment you compare Dongcheng and Xicheng with Fengtai and Mentougou, the gap is huge. Not to mention comparing the urban districts with the countryside, or Beijing with other parts of the country. This kind of resource disparity is very hard to fix.

I think the single most critical thing is, what? The supply side. Quality educational resources are scarce. That is an external constraint. Under conditions of scarce quality educational resources, how do you let children from poor areas, from poor families, also attend such schools? I think the first step is to open up education as much as possible — allow private capital or other kinds of capital into the education sector. As long as you open it up, given enough time, truly excellent private schools will emerge. In fact, many private primary and middle schools have already shown this. If universities were fully opened as well, after a number of years you would cultivate some good, non-state, non-government universities. That is how you take a quality educational resource, a scarce resource, and keep making it bigger, so there is less competition and people from more classes can attend.

So before it is opened up, under conditions of acute scarcity, how do you choose? Right now exam-oriented education does have far too many flaws, but many people say the answer is to use "quality education" to solve them. The moment "quality education" was proposed in 1997, I was firmly against it. Because when China talks about quality education, what it means is: how is your piano playing and your Olympiad math? Did you test to a certain level in a foreign language? Even painting and piano. These are all things for children of the rich — the wealthier the family, the more it can cultivate its children in these areas. Children of the poor cannot afford any of them. All they can do is study desperately. But in the gaokao, the weight of those things keeps growing. So a child from a poor family in a remote area who just grinds at their books, who could not afford anything beyond a few textbooks, let alone extracurricular reading — that child studies, gets good scores, and ends up with a lower and lower share of the total.

That is why I still have not written the piece I want to write. Why do I say that from 1997 onward, fewer and fewer rural children made it into the good urban universities? Because 1997 is when "quality education" was proposed — the rich kids can dance, play piano... What is quality? I always give the same example: I know several graduate students who come from very poor places. They cannot do much of anything else; they just grind at their books and tested their way into graduate school in Beijing.

Now, poor children have to keep doing odd jobs through primary and middle school to lighten the family's burden. They get to university, to graduate school; some hold down three jobs at once, work as tutors, and still have to support their younger brothers or whoever. Isn't that quality? Studying hard in the midst of poverty, and still helping to solve the family's difficulties — is that not quality? Is only a piano grade certificate "quality"? And yet it is precisely those children who are regarded in people's minds as having low quality. In fact, I think they have the most quality of all.

So there is one thing that moved me deeply. Once, going to Peking University to give a talk, as I neared the lecture hall a female student was waiting for me. She said, I just wanted to meet you. After the talk she walked me out again, quite a long way. She had something she wanted to tell me. She said she had graduated from a key high school in Shijiazhuang, Hebei. She did her primary and junior middle school in a remote village in Hebei, but her grades were good, so she got into the provincial key high school. The moment she arrived, she immediately felt the difference between herself and the others — the few who had come up from the countryside together, compared with the children who had grown up in Shijiazhuang. Those girls could sing and dance, their spoken English was excellent — it was especially obvious. At the time the education reform was in effect, and middle school principals could make recommendations. Every recommendation went to those who danced well or played piano well; the rural girls never got a turn. In the general opinion of the school they became the so-called exam machines who could only take tests. It was precisely after reading my article that she felt deeply moved — it struck her all at once, and she felt that we are the ones with truly high quality.

To test from such an impoverished region into a provincial key high school is actually very hard. Is that not quality? Is that not toughness and grit? Because in her original high school, people like them from the countryside were still a minority, she felt even more excluded, more on the margins, and imperceptibly developed a sense of inferiority. Reading my article, she said, at least it dissolved that inferiority, and the feeling spread among her classmates. Later she went on to earn her master's and PhD at Peking University.

So the most important things are still equality in the allocation of educational resources and an increase in supply. Most critically: under current conditions, how do you allocate resources? So when "quality education" came out in 1997, I published an article called *《教育首先要防止"更坏"》* ("Education Must First Guard Against 'Worse'"). To strive for better, you must first prevent worse. And time has proved it: how many people are satisfied with this education reform? Including urbanites — signing their kids up for this class and that class, spending so much money, and still not satisfied, thinking, I'm exhausted and I've spent so much. Because now for school advancement you don't take exams — how do I, the school, decide whom to admit? Then I have no choice but to ask: who has which piano grade? Who has which Olympiad math grade? Otherwise how would I know? And in those Olympiad math tests, plenty of parents cannot solve the problems themselves. Nowadays for the primary-to-middle-school transition, the rules say the junior divisions may not hold entrance exams. With no exam, the school says: suppose there are ten children and I can only take six — how do I choose? On what basis? There are a few ways: by power, by money, by test scores, by piano. You actually went and removed test scores — so all that is left is piano and Olympiad math. Then every parent is extremely dissatisfied. The adults are exhausted, the children more so, enrolled in countless classes.

### 2. Educational equity is a question worth discussing in all seriousness

**Wu Fei, Professor, College of Media and International Culture, Zhejiang University**

> Inequality between classes or strata certainly, unquestionably exists. Different strata, different social positions — the possibility of receiving an education is entirely different.

Inequality between classes or strata certainly, unquestionably exists. Different strata, different social positions — the possibility of receiving an education is entirely different. So the odds of testing into Peking University, for example, are very different; a student from Zhejiang and a student from Beijing do not face the same bar for Peking University. This really is an enormously complex engineering problem. Take Wang Jianlin: it is entirely normal that his child can receive more and better education. You cannot say that because you are the richest man, your child may not receive that good an education. That too would be a kind of inequality — you cannot deprive the powerful of the right to provide for their own. This is the first level at which we need to be careful.

But at another level, a socialist country puts more emphasis on fairness and equity. Because our wealth is all public wealth; from this perspective, we should build a mechanism that genuinely gives many poor children the possibility of upward mobility — the door must be held open. This is a double difficulty, and honestly I cannot propose a particularly good solution. If I have not even solved the problem of keeping myself fed and warm, and you insist that this child must get into Peking University — how is he to do it? The excellent teachers of Hangzhou or Beijing are unwilling to go to the countryside either. Even if you build beautiful classrooms and pay Beijing-level salaries, they still will not go. How do you solve that? This is not something you solve with a snap of the fingers. Educational equity is a very complex process.

Of course, perhaps in the future the internet will offer such a possibility — letting a poor child receive instruction from Beijing's best teachers. Is that possible? Of course, we can only strive to provide a possibility in the construction of institutions. But even if I were the Minister of Education, I could not solve the problem of educational injustice. It is genuinely hard.

I think that in recent years China has already undergone a great change in its ideas about fairness and equity. Early on, Chinese people were always thinking "he can be replaced by me" — always wanting to "equalize wealth," then "divide up the land and topple the local tyrants." Nowadays there are probably far fewer people who think this way. Many Chinese have already changed at this level. But educational equity is still a question very much worth serious discussion. At the very least, we must give the poorest children — children who cannot attend any school at all — the possibility of receiving basic compulsory education; that is what the state should do. Whether they end up at Peking University, Zhejiang University, or just an ordinary vocational high school is another matter entirely.

So I think that starting point is the most important. Compulsory education itself must not be denied to anyone. Of course, after doing some fieldwork of my own — for example, my own research on the Derung — I found that in that minority area, some children simply do not want to study anymore; they find it pointless, they do not want to read. Another reason, of course, is that they feel studying will not change their future life circumstances to any great extent, so they drop out and go home. That too is a problem. But in fact the state has many policies for those ethnic minorities: you can get into a decent school with the lowest scores, even lower than what urban children need. In fact, some ethnic-minority policies do exist.

(The education of migrant workers' children) — this is an area where our country, at the design stage, has big problems. Nowadays many migrant workers have come into the cities, and their children surely must be with their parents; that is only human nature, and education requires it. A child's education with the parents absent is necessarily incomplete. The schooling of these children is a huge problem that the state needs to make a real effort to solve.

On this point, it seems Hangzhou does relatively well, while Beijing seems to find it very hard — possibly because Beijing has so many people from elsewhere. In Hangzhou, basically even a migrant worker's child can attend the nearest school just like a city child; there are just some restrictions, which have been getting stricter — how long you have rented your home, how long you have held a temporary residence permit, and so on — but in any case it provides the possibility of a child not losing their schooling. Beijing, with its population problem, may have solved it with a one-size-fits-all cut. I really do not understand this — I cannot even fathom who could make this type of decision. Does it go through no scientific justification process at all? It is very strange.

So this is a major obstacle in our official governance and administration: a single leader may change things with a snap of the fingers, without ever holding a hearing on the decision. I think that sometimes, even when a single person's interests are involved, you must handle it with the utmost care. This business is utterly absurd — I find it unclear, truly unimaginable.

### 3. The problem of left-behind children is even more complicated than what we see today

**Yan Fei, Associate Professor, Department of Sociology, Tsinghua University**

> These students who attend schools for migrant workers' children, when the time comes for the gaokao or the zhongkao (the senior high school entrance exam), still have to return to their place of household registration to take the exam. After going back, a few can truly make it out, but the great majority still cannot. Those who cannot become the second generation of migrant workers' children in the city. At sixteen or eighteen they drift into the city and begin the migrant worker life all over again. The mindset and the life plans of this generation are completely different from those of their parents.

There is a teacher at Tsinghua (Jin Jun) who, every year, teaches a course to first-year students called Introduction to Sociology. In that course he surveys the family backgrounds of that year's incoming students — what jobs your parents hold, what the household income is. He found that the great majority of Tsinghua students come from middle-class families, while the number of students from truly humble or impoverished families really has been on a declining trend over the past stretch of time.

(Regarding the problem of left-behind children,) what I have learned is that, because they cannot attend public schools in their local cities, they end up in schools for migrant workers' children. First, they have no money for private schools and could only attend the nearest public school; but without local household registration, they cannot get into a public school either. In the end they can only gravitate on their own to schools for migrant workers' children. Because of school buildings and educational quality, these schools exist in a policy gray zone. The governments of Beijing and Shanghai can say they will shut you down, or they can turn a blind eye and tolerate your staying open. But the biggest problem here is that these students, when the time comes for the gaokao or the zhongkao, still have to return to their place of household registration to take the exam. After going back, a few can truly make it out, but the great majority still cannot. Those who cannot become the second generation of migrant workers' children in the city. At sixteen or eighteen they drift into the city and begin the migrant worker life all over again. The mindset and the life plans of this generation are completely different from those of their parents.

(Even though we are talking about the current situation,) in fact it seems to be the latent influence of many of the growing pains of China's transitional society over the past twenty years.

(Including the gaokao — everyone feels it is needed, yet it always feels unfair.) So now there is independent enrollment, but independent enrollment has plenty of problems of its own. Independent enrollment tilts even more toward the middle class: it means you need vision, you need to have read widely, rather than simply being good at test-taking. The Hengshui High School model only suits exam-taking, with one national exam paper for all, but independent enrollment tilts toward the children of the middle class in first-tier cities like Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen.

### 4. The different resources universities obtain determine the enormous differences in the upward mobility they offer

**Yan Fei, Associate Professor, Department of Sociology, Tsinghua University**

> They are all university students, but in fact their bigger problems have not been solved. After they graduate, they will in fact slowly slide into the lower-middle strata of society.

(But new problems arise too.) Every year Tsinghua has quotas for financial aid aimed at students from poor backgrounds. The enrollment quotas are fixed, or grow slightly each year, guaranteeing that children from rural areas have the chance to come to Tsinghua. After they arrive, we find problems like this: Tsinghua's students are all the most outstanding — many are provincial top scorers from different provinces, or students ranked in the top five or top ten — and once they gather together, they compete with one another. In that competition, enormous disparities emerge, especially for those from remote areas or so-called humble families. Because apart from good grades, these students discover, when they compare themselves with the others, that — take a child of Beijing's middle class — he may already have traveled the world by the time he enrolls, with exchange programs overseas, an Apple laptop, having read piles of extracurricular books, and so on. But for this student, it may be his first time in Beijing; he has never touched an Apple product. So his horizons and depth of reading are not as good as those of the children from middle-class families.

This produces a great psychological gap, and they study even harder, feeling they can make it up through study. But in the networked age, closing such a gap in the short term is not simply a matter of taking notes and studying; it requires constantly broadening your horizons and taking part in many club activities.

So Tsinghua is trying to find ways — I believe other schools are doing many similar things. We provide students with many overseas study, internship, exchange, and visiting programs, and give them funding to go to countries like India, Argentina, Sweden, and Belgium to exchange and broaden their horizons. For them it is genuinely a kind of elevation.

At least in the first year, you truly do find that students from humble families experience a much greater sense of gap; they become very withdrawn and insecure, and after the insecurity they are unwilling to communicate with others, or may go to extremes. But we find that by the second and third year — especially after the constant exposure to club activities through the first and second years, after the elevation provided by all kinds of exchange and visiting programs — they slowly close the distance with the other students. For now, that is about the best approach one can think of.

(But they have already gotten into Tsinghua; they are already different from most people.) In that environment they still feel insecure. But when they leave Tsinghua and compare themselves with others, they feel: I'm actually not bad — I'm from Tsinghua, right? That gap still exists.

But looking at social mobility as a whole, I think the biggest problem is that it turns out to be closely tied to what I mentioned earlier: the construction of the "Double First-Class" initiative and the disciplinary evaluations. Because the state's funding and resources all go to these so-called "Double First-Class" universities. This means that many non-"Double First-Class" schools — even the lower-tier third-tier undergraduate colleges and specialist colleges further down the ladder — receive very little funding and resources. Along with the expansion of university enrollment, going to university has long since ceased to be a luxury. But if you go to a non-211, non-985 school, or even a university whose name no one has ever heard, that in fact produces a structural difference. The students become sacrificial victims of the whole education system, unable to receive a quality education. Compared with the "Double First-Class" schools that receive state policy support and have resources, the gap grows bigger and bigger. In such an environment, you could say they experienced upward mobility — and indeed there is mobility: going from a rural area to a university in some third- or fourth-tier city is also upward mobility. But the degree of that mobility is actually of little help to their own self-improvement. In this sense, such an investment in education does not pay off.

They are all university students, but in fact their bigger problems have not been solved. After they graduate, they will in fact slowly slide into the lower-middle strata of society.

### 5. Why does independent enrollment by universities aggravate inequality when universities have no autonomy?

**Sheng Hong, Economist, Director of the Unirule Institute of Economics, a non-governmental Chinese think tank**

> I have heard some reports — for example, a student whose father works at the National Development and Reform Commission got 30 bonus points from Peking University and 30 from Renmin University through independent enrollment. Of course that is definitely wrong. Where did those 30 points come from? His father is at the NDRC, and the university is still waiting for them to approve its funding.

Who decides the gaokao? Who decides who gets to attend which school? First, the distribution of university resources is already unreasonable. Beijing has an excess of university resources. Exams are administered and admission quotas allocated by province and municipality; although these universities are national, in fact a Beijing examinee's odds of getting into Tsinghua or Peking University are much higher than an outsider's, and a Shanghai student's odds of getting into Fudan or Tongji are also on the high side. A Beijing examinee's chance of reaching the first-tier admission line is 5.2 times that of a Sichuan examinee. And on top of that there are policies like independent enrollment. Independent enrollment is one enormous corruption. I criticized it long ago: when universities do not have genuine autonomy, what is the point of "independent" enrollment? Isn't it laughable? It is certainly not independent enrollment — it merely flaunts a very nice name. Why? The higher-level administrators will certainly manipulate it; there is no question about that. So I have heard some reports — for example, a student whose father works at the National Development and Reform Commission got 30 bonus points from Peking University and 30 from Renmin University through independent enrollment. Of course that is definitely wrong. Where did those 30 points come from? His father is at the NDRC, and the university is still waiting for them to approve its funding. This is grossly unfair. It is even more unfair to students from other regions and from the countryside. Independent enrollment favors people who live in Beijing. If you live in Xinjiang, you have to make the trip — the cost is certainly high; and if you are from rural Xinjiang, it is even more of a hassle. So this policy, in terms of fairness, has enormous problems.

### 6. Educational inequality comes from structural inequality. On equality, the wealth gap, and the market

**Guo Yuhua, Professor, Department of Sociology, Tsinghua University**

> Some people say: abolish the gaokao. If you abolished it, I believe the children of important officials would all get into university, and the farmers would have even less of a chance!

Actually, at Tsinghua, Jin Jun in our sociology department has led undergraduates in several years of observation. Every year after the new students arrive, they hand out a questionnaire for the freshmen to fill in. You can see a trend — probably not only at schools like Tsinghua and Peking University; key universities generally share it — namely that the proportion of students from the countryside is declining.

I remember when we were at university in the 1980s, the share of our class from rural areas was quite high. In recent years there has been a clear downward trend; there really is such a thing as "talent no longer springs from humble homes." Because the urban-rural dual structure as a whole is unjust, it necessarily makes the distribution of educational resources as a whole unjust. As for class rigidity — or, today, the wealth gap — these are indeed problems that many people worry about a great deal.

But here a distinction must be made. How do you look at the wealth gap? If you start from that set of Marxist theories, of course the wealth gap is intolerable — it must be flattened; that is the whole class-struggle line. I think there are several questions here. First: is a gap between rich and poor a normal phenomenon in a society? There should be a normal gap; you certainly cannot equalize everyone's wealth. This is true of every society — there is always class differentiation, always a wealth gap, always disparities in income distribution. You have to correctly understand what "equality" means. Equality means people are equal in rights, equal before the law, equal in basic rights; in opportunity, they should be equal. Today's education, for example, is unequal, because with slightly lower scores you can get into university in Beijing or Shanghai, while in some other provinces you need very high gaokao scores to get in at all. That is inequality.

But this equality is absolutely not equality of distribution, not equality of outcomes. Because outcomes cannot be equal — people are simply different — no society can insist on equality of wealth or equality of social status in the end. Impossible. Things are bound to be different: you may have talent in painting, I may have talent in sports; you cannot demand that everyone be the same. As long as there is a freely competitive market economy, inequality is inevitable — unequal outcomes are inevitable.

This is a question that needs to be sorted out, rather than just waving the banner of equality and declaring everything fine. Because precisely that line of thinking (from the communist movement) waves the banner of equality while actually depriving everyone of their rights. It can invoke something like great harmony under heaven, equalizing wealth for all, and in the end the certain result is common poverty. Truly equal — everyone equally poor. We have already lived through that process; we cannot go back down that road.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180616023242yEbj72JIOfekYHzM.jpg-w600)

Second, as for today's society — of course, to say the wealth gap is enormous is to point to a real problem. When the wealth gap is too large — people today measure it with the Gini coefficient; if a society's wealth gap exceeds a certain value, it enters a dangerous state — that, too, must be recognized. But what causes an excessive wealth gap? That has to be figured out clearly. Different systems have different causes. In some societies, perhaps, the market causes it. When the market causes it, there may be other arrangements — relief, welfare, security. You cannot let the poor reach the point where they cannot survive. You still have to give them something, whether through charity, through security provision, or through other government transfer payments — giving people the conditions to survive. But it is not a simple leveling.

So what exactly caused China's wealth gap? This is what we need to think about, to pursue. Was it caused by the market economy? No — I don't think so. Is it, as some leftists say, caused by capital? I think absolutely not. It is precisely an incomplete market economy — a power-dominated market economy — that caused it! Where is China's wealth gap biggest today? Between officials and the public, between those special interest groups and the broad mass of the people — that is the greatest gap. Yet on this question there is often confusion, created by that old class-struggle mindset. What about "robbing the rich to help the poor"? It goes after entrepreneurs, after private companies. Are private companies the ones who control the most resources and wealth in China today? Private companies are extremely fragile; many do not know where tomorrow's meal is coming from. Further nationalization, further letting those special interest groups control the economic lifelines of the state and the biggest enterprises and their biggest returns — that is an enormous injustice. So China's wealth gap lies here. And the cause is precisely the use of power to seize wealth — to disrupt and to control the market economy. That is what caused it.

So I think we must really be vigilant about this point, rather than treating the market economy, as some leftists say, as the source of all evil. It is not. The wealth gaps facing our country and Western societies today are different problems.

(Educational inequality is the same.) Right now I think it is truly hard to solve any single aspect of the problem on its own; these are all structural problems, matters touching every facet of the whole. Today there may be some changes — for example, since the restoration of the gaokao system, the annual national unified exam, and so on — and in recent years there have been some changes in places, such as independent enrollment. But I think that before this structural problem is solved, independent enrollment cannot fully solve it either. Because of course a school wants to find the best students, right? And the best students will certainly not come from (poor families) — though there are occasional exceptions. But it is still impossible, through such small adjustments, to solve the problem at the root. Fair competition through examinations remains the most basic method of admission. Some people say: abolish the gaokao. If you abolished it, I believe the children of important officials would all get into university, and the farmers would have even less of a chance!
