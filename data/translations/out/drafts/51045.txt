We interviewed women who have frozen their eggs, agencies that broker overseas assisted reproduction, and several physicians with extensive egg-freezing experience, and distilled some reflections and questions.

On February 6, Zhan Yingying walked into a post office in Guangzhou with a thick stack of letters in hand, planning to mail them to every delegate of the Jilin Provincial People's Congress.

"When I learned that unmarried women in China are not allowed to use assisted reproductive technology, I felt deeply disappointed and hurt. I felt discriminated against because I'm not married, and that the possibility of freezing my eggs was being taken away from me — that is really unfair," [Zhan wrote in her letter](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/9TJ6E6AEJVdGRDd-LyTDaw).

Zhan Yingying is 29. She studied medicine as an undergraduate, psychology in graduate school, and is now switching tracks to prepare for a law exam. In January this year, she went alone to a hospital in Guangzhou to ask about the possibility of freezing her eggs. She knew she would be turned down, but decided to go anyway: "I wanted him to tell me to my face that it wasn't possible, and to tell me why."

Before this, egg freezing had not been part of Zhan Yingying's life plan. She didn't know much about it — partly because she was still young, and partly because she had never considered marriage or having children.

As she grew older and friends around her married and had children one after another, a thought one day surfaced in Zhan Yingying's mind — I don't want a child now, but by the time I'm 40, will it no longer be possible? That thought is the starting point for almost every woman who seeks egg freezing.

### "The World's Only Regret Pill"

Many Chinese women, like Zhan Yingying, initially had no idea this was even an option for them, and knew nothing about the existing legal ban either.

In 2015, the well-known actress Xu Jinglei went public about having frozen nine eggs in the United States two years earlier. Then 41, she said: "This is the world's only regret pill."

![Xu Jinglei discussing egg freezing in a magazine interview](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313021842wKRNlGCA5EnBmWYH.jpeg-w600)

That regret pill set off a public debate, and many women flocked to hospitals to ask about the technology, process, and cost of egg freezing. According to an issue 8, 2015 report in *Health*, Li Rong, deputy director of the reproductive center at Peking University Third Hospital, said the number of women consulting about egg freezing was indeed growing, most of them over 35. At the same time, she noted that while egg-freezing technology had gradually matured, it had been developed in China for less than ten years and had not been promoted on a large scale.

This wave of egg-freezing enthusiasm prompted CCTV to produce a six-minute program reiterating the relevant regulations: "The Ministry of Health stipulates that single women cannot undergo assisted-reproductive-technology procedures; some hospitals allow single women to freeze their eggs, but to use the frozen eggs they must provide three certificates — an ID card, a marriage certificate, and a birth permit."

A single statement about egg freezing stirred heated discussion — which highlights precisely how much demand and how many questions Chinese women have about it.

In 2016, a Chinese woman named Manman also went to the United States to freeze her eggs. [The BBC documented her egg-freezing process in full](http://www.bbc.com/zhongwen/trad/chinese-news-40737691). Manman is not single — she is married — but she said her current work situation left her unable to devote herself fully to raising a child, so she decided to freeze her eggs first, giving herself the option if she wants children in the future.

"Many women today face a dilemma under pressure to reproduce. It takes too much courage to be a DINK, and after having one child you're expected to have a second," Manman said.

![Manman, a woman who froze her eggs, being interviewed by the BBC for a documentary](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313021948YcxNIoWdaJE37U5w.jpg-w600)

### The Single Women Seeking Egg Freezing Overseas

In April 2017, a travel product called the "U.S. Egg-Freezing Tour" appeared on Trip.com Group's app and website, again drawing intense attention. [A Trip.com Group staff member told The Paper](http://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1662718) that China has 700 million women, and as the population of older single women in big cities keeps growing, egg freezing has enormous market potential.

Due to the restrictions of current Chinese law, more and more people, like Xu Jinglei and Manman, are seeking egg freezing overseas — and the most popular destination by far is the United States.

The United States opened egg freezing to clinical use for the general public in 2012. [Citing data last year from the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology, the New York Times reported](https://cn.nytimes.com/china/20160831/china-us-women-fertility/) that among 51 relevant clinics in California, 33 offered customized services for Chinese clients — Chinese-language paperwork and websites, and Mandarin-speaking staff. Kevin Doody, the society's president at the time, also said the number of egg-freezing cycles in the United States rose from 568 in 2009 to 6,165 in 2014.

![Himalaya Medical Service Center (image from official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313022420JdVk9yzMOsChXqSe.jpg-w600)

Many have taken note of this demand, and Himalaya Medical Service Center is one example. It has been in existence for less than a year, with offices in Shanghai and Washington, D.C. Its official website introduction reads: "We provide patients with medical care in the United States and a full range of services for clients in China with fertility-related needs who wish to give birth in the U.S."

"Look at infertility — there may be 10 million people in the Chinese market alone. IVF is available domestically, but it falls far short of demand. As far as I know, quite a few single women know that U.S. egg-freezing technology is very advanced," Dr. Xu Jingwen, founder of Himalaya Medical Service Center, told QDaily in an interview.

In fact, the number of people with infertility is even larger than Xu Jingwen imagined. According to a China Population Association survey report released in 2015 by the China Reproductive Health Network, infertile patients currently account for 12.5% of China's population — some 40 million people. This is why, in recent years, intermediary agencies like Himalaya Medical Service Center have proliferated in first- and second-tier cities.

Beyond its advanced technology, the United States has relatively loose legal restrictions on human assisted reproduction, which is why women from many countries flock there. Different U.S. states have different rules on egg freezing, and quite a few of them allow single women to freeze their eggs and give birth.

Society is no stranger to egg freezing, nor is it a taboo subject — many companies even offer egg freezing as a benefit to their female employees.

In October 2014, Apple and Facebook announced new benefit policies, one of which was providing female employees with guarantees and preferential terms for egg freezing and reproductive rights. According to internal Facebook materials, the company partnered with the well-known U.S. reproductive center Progyny to offer a series of incentives — for instance, employees who freeze their eggs through the company can receive a US$20,000 subsidy.

![Facebook offering egg-freezing benefits to female employees](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313022808Ey7C2fGhQsmLuPvK.png-w600)

"The earliest motivation for egg freezing was actually to serve cancer patients, preserving their fertility for them, but in recent years most of the demand has come from social trends," added Dr. Shen Mengxun of Aijin Obstetrics and Gynecology in Taipei, noting that the proportion of late marriages and non-marriage in society is rising.

Dr. Li Risheng, an attending physician at the Assisted Reproductive Medicine Center of Taipei Mackay Memorial Hospital, also emphasized to QDaily that the number of women undergoing egg-freezing procedures is indeed increasing year by year. Last year alone, around 400 women froze their eggs at Mackay, the majority of them over 38.

Worth noting: under current Taiwanese law, single women can legally undergo egg-freezing procedures. However, if they later need to thaw the eggs and proceed with assisted reproduction, they must still provide a marriage certificate.

Yet even this one provision — that single women can legally freeze their eggs — has drawn inquiries from some women in mainland China.

Dr. Shen Mengxun said that Aijin Obstetrics and Gynecology's figures alone show nearly ten single women from mainland China froze their eggs there last year.

![Eggs](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313022902eiqCHD93LEY4Fj2W.jpg-w600)

### Why Can't Single Women Legally Freeze Their Eggs Yet?

Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation), whose full name is egg cryopreservation, falls within the medical domain as one item under human assisted reproductive technology.

In 2001, China's Ministry of Health under the State Council promulgated the 《Administrative Measures for Human Assisted Reproductive Technology》(《人类辅助生殖技术管理办法》), which stipulates: "Only women with malignant tumors may have their eggs retrieved and frozen before radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and women with infertility before IVF cannot be performed in time." And: "It is prohibited to perform human assisted reproductive technology on couples and single women who do not comply with national population and family-planning laws, regulations, and rules."

The Ministry of Health explicitly prohibits egg freezing for single women.

However, on September 27, 2002, the Standing Committee of the Ninth Jilin Provincial People's Congress held its 32nd session and passed the 《Jilin Provincial Population and Family Planning Regulations》(《吉林省人口与计划生育条例》), which stipulates: "Women who have reached the legal marriageable age, have decided not to marry, and have no children may use legal medical assisted-reproductive technology to bear one child." In other words, Jilin is currently the only province in China with a legal provision allowing single women to freeze their eggs.

"This is a regulation of the provincial people's congress, and it sits at the same level as the prohibition issued by the Ministry of Health under the State Council. That means whether or not local hospitals in Jilin perform the procedure, neither violates the law — which actually gives hospitals a gray area," Zhan Yingying explained why she mailed the letters to Jilin's provincial delegates. She stressed that this provision allowed her to see the contradiction between central and local regulations, and at the same time the hope of amending the law.

It is understood that after Jilin enacted the regulations at the time, a few single female teachers completed egg-freezing procedures. Although the regulations remain in force, no one has undergone egg freezing since, and hospitals generally will not perform egg freezing for single women.

Regarding single women's egg-freezing rights, some delegates to the National People's Congress also put forward proposals during the Two Sessions last year. [In February this year, the National Health and Family Planning Commission responded](http://m.mp.oeeee.com/a/BAAFRD00002018022468478.html?layer=8&share=chat&isndappinstalled=0&ndsign=QTYbha2yDM911CF35fdwdjoZSP6heK80tbcoWe8Xk4yL0xbjIKDLBDrb8RgdTaFsgRuNgotazQxsXgK9eN4GhxrCTdY0xUyvI5pf1OjWBD59Z5dgzF2RauXVyM2ImXKedUTpVQii3qDj8sWUrLasg2QzMjY5NDQ0M2MzNjM1NTA1NjM3Zjc3NGI3N2E3Yjc3L0bBdh927QU9b6u5Sfb2/g==f896191e7da8059d6205439c33be7fc1), emphasizing that neither the 《Population and Family Planning Law》(《人口与计划生育法》), the 《Law on the Protection of Women's Rights and Interests》(《妇女权益保障法》), nor the marriage law denies single women the right to reproduce — but that "making specific provisions on single women's reproductive rights by amending the 《Population and Family Planning Law》(《人口与计划生育法》) at present still requires in-depth research and deliberation."

Xu Jinglei's 2015 announcement about her egg freezing prompted many experts and scholars to voice their views on the practice.

At the time, Liu Ping, deputy director of the reproductive medicine center at Peking University Third Hospital, told *Health News* in an interview: "Egg freezing for single women is a safeguard that extends the possibility of life and increases reproductive opportunities. Considering the needs of certain special groups, and provided it complies with public morality and does no harm to others or affect society, I personally believe society should respect these individuals' choices." He added that if the principle of family planning needed to be observed, control could be exercised once a person enters the childbearing stage.

But Zhai Xiaomei, then dean of the School of Humanities and Social Sciences at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, offered the opposite view: "The relevant regulations prohibit the use of assisted reproductive technology for single women. From an ethical standpoint, this is mainly out of consideration for protecting offspring."

In March 2016, Zhu Min, then deputy managing director of the IMF, publicly stated that China would gradually develop into an aging society, with the elderly accounting for 12%–13% of the total population. Against this backdrop, opening egg freezing to single women would inevitably delay women's childbearing age and exacerbate population aging.

In addition, some scholars argue that egg freezing postpones childbearing age, and the subsequent pregnancy would still face the problems of advanced maternal age — while meanwhile the relevant measures for "surrogacy" have yet to be perfected. Although humans have the right to reproduce, the act of reproduction itself still involves a series of ethical and legal questions.

Challenging traditional ethics is indeed one of the most common criticisms leveled at single women fighting for egg-freezing rights.

"I think the state's system itself is built around maintaining the monogamous nuclear family. Another important issue is distrust — a distrust that single women have the responsibility and the strength to raise a child and grow together with that child," Zhan Yingying said. "Marriage may be one vessel for carrying children, but it doesn't have to be the only one."

### Setting the Law Aside for a Moment, What Should You Know About Egg Freezing?

At the end of 2016, Cheng Xin (a pseudonym), a 34-year-old Taiwanese woman, was about to be posted to Europe. Before that, she decided to freeze her eggs first. She retrieved 16 eggs in total, which are currently stored at Tri-Service General Hospital in Taipei and have been frozen for over a year.

Cheng Xin told QDaily: "I think if I could have my own child, without emotional or legal entanglements with anyone else, and raise the child in my own way, that would be pretty good."

In 2014, Cheng Xin broke up with her boyfriend, and she hasn't found a suitable partner since. Two years later, she began to think she might never marry. But she didn't want to lose the possibility of having a child simply because she was unmarried. At the same time, Cheng Xin was aware of the biological limits — that ovarian function deteriorates with age.

Cheng Xin revealed that her mother had an ovarian-related condition, which made her pay particular attention to this area. One day, Cheng Xin went to a hospital for a checkup and chatted with the doctor about egg freezing.

When she remembered she was soon heading to Europe, Cheng Xin quickly decided to go ahead with egg freezing. She said: "My personality is such that I don't let myself have regrets afterward."

The decision was fairly rushed. Looking back, Cheng Xin admits she didn't think it through all that clearly, but she added that when the first ovulation-stimulation injection went into her belly, it was like getting braces — if the teeth are already pulled, there's not much point regretting it.

"I feel the whole egg-freezing process was bigger and more weighty than I had expected," Cheng Xin said.

For many women like Cheng Xin, deciding to freeze eggs generally means facing several stages: ovulation stimulation, egg retrieval, freezing, and (possibly) thawing.

**1. Egg quality declines with age**

The reason women's fertility is tied to age lies mainly in egg function. Although the medical community still has some disagreement over whether the number of eggs a woman has in her lifetime is fixed, there is broad consensus that egg function deteriorates with age and the quantity declines accordingly.

"In each menstrual cycle, although only one egg is ovulated, around 100 to 1,000 eggs compete in the process. You can think of it like fertilization, where many sperm compete and only one sperm finally fertilizes the egg. When a woman ovulates, only one mature egg is released — the other eggs that lost the competition die at the same time," explained Dr. Li Risheng of Taipei Mackay Memorial Hospital.

Dr. Shen Mengxun of Aijin Obstetrics and Gynecology added: "Statistically speaking, 38 is a threshold. From a fertility standpoint, why do we say older mothers face a risk of bearing children with deformities? Because the proportion of chromosomal abnormalities differs between a pregnancy at 35 and a pregnancy at 38."

However, Dr. Shen Mengxun stressed that this varies from person to person, and the key factor is each person's AMH level.

AMH stands for Anti-Müllerian Hormone, which is secreted by the granulosa cells of the ovaries and can serve as an indicator for predicting ovarian reserve. Generally, every woman undergoing egg freezing must first have an AMH assessment. This concerns not only the quantity and quality of her eggs, but also how much time and money the process will require.

"If the AMH level is between 2 and 8, the egg-freezing outcome tends to be quite good; if it's below 0.02, the cost will be relatively high and the treatment cycle longer," Dr. Li Risheng said. Beyond that, the AMH level also affects the pregnancy rate after thawing, and how many eggs should be retrieved to be safe.

**2. How many eggs do you need to freeze?**

"We still believe it's best to freeze around 10 to 15 eggs. Some people retrieve only 2 at a time, but some retrieve 20 in one go," Dr. Li Risheng advised.

Dr. Shen Mengxun shared a real case: a 37-year-old single woman came to Aijin Obstetrics and Gynecology for egg freezing, her AMH level was in quite good shape, and after just one retrieval she got 20 eggs. Conversely, if a person has a very low AMH level and retrieves only 2 eggs in one session, she has to repeat the ovulation-stimulation and retrieval process several times, and each of these is billed separately. At Aijin Obstetrics and Gynecology, for instance, one egg-freezing cycle costs roughly 20,000 to 30,000 yuan.

As for Cheng Xin, she said she was quite lucky — the first stimulation cycle yielded 16 eggs.

**3. What risks does egg retrieval carry?**

Before retrieval, ovulation stimulation is required, administered by injection into the abdomen, and the whole process can take about ten days.

"The stimulation injections rescue eggs that would otherwise have died competing in the menstrual cycle," Dr. Li Risheng explained. Some people believe the injections force out eggs from the next cycle and therefore accelerate ovarian aging — but that is a myth.

Cheng Xin still remembers the injection process; she said it was genuinely quite painful, and afterward her body swelled with water retention and she experienced bloating.

"Frankly, stimulation injections can't be free of side effects. The more common ones are bloating, abdominal pain, or emotional shifts," Dr. Shen Mengxun also said — adding that these side effects are temporary and fully resolve after the next menstrual period.

During the stimulation period, patients must also return to the hospital for follow-up ultrasound scans to observe how many eggs have been produced.

Once ovulation goes smoothly, retrieval can be prepared. Dr. Li Risheng said the risks of the retrieval stage are essentially those of any surgery — for example, allergic reactions to anesthesia, as well as bleeding or infection.

Egg retrieval involves puncturing the vagina with a needle to extract the eggs, and must be done under general anesthesia; the entire procedure takes about 30 minutes or less.

**4. Is there any difference in children born from frozen eggs?**

Egg freezing as a technology has not been developed all that long, and it has gone through several technical transitions along the way. In 1986, Australian scientists reported the first successful in vitro fertilization using frozen eggs.

However, the technology at the time was very different from today's. The older approach was "slow freezing" used for embryos. But because eggs themselves are rich in water, ice crystals easily form during freezing and damage the cells, making the survival rate of thawed eggs very low. This is why early egg freezing had poor results and developed slowly.

It was the advent of "vitrification" — the technique now in wide use — that changed the trajectory of egg freezing. "Before freezing, the eggs are first treated with a cryoprotectant, then rapidly placed into liquid nitrogen at -196°C to go dormant," Dr. Shen Mengxun said.

Because vitrification greatly reduces ice-crystal formation and fully protects the eggs, the average survival rate of thawed eggs exceeds 80%, and the fertilization success rate also exceeds 80% — far safer and more effective than before.

Even with improved safety, many women still raise a question: are frozen eggs inferior to normal eggs?

Dr. Shen Mengxun stressed that current egg-freezing technology does not harm the eggs. If there is any effect at all, it would manifest in egg function, such as the fertilization rate. "In principle, there is no difference between fresh and frozen eggs — frozen eggs don't cause deformities or anything like that. Genes are fixed; we don't alter them, so there's no need to worry that egg freezing causes chromosomal abnormalities. The key is whether fertilization proceeds smoothly after thawing; once a fertilized egg becomes an embryo, everything thereafter proceeds normally."

But that doesn't mean that once eggs survive thawing, fertilization and pregnancy are guaranteed. From egg revival to successful fertilization, and on to pregnancy and live birth, the success rates are not 100% — they decline step by step.

In August 2017, Taiwan's Department of Health released the "Analysis Report on the Implementation of Assisted Reproduction in Taiwan." The data shows that the pregnancy rate for assisted reproduction (not just egg freezing) rose from 37.2% in 1998 to 44% in 2015, while the live birth rate rose from 27% in 1998 to 32.5% in 2015.

**5. Within how many years should I thaw my eggs?**

Cheng Xin is currently preparing to pay a new year's storage fee for her frozen eggs, but she has started pondering another question: she may never use these eggs at all.

Under Taiwanese law, if Cheng Xin wants to thaw her eggs and undergo assisted reproduction, she must attach a marriage certificate. Cheng Xin froze eggs from when she was 34, but she is also aware that her aging body is increasingly less suited to pregnancy.

Cheng Xin said she does not question the egg-freezing technology — in fact, this experience left her feeling that Taiwan is quite advanced in egg-freezing technology. It is just that realizing the gap between technology and law has produced in her a contradictory, regretful feeling. "Under the current law, only married people are guaranteed the right to have children," Cheng Xin said.

![Age of wives receiving assisted reproduction in Taiwan rising year by year (image: Report on Assisted Reproduction in Taiwan)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313024836GDzZbLWqTd27hyPo.jpg-w600)

Different countries currently have different rules on egg freezing. Japan, for instance, announced in 2013 that it would allow healthy single women to apply to preserve their eggs, though it does not recommend egg freezing for women over 40. Like the United States, the United Kingdom permits single women to freeze their eggs.

These laws happen to reflect the state of local social culture and the relevant medical support systems — after all, egg freezing concerns the act of reproduction, which involves a whole chain of issues: egg donation, sperm donation, assisted reproduction, and surrogacy.

Cheng Xin said that if she intends never to marry, there may be no need to freeze eggs at all, because there may never be an opportunity to thaw them — especially absent the premise of considering going to the United States to give birth.

"According to statistics, only about 30% of single women worldwide who freeze their eggs ever thaw them. In fact, egg freezing these days is a bit like buying insurance — freezing gives you an extra layer of protection, but you may well never use it," Dr. Li Risheng said.

Dr. Shen Mengxun added: "Technically speaking, you can freeze eggs for as many years as you want. If a 35-year-old freezes her eggs, I would advise her not to wait until after 38 or 40 to thaw them — after all, the mother's condition during pregnancy also has to be considered; there can be complications from high-risk pregnancy."

Zhan Yingying is very clear about the risks and process of egg-freezing technology. She wrote her personal experience of being turned away at a Guangzhou hospital into the letter to the Jilin Provincial People's Congress. Zhan Yingying told QDaily that one delegate, after receiving the letter, personally gave her a kind response and said he could put forward a related proposal in the near future.

"I still have at least five years to push this forward and get it done in China. I can't be entirely certain it will have an effect within these five years, or that more people will stand up or do something, but I still hold expectations and hope, and I want to put a bit more effort into this myself," Zhan Yingying said.
