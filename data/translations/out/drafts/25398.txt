## Differentiated destination travel products mean replicability and scaling are that much harder. Commercialization in this direction will test whether this company, born of the "fresh and artsy" set, has stronger control over the travel industry chain.

Regular article

From the moment an idea of where to travel pops into your head, you consult reference materials, plan the itinerary, book flights, book hotels, arrange visas... Moving back and forth between several websites and apps, and after a long preparation process, everything is finally ready. Task complete.

Wait — something's off. Having everything ready isn't the end point; it's the beginning. But notice this: compared with the crowd of companies eager to do business with you before a trip, once you're actually traveling you suddenly have no idea who to turn to for eating, drinking and having fun.

Judging from the brutal price war that has left nearly the entire industry — Qunar, Tongcheng, Tuniu and the like — facing heavy losses, competition in China's travel services sector is cut-throat. And for an individual traveler, especially one traveling independently, what these companies compete over is only the money at the two ends of a trip: the flight to get there and the hotel at the destination. In reality, apart from shopping, there's a big chunk of spending they never capture.

The bad news is that hotels and airlines are also fighting for this space, and one way or another they hold their own advantages. The room left for these online travel agencies keeps shrinking.

So why not also make money from the market in between the head and the tail?

At the northwest corner of Chiang Mai's old town, you'll find a small courtyard like this: a European-style building with its roof painted entirely green, bearing a giant "Q" that you can probably spot from a plane. The surrounding wall carries hand-painted Thai-style artwork, and from time to time passing tourists stop to pose for photos with the 3D elephant.

At the front desk are several Thai staff members who speak Chinese, offering travelers basic services such as consultation, luggage storage, WiFi, and minibuses connecting to attractions. Regular small local events or parties are held here, and travel products are sold too — everything from Chiang Mai classics like elephant camps and jungle ziplining to an exclusively designed City Walk tour route priced at 118 yuan.

This isn't the work of some small local Thai travel agency — it's a job done by Qyer. You can think of it as an unofficial information center, plus a small travel agency that sells travel products.

According to the "2014 Outbound Independent Travel Industry Report" published by Qyer, among 109 million Chinese outbound travel users, more than 77 million chose independent travel, accounting for over 70%. Among them, young travelers aged 21–30 made up 62.4%. Young people are no longer satisfied with whistle-stop sightseeing tours; to them, experiencing life somewhere else is what travel is all about.

Qyer must have spotted the opportunity in its own report, but competition was already stirring. In fact, as independent travel has risen, more and more companies have set their sights on what you spend at your destination, wanting to win you over during the trip itself.

Industry giants like Trip.com Group launched a "local experiences" business in 2014, standing on the shoulders of the "flights + hotels" giant and hoping to sell attraction tickets along the way. A batch of more vertical startups also emerged — for instance, "Taozai Lushang," founded in 2012, whose core product was "Book on the Go" (边走边订); Klook, which raised a $5 million Series A last year and likewise hoisted a banner of distinctive destination travel experiences; "Zouzhe Lvxing" ("Traveling On"), which completed a 51.46 million yuan Series A last year and entered through destination charter cars; and Woniubang, a tour bus linking small attractions at destinations...

The flight and hotel markets are already relatively mature, while actual spending on food, drink and fun at a destination may account for more than half of what you pay for a single trip. Qyer has joined the fight for this market by working as a local ground operator — but can it handle these grueling, dirty jobs?

QDaily Research Lab once ran a small survey, [*What Troubles Have You Run Into While Traveling Independently Abroad?*](http://www.qdaily.com/mobs/890.html), collecting problems readers had encountered while traveling overseas. Let's see to what extent Qyer's project can solve these problems:

- You arrive at the destination but your luggage doesn't, and communication with the airline goes poorly √
- Can't find a place that sells SIM cards √
- When asking for directions, the other person doesn't speak English / you can't understand the accent √
- Can't read a French/Japanese/Thai menu and don't know how to order
- Don't know how much tip to leave at a restaurant √
- Want to buy something at the night market but have no cash on you
- Phone lost √
- Passport lost √
- Sudden illness requiring hospitalization but your visa has expired √
- Encountering racial discrimination
- Return flight is at night; you checked out in the morning and leaving your luggage at the hotel to go back for it is too much hassle √
- Arrive at the airport and discover your return ticket was booked for next month
- Something left behind at the hotel, but you're already back home √

Actually, some of these problems can already be answered in Qyer's forums. And for many years after its founding in 2004, Qyer was an independent outbound travel community, with advertising as almost its only profit model. If it had stayed a community for sharing travel journals and providing travel guides, Qyer might have been able to survive on its accumulated users and brand.

But competition in this business is intensifying. Besides old rivals like Mafengwo, new players keep appearing. From something as small as Breadtrip, an app for sharing travel photos, to something as big as Trip.com Group, all of them encourage you to write travel journals and share guides. Even WeChat Moments has, to some extent, become an alternative platform where many people post photos and write travel notes. The barrier to "sharing travel journals" is getting lower and lower.

At the same time, how to monetize has always been the perennial problem for travel websites built on communities: despite having amassed large numbers of users and content, and sitting upstream of the whole chain, they are too far from the transaction stage to profit directly. By contrast, travel websites closer to the transaction stage find it easier to become giants / make money — after researching guides on Qyer for a month, many users' habitual path used to be to go book tickets on Trip.com Group or Alitrip.

In fact, starting in 2011, Qyer began commercialization attempts, successively moving into retailing products like flights and hotels beyond content. This January, Qyer raised a nearly $60 million Series D round led by UTour, and its next step is to draw on UTour's supply-chain resources to raise the conversion rate of Qyer users.

According to data provided by Qyer, from its opening last August to March 15 this year, the Chiang Mai store received more than 6,200 users, averaging only about 30 visitors a day. Whether compared with the nearly 8 million Chinese travelers who went to Thailand in 2015, or with Qyer's own transaction volume, it's not in the same order of magnitude.

Qyer named this project Q-Home. This is only the first one; in Qyer's plans for this year, it intends to open Q-Homes in Kyoto, Paris, London and Queenstown, New Zealand. Clearly they feel it's time to publicize: in March, they dispatched a video team to camp out in Chiang Mai for a month to shoot a promotional video, and invited a group of domestic media to visit the Chiang Mai store.

"Q-Home isn't independent; it's embedded in Qyer's commercialization — and Qyer's commercialization is definitely something that requires committing all our resources," Han Zhe, COO of Qyer, told the media on site. Han Zhe's previous startup was "Tonight's Hotel Specials"; after joining Qyer in 2014, he has been mainly responsible for Qyer's commercialization.

![Qyer COO Han Zhe](/uploads/image/201604/09531ca1594d.jpg)

For Qyer, with heavyweight players like Trip.com Group and Tuniu in the field, if it only sells homogeneous agency products, fighting head-on for traffic against the big OTAs is a losing battle. With flights and hotels basically settled, non-standardized local experiences have become the focus of the contest. "We choose some small, grueling categories. We can't fight across that many categories, but investing $100 million to build one category is worth a try," Han Zhe said.

That category is local experiences. Q-Home is the core support of Qyer's local-experiences category; the Chiang Mai team is not just there to serve walk-in customers at the store — its more important value is local sourcing. Behind the five Q-Homes lies local procurement capability in five countries.

Procurement capability is crucial to the travel industry. The traditional travel industry chain generally runs: resource holders (e.g., attraction operators) → local ground operators → tour organizers → retail channels → users. Many websites currently sell destination products, mostly procured through agency channels; the offerings are highly homogeneous, and because they sit at the end of the supply chain, margins are actually very low.

In Chiang Mai, Qyer's procurement method is direct sourcing and outright buying — for example, buying 100,000 attraction tickets early in the year. "You can think of it as us doing wholesale," Han Zhe explained. The result of shortening the travel-product supply chain this way is that Qyer's margin in Chiang Mai is several times higher than the OTA industry average in China.

Han Zhe said the Chiang Mai Q-Home is already profitable in its own right, with most revenue coming from the distribution of local travel products (attraction tickets, etc.). Besides selling to the Chinese market, it also distributes through KKday, a Taiwan-based local-experiences company.

From the traveler's point of view, a local organization that can provide Chinese-language service is naturally a good thing. The factors considered when buying a travel product are nothing more than: good price, good product, convenient operation, and timely after-sales handling.

But as we said earlier, independent travelers today are mostly young people, and they differ from the customers of traditional travel agencies: they don't want an agency to arrange everything for them, but they do have expectations for differentiated services. Qyer is conscious of this.

So in Han Zhe's vision, he hopes the ratio of "standardized products" (attraction tickets, etc.) to "non-standardized products" (self-sourced routes like City Walk) can reach 5:5 — all standardized products would mean sinking into a red-ocean price war, while all non-standardized would be genuinely too heavy. So Qyer hopes to drive volume through standardized products and lift brand influence through personalized self-operated products — which is also the part users are willing to pay a premium for. This also means Qyer must pour more money and energy into it.

Beyond procuring destination products, Qyer also develops its own travel products locally, such as City Walk — which is another role of Q-Home: making self-operated products.

For self-operated products, Han Zhe said their core logic is whether the market is big enough, whether the demand is hard demand, and whether team capability matches — and City Walk satisfies all of these. It is essentially a city walking route designed by Qyer itself, led by a guide taking a group of no more than 10 people. It sounds like a Qyer-brand "boutique tour group." City Walk currently has routes in more than 20 cities worldwide, and Qyer hopes to scale it up; in Kyoto, they simply built a guide-training school before even building the routes.

![Chiang Mai City Walk](/uploads/image/201604/6d113e32fca5.jpg)

City Walk demands a great deal of its route designers and guides; the current team includes no shortage of professionals such as architecture designers, geography teachers, and history graduate students. This reliance on talent is, in a sense, also a constraint. For example, Anne, the route designer of the Chiang Mai City Walk, spent three months on research, scouting, route design and the narration script — and the subsequent four Q-Homes will basically also depend on her leading the team to design City Walk products. On the other hand, if City Walk achieves scaled production in the future, can quality still be guaranteed, and will the line with mass-market "tour groups" grow increasingly blurred? That remains unknown.

They also plan to open a Thai cooking school in Chiang Mai, because the existing five or six cooking schools all run from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m., which doesn't suit Chinese travelers on tight itineraries — so Qyer's cooking school will operate from 7 p.m. to 9 p.m.

"At first we were all agency, but later we found a pile of problems. Chinese people's travel habits differ enormously from foreigners'; existing products have no Chinese-language support, are all built the foreign way, and are expensive — they simply won't sell," Han Zhe said. "So self-operation was actually forced on us; for this category to grow, this is the only way."

One advantage Qyer has in making self-operated products is that it has already built an independent-travel brand. The word "Qyer" itself has become a way of life, with a certain pull among young people. One piece of evidence: last year Qyer opened a Taobao store selling merchandise, and sales were surprisingly good. For a phone case priced at 49 yuan printed with "Addicted to the World" and the Qyer logo, the product review section was full of declarations of love for that tagline and for Qyer.

![Some merchandise from Qyer's Taobao store](/uploads/image/201604/07c3abd81de8.jpg)

If the goal is only to build the brand through personalized products, City Walk might be a decent choice. Sales of the Chiang Mai City Walk are currently strong (Han Zhe joked, "This means at least 10 Chinese people in Chiang Mai every day really know how to have fun"), but compared with the number of Chinese tourists in Chiang Mai, it's still far from the same order of magnitude. The price of 118 yuan per person guarantees profit margin, but for now, scaling up still looks extremely hard.

Qyer has gone from a UGC sharing platform to a heavy-asset company — although they shy away from words like "transformation." "When the market and capital are both driving a company's volume upward, the 'commercialization' Qyer talks about today isn't how to commercialize as a media outlet (selling ads), but how to break through the media bottleneck and commercialize in a larger business domain (selling products)," Han Zhe said.

Looking purely at the finances, the cost of operating a Q-Home isn't high — mainly labor and rent. But the Chiang Mai Q-Home spent two years just finding a location; assembling the team relied on recruiting a local Chinese practitioner who understood Thai; and opening the store required applying for five business licenses covering tourism, restaurant, trading company and more — an extremely tedious process. This determines that Q-Home and Qyer's self-operated products do not have the ability to replicate quickly, and can hardly achieve scaled production — even though Qyer claims the margins of its self-operated products in Kyoto and Chiang Mai both exceed 50%.

Competition in the broader guide/content space is also getting fiercer, and now it has stepped with one foot into a market that is even more competitive and uncontrollable. This is rough, grueling work. The upside is that it sits in a market expected to keep growing 30% a year — possibly a 78.64 billion yuan market.

![(Chart source: iResearch, "2015 China Online Travel Vacation Industry Research Report")](/uploads/image/201604/ac1e915546e0.jpg)

Another upside is that Qyer may understand better what users need at their destination — but this also tests whether this company, born of the "fresh and artsy" set, has stronger control over the travel industry chain.

Q-Home's brand value to Qyer now looks far greater than what the store operation itself earns. This is also evident in Q-Home's site selection. Chiang Mai was chosen as the first stop because Thailand is the destination of many Chinese travelers' first trip abroad, and Chiang Mai is small enough to reach more user groups more easily. Of the coming stores, apart from Paris, Kyoto, London and Queenstown, New Zealand are all relatively compact cities with concentrated populations — so that they can cover local sourcing resources while reaching more travelers and building the brand.

Considering the commercialization strategy around local food, drink and fun, what Qyer wants to do is a very hard thing, and it will remain just as hard in the future. And it should also be a fairly cash-burning affair — Qyer's founder Xiao Yi expressed his wish to go public early this year.
