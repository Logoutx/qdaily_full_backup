"Freitag talks about sustainability, but it still gets people to buy more bags — isn't that a contradiction?"

At 6 p.m. on September 21, 2018, nearly 600 people queued outside a fair-faced concrete building at the corner of Julu Road and Fumin Road in Shanghai. Markus and Daniel, who had just wrapped up registering their China company, showed up in baseball caps with backpacks slung over their shoulders, planning to personally welcome fans into the store. But many of the visitors rushed straight up to the second floor the moment they stepped in, and within minutes were carrying four or five bags—each priced around a thousand yuan—under their arms and in their hands. Only after the grabbing was mostly done did they remember to greet the two brothers, ask for autographs and snap photos.

In China, brothers Daniel Freitag and Markus Freitag are no better known than the bags named after their own family name.

Nor do they really know who exactly is buying their products. In 2004, Glossy, a store in Shanghai's Xintiandi, became Freitag's first Chinese dealer; its owner Guo Ben was a Shanghai native who had studied in Switzerland. But it wasn't until ten years later, in 2014, that the founders first saw their enormous fan base through WeChat. Another three years after that, they finally decided to come to China in person and open a store, finding local partners to work with.

Freitag has 8 such semi-directly-operated stores worldwide, similar to the one on Julu Road. Internally they are called By-Stores, a distinction from dealers who are purely wholesale-retail relationships.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181107012053Vpv6s47qin30QwOg.jpg-w600)

Twenty-five years ago, designers Markus Freitag and Daniel Freitag, then twenty years old, took inspiration from the countless trucks rolling along Europe's highways, using reclaimed waterproof truck-tarpaulin for the bag body, car seat belts for the straps, and bicycle inner tubes for the edging—the first bike messenger bag made from waste was born. In Swiss German, Freitag means "Friday."

Today the brand remains independently operated, producing 450,000 grungy-looking bags and accessories a year. The pattern on every Freitag bag is one of a kind, and for urban creative types, a Freitag is something like a tattoo, a bicycle, or a pair of Air Jordans—an expression of individuality, a lifestyle, even a collectible.

On a page of the Freitag website aimed at dealers, there is a line that reads: ["Our experience shows that the more choice there is, the more successful Freitag products are."](https://www.freitag.ch/en/wholesale)

In a café next to the new Shanghai store, the two brothers explained to us how Freitag, now with 200 employees, has built a labor-intensive enterprise in Switzerland—where wages are the highest in the world—and grown into a brand whose influence far exceeds its size.

In 1993, that first truck tarpaulin was washed in the bathtub of Markus's apartment, then sent to a studio to be cut and sewn into a messenger bag. That workflow has since moved to a factory in northern Zurich, Switzerland. The company employs 5 buyers dedicated to liaising with roughly 5,000 trucking companies across Europe, who each year source 460 metric tons of reclaimed truck tarpaulin. The material goes into industrial washing machines at the plant, is washed with recycled rainwater and hung to dry, and bag designers then cut and sew it according to their own aesthetics.

Daniel said: "Cutting through the stiff truck tarp to make bags is exhausting—it's like going to the gym. But the moment you open up a tarpaulin bag, it's a bit like Christmas."

In the process, about one-third becomes waste; the rest turns into bags. "We almost never sell out, but at the start it really was hard to predict how many of a given pattern we should make," Markus said. With the growing number of dealers worldwide, they can now better balance the different patterns. Dealers can't choose the bag color schemes themselves—"they can only tell us which colors they don't want."

For now, Freitag has only reclaimed a portion of the retired truck tarpaulin discarded across Europe. And every trucking company's deal with Freitag is different: "Some will say: 'Cool, just give us a call.' But it's a matter of luck; others will call us up and say, 'Next year we'll be retiring the tarpaulins from 50 trucks.'"

![Left: Daniel Freitag; Right: Markus Freitag](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201811070141388TJjFYuapxRCL4Ht.jpg-w600)

Freitag does no traditional advertising or marketing, relying instead on media coverage, events and word of mouth. "I think the great thing about Freitag is that we have a story to tell. It's really not just about product and promotion—it's about how we make bags, how we recycle." At the opening event, Markus and Daniel connected their phone photo albums to a screen and invited everyone to pick any photo that caught their eye; they would tell you what had been happening at that moment—say, a foodie party with fans in Hong Kong, or an excursion to France in search of recyclable fabrics.

Freitag's aesthetic appeal is a bit like ripped jeans, yet from the recycling-and-environment angle it stands on exactly the opposite side of jeans. On this China trip, the brothers spent most of their time giving talks around the country—at venues including Tsinghua University and the Central Academy of Fine Arts—fine-tuning the content to each audience. ["At first we said this was recycling. But over the years we have learned to describe what we do more precisely, breaking sustainability down into finer questions: rebuilding culture."](http://www.thatsmags.com/shanghai/post/25485/freitag-brothers-on-taking-their-brand-to-china)

It sounds mystical, but it is precisely this cultural communication that has built a strong intimacy between Freitag and its consumers. Just as IKEA's FRAKTA shopping bag has become a visual badge of identity for urban renters, Freitag weaves multiple transport-related images into the portrait of a different urban crowd: trucks and highways, messenger bags and bicycles, container headquarters and globalization—driving toward modernity while, remarkably, remaining sustainable.

![Freitag's headquarters and flagship store in Zurich, Switzerland, 26 meters tall, built from containers that had been in use for over 20 years.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181107021610rZza8xtM1P5Hu3Qh.png-w600)

This leads many people, when telling the Freitag story, to overlook the fact that it is still a business.

A student from the Central Academy of Fine Arts asked after one of the talks: "Freitag talks about sustainability, but it still gets people to buy more bags—isn't that a contradiction?"

Markus's answer fell somewhere between pro-consumerism and anti-consumerism: "I believe our products are more sustainable than any other brand's—you can buy 5 Freitag bags and still have a better carbon footprint than if you bought 1 bag from another brand."

Made of recycled plastic, two-piece and detachable, you can find the same shelving at any Freitag dealer—it is the brand's signature retail symbol. On a dealer's first delivery after being approved, they are shipped from Switzerland together with the first batch of bags. The V30 SKID shelving system was originally designed by then-Freitag-intern Colin Schaelli and won a Swiss design award in 2009, allowing dealers to use space efficiently and expand at any time.

That is the brand's sales secret: display as much choice as possible—there is always one bag that is yours.

![Shanghai's Freitag Store has two floors and is the brand's largest retail space in China, able to stock 1,100 bags in different patterns. "We often run into customers who ask us to help them choose." Zhang Zunxiang is the founder of Harbook+ (Wanli Shuxiang), the local partner of Shanghai's Freitag Store, and has run a design bookshop for many years; his friends call him Gavin. "Being torn is exactly right. The longer they linger, the better the odds of a sale."

"We did a rough count, excluding the old customers who know Freitag models by heart. If it's a new customer referred by a friend, it takes an average of an hour to decide—even for a very small bag. If someone comes alone, without friends along, it takes even longer."

One month after opening, the "Freitag Store Shanghai By Harbook+" sold more than a thousand bags; according to Zhang Zunxiang, it has already been restocked twice. "Each restock is quite large. The original plan was to sell 500 a month, and actual retail numbers are well above expectations."

Compared with the design itself and the sustainability philosophy, the retail side is often downplayed in the Freitag story, yet it is an important component of why the brand succeeds. Freitag mostly does not talk to consumers directly; that job is done by its 364 dealers and 9 By-Stores around the world.

Fan Qi, who joined Freitag last August, is currently the brand's only Chinese employee, mainly responsible for developing the China market, liaising with dealers, and drafting marketing plans. On Freitag's criteria for choosing partner dealers, Fan Qi listed these points:

- Location: preferably a street-facing shop, and "we don't look for A Street—we look for B Street (a back street)"
- Category: what the store sells should overlap with Freitag's user base
- Decor style: not too street-fashion or hypebeast—our shelving needs a fairly clean space/wall
- Price: Freitag bags should sit in the mid-range of that store's price points

More than a third of Freitag's 22 Chinese dealers are in Shanghai, which is also why the brand picked Julu Road for its new store—within a 3-kilometer radius, two other shops also sell Freitag. "Our sales have gradually formed a kind of network in Shanghai," Markus said. "Because every bag is unique, your favorite bag might be at another shop at the end of the street—you can go browse."

In fact, Freitag's relationship with retailers is a fairly old-fashioned model in the fashion industry—the brand focuses on maintaining allure and scarcity, while distribution is left to buyer stores and boutique department stores that carry community influence.

The difference is that for Freitag, which trumpets sustainability, fashion chain landmarks like Dover Street Market are too commercial, while independent multi-brand buyer stores often lack their own mature logistics and warehousing facilities. Many Chinese Freitag dealers make slim profits on the bags; on top of that, the products are air-freighted from Switzerland, and the ordering-to-customs-clearance-to-logistics pipeline takes a month, so stockouts of in-demand patterns happen from time to time.

After registering a company in China, Freitag is preparing to build its own warehousing and logistics in Shanghai. "It will help us a lot and cut out many steps," Zhang Zunxiang said. "It's expected to be completed around the end of next year, and costs will come down a lot by then. For now it's still air freight from Switzerland—customs duties and shipping are both steep, and a lot of the margin in between is simply gone."

Zhang Zunxiang learned around May that Freitag was looking for a partner to open a store. At the time, Harbook+, the design-goods-and-bookshop he ran in Shanghai's Réel mall, had been forced to close over property issues, and he had already been planning to find a new location. Through multiple channels he heard that a 500-square-meter street-facing shop on Julu Road was for lease, and he immediately signed a long-term contract. Counting renovation and the cost of the first batch of goods, the investment in this store exceeded 4 million yuan. Zhang Zunxiang said his goal is to make the shop an urban landmark like Merci, the Paris lifestyle concept store; the Freitag partnership came as a happy accident.

"Give us priority on the good stuff (patterns), hold your events at our place first—that's enough." What Zhang Zunxiang values is the foot traffic Freitag, as the anchor brand, brings to his bookshop-and-goods store. "There will be a lot of people at Freitag's opening. But sales going forward will still depend on all the goods in the back."

To catch the founders' first joint trip to China, a location was found by the end of June, contracts signed by both sides in July, and the new store's renovation period compressed to just 26 days. One month after opening, the goods-and-books area—which occupies more than half the store—still hadn't been fully set up and formally opened. That also means Harbook+ missed the two holidays with the highest foot traffic after the Freitag Store's opening: Mid-Autumn Festival and National Day.

Harbook+ also has its own Taobao store, selling a carefully curated selection of moderately priced Nordic small goods and lamps that don't depend on an offline experience, as well as the Danish furniture brand Normann Copenhagen, which had previously authorized them to sell its products. But this time, Freitag did not extend online sales to Harbook+.

This, too, differs from apparel fashion brands that follow the wholesale model; the latter usually hand their e-commerce business to quality retailers as well. By contrast, even though every bag's pattern is unique, Freitag is more standardized, and the brand still has the bandwidth to manage e-commerce itself.

"The timing isn't ripe yet. I think for this bag's die-hard fans, once they see the color scheme and colors, they already know the style. It's actually suited to being sold online," Zhang Zunxiang felt. Perhaps precisely because of this, Freitag for now keeps the initiative on online sales in its own hands.

In mid-September, before the store opening, Zhang Zunxiang and Fan Qi flew to Bangkok for a meeting. Bangkok is Freitag's best-performing market in Asia. At the meeting, By-Store owners from various regions met one another and exchanged operating know-how, while the brand presented its upcoming new products and marketing plans. In November, Freitag announced a temporary position opening worldwide: [Global Vice President of Cheerfulness](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/wtTpq50I8erxKVswvZHUgg)—whose duty, for the brand's 25th anniversary, is to plan a series of content that "achieves sustainable growth in global cheerfulness."

It is still that familiar Freitag language; only, compared with growth in cheerfulness, the focus of Freitag's future sales growth undoubtedly lands on China. "Headquarters has invested a lot of time and resources in China and has high hopes," Fan Qi said, noting that the company already has very concrete plans to add local dealers.

After the Tsinghua talk, an audience member asked: how do you recycle Freitag bags?

Markus Freitag said: "For a long time I didn't need to answer this question, because Freitag bags are extremely durable. But after 25 years, we're about to reach the moment when someone says, my bag is worn out! What did we do? We answered the question with a creative project."

Playing on the screen was a 2015 [art exhibition](https://www.freitag.ch/en/adabsurdum) the two brothers did jointly with mudac, the Museum of Contemporary Design and Applied Arts. On the streets of Zurich and Lausanne in Switzerland, they persuaded people to freely give them Freitag bags too old to use anymore; after collecting 100, they cut and resewed them into a colorful truck tarpaulin.

![Freitag ad absurdum](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181107013636CSygdfvbiKl69IW3.jpg-w600)

But over 25 years, Freitag has recycled and re-produced more than 4 million bags, many of them sold to the same person—and there are plenty of such consumers in China. An [article](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/aSnf0ifTX_PGZW7ups-8cA) pushed by Freitag's official WeChat account on October 16 introduced one such Guangdong boy, known in "F circles" as "Qiao Bangzhu / Emperor of the Universe." Born in 1992, with 5+ years of bag-collecting under his belt, he runs a fashion boutique. He has already bought more than 40 Freitag bags.

It is all just as Markus said when describing Freitag's relationship with those European trucking companies: "The moment you start asking for something, waste starts getting expensive too."
