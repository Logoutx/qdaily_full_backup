We spoke with the event's founder, Will Dean.

Climbing out of a freezing mud pit is hardly a pleasant experience, but every year more than 2.5 million people scramble to jump into one. They are taking part in an off-road obstacle challenge called Tough Mudder, and the mud pit is just one of the wide array of obstacles the event has dreamed up.

Will Dean is the founder of Tough Mudder. In an interview with QDaily, Will Dean carefully corrected one small detail: "Tough Mudder is not a race, and not a competition. We call it an event. There are no prizes and no medals. We hope participants will experience Tough Mudder with a different attitude."

Playing down the competitive side of sport — that is a common thread among the new sporting events that have emerged around the world in recent years. Take The Color Run, a running event that douses participants in clouds of colored powder: the point is not the running but reveling in the powder or taking selfies.

Of course, Tough Mudder has not gone to that extreme either — after all, on some websites it is billed as the world's toughest obstacle-course event, and it makes real demands on participants' fitness and mental fortitude. Will Dean is looking for a balance: neither as purely a test of self-challenge as a marathon, nor like The Color Run, where the entertainment overwhelms the sport itself.

The obstacles in Tough Mudder play the most important role in maintaining the balance between sport and entertainment. Take the one called Tarzan: you leap from a high platform, grab hold of a swinging bar in front of you, and use its momentum to reach a bell further ahead. If you miss the bar — sorry — you drop into the mud pit below.

Will Dean's favorite obstacle is called the Loch Ness Monster. Participants first jump into a pool where several smooth rotating barrels, each 18 meters in circumference, await; with the help of teammates, they must climb over them. "First, it's fun. Second, it requires teamwork and a certain level of fitness. Finally, there's a bit of a thrill — rolling off the barrel is a real rush," Will Dean said. In short, whatever the obstacle, it keeps Tough Mudder from being as boring as a marathon while adding more of an athletic element.

In fact, all of these factors are taken into account at the very start of designing every obstacle. "We make sure obstacle designs stay consistent with Tough Mudder's brand values and the company's mission. They must meet four criteria: demonstrate teamwork and friendship; be fun and exciting; be physically challenging; and psychologically feel a little scary."

These criteria come from Will Dean's thinking when he founded Tough Mudder: "When I started Tough Mudder, I didn't want to create a company that cared only about revenue and profit. I wanted to make the world a better place, to teach people to work together, to make people happier."

Tough Mudder was an idea Will Dean came up with while studying at Harvard Business School. Before that, he had served as a counterterrorism officer at the UK's Foreign and Commonwealth Office. Life inside the bureaucracy was not always interesting, and Will Dean found that what fascinated him was launching, running, and managing a project. That made him realize the business world was probably the best place for him, so he went to Harvard Business School.

During his years at HBS — where he already had habits of fitness and exercise — he came to feel that sports could be a way to realize his ideal of changing the world, and the idea of an obstacle-course race began to take shape.

One day in 2008, over drinks at a Boston bar with his three friends, they discussed names for the obstacle run. Like a light bulb suddenly switching on, Will Dean said to his three friends: "How about Tough Mudder?" With his friends' approval, Will Dean rode his bike home through the bitter winter streets, registered the Tough Mudder domain name, then cycled back to the bar to finish the drink in his glass.

In Tough Mudder's early days, Will Dean's team was tiny: besides himself and co-founder Guy Livingstone, there were only four unpaid interns. It was those six people who put on the first Tough Mudder event in 2010.

Looking back, the adjectives Will Dean uses are hard and lonely. Searching for a venue, he was met with all kinds of mockery. "You're a Harvard Business School graduate, and this is the best you could come up with?" Will Dean recalled them saying.

Still, Will Dean eventually found a ski resort in Pennsylvania to host Tough Mudder. They built a registration website and began recruiting participants through Facebook. The Facebook marketing worked so well that it scared Will Dean. "When we saw 5,000 people sign up, our faces went white," he said. "We had expected only 500."

That was not good news — it meant the course itself had to be adjusted, the logistics had to be adjusted, the medical setup had to be adjusted. All of this put more pressure on the six-person team. The only comfort may have been that the fees from those 5,000 registrations became the most important source of startup capital.

When event day arrived, things did not go entirely smoothly. The water prepared for participants ran out early, long lines formed in the parking lot — but fortunately the event itself went off without a hitch, and participants were reasonably satisfied. The final accounting showed that the first Tough Mudder broke even.

With the first event's experience under their belt, the rest was not too hard. Tough Mudder expanded quickly, and today it has grown into a company with 200 employees, valued at US$100 million, hosting 120 Tough Mudder events around the world each year and drawing more than 2.5 million participants.

Along the way, controversy followed. In 2013, a 28-year-old participant drowned while crossing an obstacle. Will Dean later acknowledged in an interview that accidents do happen: "But compared with triathlons, our events are 20 times safer."

In this interview with QDaily, Will Dean repeatedly brought up safety measures. Safety research is conducted at the obstacle-design stage, and at every official event, safety staff stand watch beside every obstacle.

Another controversy came from plagiarism claims. Billy Wilson, who founded Tough Guy in 1987, believed Will Dean had copied his idea and sued Tough Mudder. In 2011, Billy Wilson and Will Dean eventually reached an out-of-court settlement.

"I didn't invent obstacle-course racing, and I didn't invent mud," Will Dean said. "We just wanted to create the biggest and best obstacles, and the spirit of teamwork."

These two points are, in Will Dean's view, Tough Mudder's biggest difference from other similar obstacle races. At Tough Mudder, a dedicated obstacle innovation lab develops new obstacles. Around 500 different ideas are born each year, but after passing hurdle after hurdle — tested against criteria such as safety, feasibility, and whether they reflect the values Tough Mudder advocates — only three to five obstacles ultimately make it into reality.

For this entry into China, Tough Mudder partnered with IMG, one of the world's largest event-management companies — the same IMG that helped The Color Run enter China.

"Because of language and cultural differences, partnering with IMG was the best choice," Will Dean said. Tough Mudder had previously entered countries such as the UK, Australia, and Germany, whose cultures are largely the same as America's, which made things relatively easy. For countries like Mexico, Tough Mudder chose to license operations to local organizers.

IMG's help for Tough Mudder is all-encompassing — event hosting, marketing, government relations, IMG is involved in every aspect — so much so that Will Dean uses the word "supporting" to describe Tough Mudder's position in the partnership.

In the course of the collaboration, IMG did a great deal of localization. At Tough Mudder itself, the post-event party adopted celebration styles better suited to Chinese tastes. In marketing, IMG translated large amounts of content into Chinese for WeChat. They also partnered with the gym chain Will's and the fitness app Keep to promote Tough Mudder. They even sent teams into parks to find people working out and organized training camps for them.

But in Will Dean's view, Tough Mudder's biggest advantage is Tough Mudder itself. "If you go running in New York's Central Park wearing a Tough Mudder T-shirt, people will come up and high-five you." Describing Tough Mudder's expansion, the phrase Will Dean used most often was word of mouth.

Will Dean played a video whose protagonist was a Tough Mudder participant named Randy Pierce — the only difference being that he is blind. In the video, Randy is crossing the obstacle called "Tarzan" mentioned earlier: he first raises a walking stick, probing the air for the swinging bar's position. Then he hands the stick to the two fellow participants who have been accompanying him, takes aim, and leaps. He grabs the bar and holds on in midair for a second, then drops into the mud pit below.

Tough Mudder gathered participants like Randy together to film a documentary, which will air on CBS on December 25. In Will Dean's view, such documentaries are Tough Mudder's best advertising — they raise awareness and also convey the idea that Tough Mudder can make the world a better place.

Participants like Randy are also being used by Tough Mudder to overcome Chinese participants' psychological barriers. Tough Mudder is a bit hard, but you don't need to be a professional athlete to take part. "For some obstacles, if you find them too difficult, you can go around them."

Still, Will Dean seems unconcerned about their prospects in the Chinese market, even though sports have not yet become a lifestyle there, and sports are not yet mature enough to be called an industry. "China has a huge population. The proportion of people who might take part in Tough Mudder may not be high, but because the base is so large, the number of registrations won't be small."
