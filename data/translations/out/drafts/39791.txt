For companies like Alibaba, Tencent, and Samsung, architecture is an indispensable part of corporate image — but it is no longer as simple as "a landmark."

Eric Philips is a partner at NBBJ Asia. He still remembers clearly that until a little over three years ago, the tech companies NBBJ encountered in China all shared a similar anxiety: "They were curious about what Amazon's latest offices had, they paid attention to Google moving foosball tables into its work areas, mobile dining tables, coffee bars... They were hyper-sensitive to every little move their American peers made in their own offices; some of them wished they could just copy it all wholesale."

Others, openly at a loss, would put it bluntly: "I'm not sure what I want either. Maybe it's that kind of work atmosphere. Why don't you show me some case studies first — when we get to the right place, I'll tell you."

But today, faced with beanbag chairs and ping-pong tables in designers' reference images, NBBJ's Chinese clients are starting to ask a different question: "What does this mean for me?"

As early as 2012, NBBJ designed the headquarters building for Alipay — office space that stretches out in a zigzag shape and is expected to be completed this June, with above-ground floor area exceeding 80,000 square meters. People have jokingly likened the building's form to, viewed from above, a replica of "ORZ" (an internet emoticon carrying the meaning of "worship" or "frustration").

Eric Philips explains the design concept this way: "Just as Alipay has built that powerful urban commercial center in the virtual world, we wanted the architecture to embody this scene of the free flow of money and goods, creating a series of connection points that simulate internet transaction nodes — or, to put it another way, the smooth feeling of a main commercial street in a bustling business district."

![Rendering of the Alipay building](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170414111854zSZAMQPi4VTWK5Do.jpg-w600)

NBBJ was founded in the United States in 1943 and entered the Chinese market in the 1990s by participating in the renovation of the Beijing Hotel. One of the firm's specialties is designing office spaces for major tech companies, including Google, Amazon, Samsung, Tencent, and Alibaba. Beyond that, public projects — chiefly healthcare architecture — also fall within the scope of its business.

On March 20, 2017, the firm officially unveiled its design for Alibaba's new headquarters campus. Located in Phase IV of the Xixi campus in Hangzhou, the new building has a floor area of 180,000 square meters and is expected to be completed in 2019.

NBBJ is not the only design partner Alibaba has worked with to create architectural space. Australian firm Hassell designed another Alibaba headquarters on the other side of Hangzhou, in the Binjiang district, while Phase I of the Xixi campus is "Taobao City," designed by Japanese architect Kengo Kuma. The cluster of office buildings Kuma designed is arranged around a central expanse of wetland landscape.

The new project's distinguishing feature is this: to bring people and nature even closer together, NBBJ worked its landscaping magic on relatively scattered greenery across adjacent plots, and the office spaces are organized around several internal courtyards — every employee's desk is within a one-minute walk of outdoor space.

![Night rendering of the Alibaba headquarters](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170414112138nLIAjszUSlVrXx08.jpg-w600)

![Rendering of an internal courtyard at the Alibaba headquarters](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170414120751ShRPbLIkKyi4AO7D.jpg-w600)

"We believe that being closer to the natural environment can heighten the brain's activity and stimulate the thinking of people in creative industries," Eric Philips told QDaily. The theory was put forward by a research fund program NBBJ launched in 2014, in which the firm collaborates with scholars in neuroscience and the social and behavioral sciences to explore the intersections between these disciplines and architectural design. The first project's partner was John Medina, a professor of molecular biology at the University of Washington.

Because of their sheer scale, building office towers for large companies like Alibaba can put a number of cross-disciplinary theories to the test. For example, the late geographer Jay Appleton proposed the "Prospect-Refuge Theory," which is often used to explain the anxiety that open-plan offices create — because of biological instinct, people prefer to work in a space that is open, allows them to observe their surroundings, yet still affords relative independence and privacy, as if in a cave amid open wilderness. According to this theory, an open office space needs to have a few "caves" added in.

But NBBJ's projects carry significance beyond design itself. On the one hand, their enormous scale usually transforms the local environment, infrastructure, and even community culture; on the other, for companies like Alibaba, Tencent, and Samsung, architecture is an indispensable part of corporate image — just as a city usually has its own landmark, these companies also need a sufficiently "weighty" building to mark themselves.

### Landmark! Landmark!

Comparing the differences between NBBJ's two projects with Alibaba, Eric believes that Alibaba's official listing on the New York Stock Exchange in September 2014 was an important turning point. Beyond the difference in architectural scale between the two projects, "today, this multinational company has to consider how the global market will gaze upon it."

![Alibaba's official listing on the NYSE (photo / The New York Times)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170414115011J16USkPYwo7dFBDA.jpg-w600)

"In the United States, we rarely use words like 'iconic' or 'distinctive' when we talk about architecture. I'm speaking of cultural differences — neither is better or worse. In fact, I can understand people's obsession with landmarks here; in the Chinese context, skylines, building heights, and forms have long occupied the focal point of urban discourse."

Early tech entrepreneurs, Chinese and foreign alike, didn't care about the exterior appearance of their offices. In Silicon Valley, where startups live and die as a matter of course, changing only the interior space design was simply more cost-effective. The offices Facebook originally moved into had belonged to a company called Sun Microsystems, which went bankrupt and was acquired by Oracle. Mark Zuckerberg flipped Sun Microsystems' sign board over, painted his company's signature Like button on the back, and kept many of the previous logos on the glass. This "anti-aesthetic," hacker "couldn't-care-less" attitude carried over to their new headquarters: even though they brought in Frank Gehry, the architect behind the Guggenheim Museum in Spain, the new headquarters he designed, MPK20, still looks understated compared with those of other tech giants, with exposed pipes and wiring everywhere. Of course, this time they did build a rooftop garden of a full 9 acres.

![The back of Facebook's sign board still bears the Sun Microsystems logo of the office's former tenant (photo / Business Insider)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704141140027KkeZEl0rzgdcJtT.jpg-w600)

"Of course, if we run into a client dead set on being strikingly different, my team and I still get nervous — especially when that's all they're after." Usually, at the project kickoff meeting, Eric asks clients to list five requirements for their corporate headquarters and then rank them in order. "In the end, the landmark usually gives way to function" — function and use efficiency rank at the top; no one wants the building to have too much redundant space, and the others are usually cost and time. "The 'iconic' sometimes shows up on the list too, but today almost no one puts it first."

Even though being a landmark is no longer the sole purpose, the recent wave of headquarters construction among global tech giants has still produced quite a few landmark buildings. Norman Foster designed a giant ring-shaped "spaceship" for Apple in Cupertino, and people marvel at the curved glass and the polished concrete roof panels — the design and construction craftsmanship of the ring rivals the products this company designs.

BIG and Thomas Heatherwick designed movable clusters of translucent glass tents for Google. [In an official blog post in 2015, Google explained](https://googleblog.blogspot.jp/2015/02/rethinking-office-space.html): "Instead of constructing an immovable block of concrete and steel, we will create lightweight, modular structures that can be moved around; inside the translucent tents the temperature and humidity will be controllable, and trees, landscaping, coffee bars, and bike paths will run through these structures." They were trying to break down the clear boundary between architecture and nature. In a similar vein, NBBJ's design for Amazon's Seattle headquarters simply built three glass domes, recreating nature inside giant greenhouses.

Stripping away the pure landmark impulse, what else lies behind this wave?

### What kind of office can bring creativity?

Eric has repeatedly mentioned an almost irreversible wave of conviction: global tech giants all believe that when knowledge workers — especially people who cross different departments and hold different bodies of knowledge — meet, inspiration is sparked. Based on this, architects hope to "manufacture" this kind of purposeless "chance encounter" to the greatest extent possible. They simulate street life and replicate urban functions, hoping to give the public spaces inside companies a sense of community.

Of course, there are detractors who question how one can measure whether this impulse to build "community" really has value — especially when no one can clearly say how these clients, the tech giants, define "community."

In the 1930s, in order to find a secluded place convenient for experiments, AT&T's Bell Telephone Laboratories established the first corporate campus in the New Jersey suburbs. Today, within Facebook's campus, besides a permanent medical team, fitness facilities, bike lanes, screening rooms, and dry-cleaning services, there is almost nothing you cannot get. The tech giants provide virtually every function a traditional community can offer within their campuses — the only difference being that everyone you come into contact with works for the same company. Chinese people who lived through the work-unit (danwei) system are no strangers to this kind of inward-looking community life.

Tencent's new headquarters in Shenzhen is located on Binhai Avenue in the Nanshan District. Unlike other tech companies that choose suburban sites, Tencent had no vast tracts of land and could only choose to go "upward." The two towers are connected by three sky bridges at different heights and are expected to be completed in July 2017. How to connect the interior spaces of the two supertall buildings became the focus of building a "vertical community" — people can move laterally via the three bridges and encounter each other there.

![Rendering of Tencent's new headquarters](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170414122730bHYe2fwgL4hnrEBi.jpg-w600)

For Amazon's Seattle headquarters, NBBJ designed three low-energy domed greenhouse "Spheres" built with tempered glass and low-energy electron diffraction technology. Their interiors will be planted with 3,000 species of plants from different regions of the world, and Amazon's employees will meet, exchange ideas, and have lunch inside the domes. The Sphere design also connects Amazon's office towers, and the overall project is expected to be completed in 2021.

Both Amazon and Tencent abandoned the model of choosing a remote suburb, replicating some urban functions, and rebuilding a mini-city. They stand in bustling downtown areas and extend street life into their office spaces. Step into the ground floor of Amazon's Seattle headquarters and you could be forgiven for thinking you've wandered into an ordinary mall — you can find a flower shop, a grocery store, and a café here.

![Despite its eye-catching exterior, the ground floor of Amazon's Seattle headquarters looks like an ordinary mall](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170414115637NhgcxXS2plOHzRrm.jpg-w600)

"There is a typical critique that everything we do is just to 'tie' employees to the company, and I cannot agree with it. Today, our definition of 'work' no longer has clear time boundaries — a whistle blows and work begins, another whistle blows and you can pack up and leave. Can creative workers still tell where work ends and life begins? We architects are the same. And what everyone is doing is providing the necessary facilities for this shift in the paradigm of work."

Eric emphasizes the seamless connection from street to office space (Seamless). "For everyone who works here, you cannot tell where street life ends and where work time begins. We can do a whole series of things with the blending of public and private space." Blurring this boundary is nothing new — just as people speculate that the entrepreneurs of yesteryear, out of a longing for pastoral life and the dream of building a utopia, threw themselves into corporate campuses in the far suburbs, this time people are placing their bets on the other end, demanding vitality and street revitalization — or simply simulating a city.

Even though designers rack their brains to create chance encounters within campuses, there are still invisible walls separating them from the city. After Amazon's eye-catching domes are completed, they will not open to the city's public for quite some time — this remains the multinational's private garden.

### What does it actually change when big companies build office towers?

People marvel at the spaceship form and the craftsmanship of Apple's new headquarters — a mini-city that can operate on its own, one might even say it is completely severed from Cupertino's existing urban fabric, the latter being just a traditional small West Coast town of red brick and flat tile roofs.

[In 2012, The New York Times quoted an Apple executive](http://www.nytimes.com/2012/01/22/business/apple-america-and-a-squeezed-middle-class.html): "The iPhone is sold in more than a hundred countries around the world; other than designing better products, we have no obligation to solve the problems of the United States as a country." Architecture critic Alexandra Lange points out that swapping "country" for "Cupertino" works just as well — these tech giants do not necessarily care about forging a connection with the cities they inhabit. Steve Jobs might have disagreed with this critique, though: when he applied to Cupertino's city managers for permission to build, he made sure to emphasize how heartfelt his attachment to the place was, promising to restore the large number of apricot trees Cupertino once had.

Of course, not all companies are content to build just an inward-looking mini-city. In fact, whether intentionally or not, this headquarters-building wave has already affected the urban landscape.

Samsung's headquarters in San Jose, California, also comes from NBBJ. Like any car-driven city, the area lacks walkable public space. NBBJ designed genuinely walkable passages inside the building, and the internal courtyards give people places to stop and rest. Unlike Samsung, a company long known for its rigid hierarchy, Eric stresses that this was a need Samsung explicitly expressed at the very start of the design — to create the public space this city lacks. A 10-story tower built like a sandwich structure: besides the central courtyard on the ground floor, there are walkways in the middle of the building. The sports field, art area, and coffee shop in the central courtyard will all be partially open to the public.

Companies like Google and Alibaba have also gradually shown an impulse to extend beyond the campus into urban space. In March 2011, Facebook sponsored a cross-disciplinary event outside its campus called Hack-A-Thon, inviting architects and urban planners to propose innovative schemes for the connections between its campus location and the surrounding areas, including the wetland landscapes. In a 2015 official blog post introducing its headquarters design, Google talked about adding bike lanes and retail opportunities for the local community. In November 2016, Google negotiated for the first time with the Mountain View city government, where its headquarters is located, about building residential housing near the campus. Alibaba, too, has begun studying how to provide resources for the education of employees' children.

Of course, the most direct impact of a big company moving into a neighborhood is usually rising housing prices. Silicon Valley is not only a testament to tech innovation; it is also the best illustration of how big companies change real estate prices. The story of how Twitter angered San Francisco back in the day tells us that a big company building a headquarters is not always a positive influence on its city — it is closer to a contradiction.

For a tech giant like Alibaba, what does the shift from blindly worshipping landmark buildings to building inward-looking communities mean — for them, and for the cities themselves?

At least one thing is certain: change will happen. The Chinese tech companies Eric works with have already started discussing with designers whether they could open up at least part of their once-sealed campuses and let the rest of the city flow in. He remains cautiously optimistic about where these discussions will lead: "You can feel that this kind of conversation has already begun."
