The Columbia Circle is today a baffling name found only on the walls along Shanghai's Xinhua Road. Yet this old mansion is its true origin.

No. 1262 West Yan'an Road is a thoroughly unremarkable compound entrance. On a pale metal plate, in gold regular script, it reads "Shanghai Institute of Biological Products Co., Ltd." The company's main business was vaccines; the BCG vaccine, the chickenpox vaccine, and the rabies vaccine were all once developed and produced here.

The compound is shaped like a bottle, with a small mouth and a broad belly, almost perfectly shielded from every kind of street-level prying except from high above. Few people know that behind the staid "front door" lies a 90-year-old expatriate history, and that this neighborhood once carried a code name now forgotten: the "Columbia Circle."

More than 90 years ago, this was a gathering place for American expatriates living in Shanghai's International Settlement. To this day it holds the only swimming pool in Shanghai that retains its original imperial measurements. Beside the pool stands a baroque building, perfectly preserved inside and out; carved into the fireplace is a shield-shaped logo — 13 stars above, and below them three nested letter C's: the initials of the Columbia Country Club, from which the name Columbia Circle derives.

Ninety years on, the Shanghai Institute of Biological Products (hereafter "SIBP") leased this land to Vanke, which brought in the Office for Metropolitan Architecture (OMA) to transform it into a new creative industry park. Old buildings given new life — will that reanimate this dormant history? Can modern living stir curiosity about the past? That was never the intent of the Vanke and OMA renovation, but it is a gateway into Shanghai's past.

### "Columbia Circle": A History of Expatriates

In 1924, there were no elevated highways here, no Shanghai Jiao Tong University, no Shanghai Film City; this patch of farmland on the edge of bustling metropolitan Shanghai didn't even have a street name.

While warlords battled each other in Jiangsu and Zhejiang, the foreigners of the International Settlement seized the chance to expand beyond the settlement's bounds, building roads across the farmland. Avenue Amherst (today's Xinhua Road) and Avenue Columbia (today's Panyu Road) were the products of the Municipal Council's road-building beyond the boundary.

Real estate development followed. The American Development Company bought up over a hundred mu of nearby farmland in succession and replotted the land. An advertisement for the "Columbia Circle" from those days, in warm tones, bore the Chinese name "Puyi Model Village." "Model" signified "modernization"; "village" sold an urban pastoral dream. Unlike the lilong lane houses common at the time, the expatriates looked forward to returning to the countryside — enjoying modern facilities and modern living in Western-style houses.

The Hungarian architect László Hudec was invited to design residences within the Columbia Circle, and the legacy he left to today's Xinhua Road Historic Conservation Area is 13 buildings in widely varying styles. The Sun Fo Villa in today's Columbia Park is one of Hudec's works; its former owner was Sun Fo, eldest son of Sun Yat-sen.

At this year's Shanghai Fashion Week, the Sun Fo Villa became a show venue. The building still retains its pointed-arch windows, thick round wooden doors, vaulted ceilings, and an enamel bathtub; the small door behind the bathtub wall was the opening left for bathtub repairs back in the day. Hudec originally designed the villa as his own private residence, but later, to thank Sun Fo for stepping in to resolve a construction problem he was having, he gave the villa — which he himself never spent a single day living in after it was completed — to Sun Fo. In 1949, Sun Fo sold the villa and moved his whole family to Hong Kong.

The gathering of expatriates gave rise to the Columbia Country Club. The ornate details inside the Columbia clubhouse are a faithful portrait of Shanghai's golden age of the 1920s and 1930s. The ground floor was the ballroom of the time, with eight spiral Corinthian columns whose capitals carry a pattern of tendrils and buds. Upstairs, several rooms still have their Plexiglas doorknobs. The baroque building was designed by Elliott Hazzard of the Hazzard firm.

The club, swimming pool, bowling alley, and broad lawns became the leisure center for expatriates of the International Settlement — until the Second World War shattered the glamour.

Hazzard himself became trapped in the Western-style house he had designed: in 1943 it was occupied by the Japanese army and renamed the "Fourth Internment Camp, West Shanghai." According to the research project "A Study of the Allied Expatriate Internment Camps in Shanghai" by the Shanghai Academy of Social Sciences and follow-up research by Professor Su Zhiliang of the Center for Urban Culture Research at Shanghai Normal University, between February 1942 and 1945 the Japanese army established a total of 20 internment camps in Shanghai. Hazzard was later transferred to the larger Longhua internment camp, where he eventually died.

![The “3C” logo on the fireplace](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170522194746tMVcq2FXemrfSaA0.jpg-w600)

After the founding of the People's Republic of China, the Columbia Country Club and the Sun Fo Villa were handed over to SIBP. Researchers worked out of the villa, and SIBP built a nine-story research building in the middle of what was once the great lawn; designed by Guo Bo, son of Guo Moruo, it split the huge internal courtyard in two.

Over SIBP's sixty years, the Columbia Country Club grew mysterious; few people entered from West Yan'an Road and passed down the long, narrow corridor to reach this enclave of 1920s–30s glamour. The pool was open to SIBP staff and their families; the arched gateposts remained, and at the shallow end of the pool mosaic tiles spelled out "Shallow End." The revolutionary years left few traces here — except for the ram's-head relief that once spouted water on the side of the pool, smashed during the "Destroy the Four Olds" campaign.

### An Old Building, Renovated at "China Speed"?

"But we didn't want to merely restore a few antique buildings." OMA, the firm behind Columbia Park's renovation, told QDaily — lead architect Chris van Duijn said they hoped to create space for entirely new functions here, rather than restore a few vacant heritage buildings open only for visits.

Urban renewal and historic building renovation have been global topics in recent years, and OMA — the firm behind the CCTV headquarters — has joined in. The Prada Foundation is one of its signature works of recent years: inside a former distillery in Milan they added an exhibition hall, a tower, and a theater. They also converted a restaurant abandoned for nearly thirty years in Moscow's Gorky Park into the Garage Museum of Contemporary Art.

The oldest "heritage object" OMA has taken on came from Venice — the Fondaco dei Tedeschi, first built in the 13th century and rebuilt in the 16th; OMA inserted vertical circulation inside, and the trading post that had witnessed Venice's rise and fall became a department store.

Renovating old buildings has become a trend, but not every renovation is bound to succeed. Ippolito Pestellini Laparelli, the lead architect of the Fondaco dei Tedeschi, once [wrote](http://archiposition.com/information/projects/item/856-oma-renovation-the-possibility-of-abstinence.html) a critique of this "nostalgia wave," which he argued had become a form of opportunism. Developers and architects unthinkingly restore the building itself and rest content with that, without knowing "how to keep the past alive." Like shoddy faux-antique construction, these "genuinely" old buildings are hollow shells that cannot take on their new functions. Designs driven by political correctness rather than genuine humanistic care, Ippolito said, are nothing but consumption.

What should old buildings be used for today, and how are those functions achieved? [We have reported several times on AMO, OMA's think tank](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/11116.html) — this "squad" with the reversed name studies the kinds of businesses that can be implanted in a space; they designed the internal fashion show venue for the Prada Foundation.

![A Prada runway designed by AMO (Photo / OMA's official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170522182851ECSWFOfetkUuqwjd.jpg-w600)

Chris said another ongoing AMO project in recent years concerns the countryside against the backdrop of urbanization. "In those overseas projects, our role was never simply that of designer — we went beyond providing technical solutions; more often than not, we joined the developers' ranks."

But in the Columbia Park project, the room left for OMA's exploration was extremely limited. The renovation focused on hard space: for instance, they added connecting bridges built of new materials inside the old buildings, enriching the originally rigid floor circulation to better suit the needs of creative offices.

OMA also made a point of opening up sight lines, "stuffing" a glass box into the ground floor of the research building that splits the courtyard in two. The north courtyard is the more public one, ringed by a Michelin restaurant set to open soon, cafés, the swimming pool, and co-working space. The south courtyard is comparatively private, belonging to the workers in the office buildings. Visually, the glass connects the two courtyards.

Chris still remembers their first meeting with Vanke. Vanke cited the Prada Foundation as a reference for the Columbia Park renovation. "We're honored everyone likes that project — we like it too — but the problem is, these are two completely different cases." Beyond the difference in functional positioning, the other word Chris reached for was "context."

![Prada Foundation (Photo / OMA's official website)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705221936086mKkJ3LyXpfxj48i.jpg-w600)

The first thing to adapt to was "China speed." Research for the Prada Foundation project began in 2007, but as late as last year they were still designing the last building within that project's scope. "We spent three months imagining an entrance — maybe you could call that a misfortune — but such long design cycles are common in Europe. For the Garage in Moscow, we also worked five years."

But the Chinese urban renewal projects OMA faces have design cycles of only two or three months. "We discuss a proposal in a meeting today, and the revised proposal has to come out tomorrow." Chris stressed that beneath the surface of China speed and European speed lies land tenure: unlike European clients, developers like Vanke are not the owners of the land — what they win by bidding is a 10-to-20-year land lease, and turning a profit within a limited time is the most practical question.

Vanke obtained a 20-year lease for Columbia Park, while Red Town, another creative park in Changning District, has just seen its ten-year lease expire and will wrap up this year.

Another challenge lies in constant change. Unlike the renovation of a single building, Columbia Park is positioned as a mixed-use compound centered on offices, combining lifestyle, exhibition, and public space. Vanke is ambitious for this prime Shanghai site, imagining every possible kind of business and function for it.

"One building — at first it was going to be a restaurant, then for a while it was changed to a bookstore, then they wanted office space, and now it's back to a restaurant," Chris said. Unclear functions bring design uncertainty.

"It's more like an improvisational live-action painting," Chris explained. "This isn't to say Europe's relatively longer design cycles are better — there's no point arguing over who's good and who's bad. What matters is that this is the real context we have to face."

### Adidas, Sound Art, City Cycling… What Else Will Happen Here?

Compared with the constrained designers, Vanke is busy.

The building renovation has only just begun, but No. 1262 West Yan'an Road, its gates shut tight for more than sixty years, has suddenly begun warmly hosting events in one old building after another.

Last year Adidas's product launch happened on a "blue night": the whole compound was bathed in blue light, and a giant logo "floated" on the surface of the pool. Artist Zhang Ding's solo exhibition under his "Control Club" — "A Giant Sound-Field Installation of Sixteen Channels, Ten Frequency Bands, and Fifty-Nine Loudspeakers" — was held in the annex building, whose open 10-meter-high space had once been used by SIBP as a production workshop.

![Adidas product launch](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170522193856LXWSfxoJO3C0cbkT.jpg-w600)

![Adidas product launch](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170522184526yixj5VmwIo1nJvlT.jpg-w600)

Early cultural-creative parks all seemed to share a formula: on one end of the formula, old buildings, big factory floors, artists, and relatively cheap rent; on the other end, pilgrims, tourists, rent rising with the tide, and land prices promising a bright future. But many people long ago grew sick of the round fans printed with qipao-clad women in Tianzifang, the dense snack stalls, and the jostling crowds of tourists.

That formula has expired, and "second landlords" like Vanke are busy with operations.

Who is the ideal tenant? "I'm afraid no one remembers that when Xintiandi opened, there was a McDonald's there," the person in charge of the Columbia Park project said, citing Xintiandi as an example — operators need to control the mix of businesses and their proportions within the park. "McDonald's, which entered the Chinese market back in the nineties, has been a stable tenant to this day; if I remember correctly, they signed a long-term lease with Xintiandi at the time. But five years later they realized something was off, and the operator voluntarily paid compensation and asked the McDonald's — which didn't fit the park's positioning — to leave."

"Have you ever visited the compound where IBM is based? Standing outside IBM's gate you can almost feel an 'invisible wall'; its corporate culture is too powerful. What services could we possibly offer it? It doesn't even need us for intern recruiting." For Vanke, which in recent years has stressed its transformation into an "urban supporting-services provider," such companies don't need the park operator to solve their needs, and thus cannot create "invisible value." "They have a strong ability to pay rent, which is great, but they're not the most perfect tenants — just very good anchors."

Naked Hub, which operates co-working space as a brand, will open yet another location in Columbia Park, and Vanke hopes it can be a "catfish" that breaks down the formerly rigid barriers between different companies in the same park.

The warm-up events also included a cycling ride. Last December, Vanke joined forces with bicycle brand WKUP and architecture new-media outlet AssBook to hold "Ride Awake," inviting architects and urban planners to design several cycling routes weaving between historic conservation areas — Yongjia Road, Jianguo Road, Huashan Road. Hudec has plainly become a recent "internet celebrity"; every kind of event likes to ride on his fame. Cyclists visited old buildings designed by Hudec along the way, stopping to take photos and studying the scale of the conservation areas and the cycling infrastructure. Columbia Park was the ride's terminus, where participants exchanged ideas after the ride.

"Why must cycling circle Taihu or run along the Huangpu River? It can also be in the middle of the city, centered on exploring architecture and tied into the city's historical fabric," the Columbia Park team said, having organized several editions of the "Gua Appreciation of Old Buildings" event.

![Sun Fo Villa with its vaulted ceilings](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170522195211b4gfW9S2oPdKVLk5.jpg-w600)

Early creative parks were like isolated islands, unconnected to their surroundings. Maybe you still occasionally run over to M50 to see an exhibition, but most people can't name what's around M50. "Linkage" is a high-frequency word in Vanke's vocabulary — the Shanghai International Film Festival, for instance. This place is only about a kilometer from the Shanghai Film City, an easy walk.

"We often hear complaints: every year during the film festival, when people come out of the Shanghai Film City cinemas, they can only take the subway somewhere else — it's as if there's no suitable, concentrated spot around the cinema for people to stop and talk," the project's head said. Columbia Park can provide space for filmmakers' press conferences and salons, but they are also aware of a potential "competitor": the renovation plans for the Shanghai Film City and its surroundings remain unclear.

![At one end of the pool's shallow area, mosaic tiles spell out “Shallow End“](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170522200812d1ClQFRb0HqDWXEm.jpg-w600)

The fashion industry is another target for the "linkage" Vanke hopes for. Spaces like the swimming pool and the Sun Fo Villa are well suited as runways and exhibition venues. "Beyond the display function at the very end of the fashion industry chain, we also hope to link with universities like Donghua University and Shanghai University of Engineering Science — to move upstream in the industry chain, reach designers, hold forums here, and share ideas."

"Of course, from the front end to the back end, the rent-paying ability of each node in an industry chain is also something that has to be considered."

"We hope to stick some 'labels' on Columbia — like how the mention of theater makes people think of the Wuzhen Theatre Festival." Those labels include "fashion," "film," and "historic conservation area." A recent post on Columbia Park's official account was about David Beckham coming here to shoot a fashion spread. Behind these somewhat scattered labels, the person in charge said, "we're still testing, to see what ultimately sticks."
