His contribution to the world amounted to far more than the "little black dress" — he also carried forward and popularized elegance, though his successors did not.

French designer Hubert de Givenchy died on March 10 local time at the age of 91. On March 12, a company representative announced the news, but revealed no further details.

Even if you have never seen *Breakfast at Tiffany's*, you remember at least one scene: the gold digger Holly Golightly, played by Audrey Hepburn, standing on New York's Fifth Avenue in the early morning — dark sunglasses, long gloves, a pearl necklace wound four times around her neck — munching a baguette as she gazes into a jeweler's shop window. The Givenchy little black dress she wears makes her look both poised and out of place, as if to say: she yearns for glamour, but has not yet been admitted to it.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313161342pe40XVFwqbnzAktj.jpg-w600)

Hubert de Givenchy, founder of Givenchy, was the creator of this classic Hollywood image. In 2006, the little black dress sold at the London auction house Christie's for US$920,000. Beyond Audrey Hepburn, the fashions he designed also appeared alongside many well-known women at important historical moments: Jacqueline Kennedy on her first visit to France with Kennedy, and the Duchess of Windsor at the funeral of Edward VIII.

But these anecdotes cannot simply explain Hubert de Givenchy's significance to the fashion industry. From founding Givenchy in 1952 and showing his first haute couture womenswear collection, to selling the brand to LVMH in 1988, to formally stepping down as creative director in 1995, Hubert de Givenchy's 43-year career reads like a microcosm of fashion history, witnessing the golden age of the postwar Paris couture houses, the democratization of luxury in America in the 1960s, and the opportunities and risks that corporate capitalization brought to a brand from the 1990s onward.

He also broke many of the industry's conventions: the first to introduce separates for women, the first to launch a ready-to-wear line, the first to use a Hollywood star as a brand ambassador.

Bernard Arnault, chairman and CEO of LVMH Group, said the fashions Hubert de Givenchy created "combined two rare qualities: innovation and elegance."

In an obituary published on March 12, *The New York Times* described him as "representing the generation of gentleman designers who built fashion houses from scratch in postwar Paris, who knew how to forge personal relationships with their clients and how to create entire collections around a specific image of a woman in their minds."

### Aristocratic Blood and Postwar Paris

"If possible, you had better be born elegant. Elegance must be part of you, or it must be you yourself," Hubert de Givenchy said, standing at the podium**.** This was a lecture at Oxford University in 2010, and the only advice Givenchy gave the young people in the audience who aspired to become designers.

For Hubert de Givenchy, this took no effort at all. Born on February 21, 1927, in Beauvais, capital of France's Oise department, his full name was "Count Hubert James Marcel Taffin de Givenchy." His father Lucien was a hereditary marquis, and his mother, Béatrice Badin, came from a family of tapestry ateliers.

His father died of influenza when Givenchy was three, and Givenchy and his older brother were raised by their mother's family, whose influence on him ran deeper. At ten, after seeing designs by Chanel, Elsa Schiaparelli and others at a presentation organized by Jeanne Lanvin in Paris, Givenchy settled on his ideal profession. At seventeen he entered the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris to study fashion design; a year later, through family connections, he became an apprentice in designer Jacques Fath's studio, and over a six-year apprenticeship he also trained under Robert Piguet, Lucien Lelong, and Elsa Schiaparelli.

![A young Hubert de Givenchy](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313161936ainO2YJqHPxFIswh.jpg-w600)

The years from 1945 to 1952 were precisely a period in which the Paris fashion industry revived from utter ruin. Dana Thomas records in her book *Deluxe* that in the 1920s the French luxury industry employed 300,000 workers, including cutters, fitters, seamstresses, embroiderers, furriers, shoemakers, weavers, spinners, and milliners. In 1940, Nazi Germany invaded Paris, looted the headquarters of the couture association, seized all its documents, closed the shops of Madame Grès and Balenciaga, and "made 14 attempts to destroy the French luxury industry, with the aim of moving the couture houses to Berlin and Vienna, then the new cultural centers of Europe."

This raid dealt Paris a heavy blow. Raw materials were scarce and workers had gone missing; Lucien Lelong, then president of the French couture association, had to persuade designers to stay in business: "You can force us to do anything, but the Paris couture houses will never relocate, no matter what happens. Haute couture that is not in Paris is no longer haute couture." To survive, houses like Louis Vuitton had no choice but to sell their goods to Nazi officers.

What truly restored the luxury industry was a few new names. Pierre Balmain, Christian Dior, and Jacques Fath were known collectively as the three great designers who created the new postwar haute couture trend; in general their designs returned to feminine charm, but simplified — among them, Dior's New Look, which emphasized the female waistline, became the most famous.

The young Givenchy had ties with the latter two. In 1946, when Givenchy was working under Lucien Lelong, Christian Dior had just left to found his eponymous brand. Jacques Fath's influence ran deeper — he was Givenchy's first boss in Paris, and later created Givenchy's first muse, Bettina Graziani.

### Two Muses

Whether in fashion design or brand promotion, Givenchy's success owed much to two muses. If Hepburn helped him establish a classic image and expand his influence in America, Bettina Graziani was the one who opened the American market for him.

Bettina Graziani was born Simone Micheline Bodin, two years older than Givenchy, and came from a poor family; like Givenchy, she arrived in Paris in 1944. She showed her design sketches to designer Jacques Costet, hoping to be hired as a designer, but he felt she was better suited to modeling.

That proved to be true. Bettina Graziani became one of the favorite models of Paris's three great postwar couturiers — Balmain, Jacques Fath, and Christian Dior — and was "the most photographed woman in France." Among them, it was Jacques Fath who gave her the name Bettina Graziani and cut her red hair short, which became her most iconic look thereafter.

"Back then I wore no makeup, had red hair, and was young and lively — not exactly pretty, but I looked different from other models. Fath wanted to project an image with an American spirit, a brand-new, modern attitude... He said to me, 'We already have a Simone; you look more like a Bettina'... and then I became Fath's spokeswoman," Bettina recalled in a 1980 interview with *Interview Magazine*.

One journalist even wrote in 1947, "Dior may have the New Look, but Fath has Bettina Graziani." Until Jacques Fath died of leukemia in 1954, his fashion house ranked second only to Dior in sales.

![Jacques Fath and Bettina Graziani in the studio](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313162214nog8ABYbGhxSLfuI.png-w600)

In 1950, Graziani signed with the American modeling agency Eileen Ford and came to the United States to develop her career. Within a week of arriving in New York, she received an exclusive modeling offer from *Vogue*. She was also among the first French models to break into the American market successfully; she could do showroom modeling as well as photograph beautifully, at a time when the "cover girl" was still a new concept in France.

Former *Vogue* fashion editor Bettina Ballard wrote in her 1960 autobiography *In My Fashion*: "Photographers liked to book her, because she knew how to listen, never lost her temper, never made things difficult, and had a gift for striking poses that were distinctive but not strange." *Life Magazine* ran a feature in its July 24, 1950 issue titled "French Models Thrive in US," in which Bettina Graziani was one of the two models photographed; the piece said they had "quickly become the most sought-after top models in New York."

In March 1952, the 25-year-old Givenchy left Elsa Schiaparelli and founded his eponymous brand. When he showed his first collection in a small showroom on Rue Alfred de Vigny in Paris's 8th arrondissement, he named the collection directly after Bettina, persuading Bettina Graziani to serve not only as a model but also as head of PR.

Perhaps it was also thanks to Bettina that *Life* magazine in faraway America covered the show across four pages, under the headline "De Givenchy, a New Name in Paris." Describing the chaotic scene at the presentation, *Life* wrote: "She (Bettina) rounded up the most important American fashion editors, posed for the photographers, arranged chairs for the guests, tidied the clothes backstage, walked the runway, and afterward came out to sell the clothes."

The show was a huge success. Every garment sold out at the show, and one of them, a blouse named Bettina, became Givenchy's first signature piece: a light, airy blouse with loose cuffs trimmed in ruffles, embroidered with black motifs. During the shoot, Bettina raised both arms with her hands placed at either side of her head, letting the ruffles spread out, perfectly displaying the blouse's romantic character. She explained: "A model is not a passive role; she can inspire the designer through her gestures, movements, or the way she stands — sometimes it's just an adjustment of a detail, and sometimes she can create an entirely new garment."

Compared with Bettina, the story of Audrey Hepburn and Givenchy is probably more familiar to people.

In the 1950s, relations between Paris's haute couture world and Hollywood were frosty. Biographer Marie-France Pochna wrote in *Christian Dior: The Man Who Made the World Look New* that "Dior would never let his clothes be displayed vulgarly on the big screen, which would offend his most elegant clients — he would never take that risk... He believed that, aesthetically, a living aristocrat was far superior to a clumsy imitation on stage or screen."

Givenchy did not see it that way. In 1953, the year after his first collection, Paramount Pictures contacted Givenchy, asking him to select several outfits for Hepburn's new film *Sabrina*. At the time, *Roman Holiday*, starring Audrey Hepburn, had not yet been released, and Givenchy assumed he would be working with Katharine Hepburn, so he gladly agreed. After meeting her, he was visibly disappointed — "I had no time to attend to her; I was designing my second collection" — and let Hepburn pick for herself. But after one dinner, Givenchy changed his view of the newcomer: "Before dinner was even over, I said to her, I would do anything for you."

His positive attitude toward brand promotion may also have been influenced by his first boss, Jacques Fath. If Dior and Balenciaga emphasized structural beauty like architects, Jacques Fath was known for flamboyance and vitality. He himself loved socializing and often organized all kinds of high-society parties, letting his wife wear his designs as she moved through them. He had a famous line: "The foundation of haute couture is publicity. If you don't realize that, you cannot understand how haute couture works."

After meeting Hepburn, the pair's first classic look was born — the "décolleté Sabrina," a black evening gown with a square neckline that covered the collarbone. Until her death, Edith Head, the costume designer of *Sabrina*, insisted it was her own design. According to *The New York Times*, Hepburn was angered by this and therefore required that all her future films be dressed by Givenchy. She also shot advertisements for free for L'Interdit, the perfume Givenchy later launched, and allowed Givenchy to use her name — the first time a luxury brand had ever used a film star's face to promote a fragrance.

![Hepburn wearing Givenchy's "décolleté Sabrina" in Sabrina](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313163158Q9lNjRUcCw4oY302.jpg-w600)

![Hepburn in a 1975 Givenchy perfume advertisement](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313163258M8QelpfY2BAdiECr.jpg-w600)

With the spread of television in the 1950s, the name Givenchy became known to a wider American public. The civil rights movement and social changes of the 1960s also broke down the barriers to luxury consumption; as described in *Deluxe*, "the middle class strove to emulate the rich, including patronizing the top establishments the rich frequented, without fear of being warned off or ridiculed."

Holly — the gold digger who, in the early morning, eats bread on New York's Fifth Avenue in a Givenchy little black dress and admires Tiffany's jewels through the window — is the epitome of this 1960s middle-class mindset. It is fair to say that Hubert de Givenchy, together with Hepburn, created an image that captured the zeitgeist.

And it would take his peers another 30 years, until Hollywood's golden age, to realize that they needed Hollywood.

### Two Transformations

That the fashion house Givenchy was able to keep operating for 36 years, while remaining financially independent, before it was sold to LVMH certainly could not be due to marketing alone. Everything ultimately came down to design.

During those 36 years, Givenchy's designs brought two transformations to the fashion industry.

The postwar business environment in Paris was hostile to the European haute couture industry. In an age of austerity, fine fabrics were expensive, and people regarded overly extravagant design with suspicion, even outrage. The controversy sparked by Christian Dior's New Look in 1947 was a typical example. Designers Edward Molyneux and Robert Piguet went out of business as a result.

When Givenchy designed his first collection in 1952, he had already taken this into account. For the first time he presented several outfits of separates — before that, haute couture had only been one-piece. These included a white knit top with coral-red buttons paired with a black skirt, an organza blouse with a long black skirt, and a black embroidered sweater with a black striped pencil skirt. Three tops plus three bottoms could create nine looks. His fabric choices were practical too, priced at only two-thirds of his competitors'.

![Three separates outfits](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313163524xXuVJNMIqFK9YshP.jpg-w600)

The 1950s American market differed from Europe's: casual ready-to-wear was in vogue. Retailers would typically buy licenses from European designers, then mass-produce copies of their designs on assembly lines. Givenchy noticed this too, selling the rights to his first signature piece, the "Bettina" blouse mentioned above, to the New York retailer Russeks. The copied blouse sold for $10.95, while the original cost $2,800. This business decision further expanded Givenchy's influence in the American market.

In the 1960s, the youth culture movement began rising around the world, and Givenchy proved quite sensitive to this change.

In 1957, he and his idol Cristóbal Balenciaga jointly developed a new silhouette called the "Sack," [and launched it simultaneously in their respective new collections](https://waldina.com/2018/02/21/happy-91st-birthday-hubert-de-givenchy/). Derived from a kind of 1950s dress (the shirt dress), this shape hung loose like a sack, showing no waistline, but its hem was shortened to below the knee and tapered; it still wore elegantly, but was much lighter.

![The "Sack" design: the traditional shirt dress silhouette on the left, Givenchy's 1957 version on the right](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313163552QWys0rJD3mIlea7u.jpg-w600)

![Hepburn wearing Givenchy in Vogue, 1960](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313163705JesIbQdCM6l0Amon.jpeg-w600)

After that, all of Givenchy's designs also shortened their hemlines and placed less emphasis on the female form. In 1968, seeing the potential of the ready-to-wear market, Givenchy opened a ready-to-wear boutique, and in the 1970s successively launched menswear and childrenswear, becoming one of the few designers to win acclaim in both haute couture and ready-to-wear.

"He made traditional couture relevant to modern life," [a 1971 report in The New York Times](http://www.apple.com.cn/) put it.

### Givenchy's Tumultuous Fate After the Sale to LVMH

In 1988, after 36 years of independent operation, Givenchy sold the Givenchy Couture Group to LVMH for $45 million, then continued to serve as creative director for another seven years, until his departure in 1995. After that, the Givenchy brand fell into a decade of chaos.

Over those ten years, three strikingly different British designers, each with a strong personal signature, took the helm at Givenchy. The first to be appointed was John Galliano. When the news was announced after Givenchy's own final show in 1995, everyone was shocked — his flamboyant, theatrical style obviously did not fit Givenchy's trademark poised elegance, and he was only the second British designer in history, after Charles-Frederick Worth, to head a French fashion house.

John Galliano left after less than a year in the job; his successor was another British designer famed for his eccentric genius, Alexander McQueen.

McQueen was only 27 when he took the job, signing a four-year contract at an annual salary of £1 million. But his bizarre style failed to reinvent Givenchy, and at the end of 2000, by selling a 51% stake in his eponymous brand to Gucci — owned by Kering, LVMH Group's arch-rival — he angered his employer. McQueen's final collection for Givenchy had no runway show; it was replaced by two low-key presentations, to which fewer than 90 guests were invited.

![Alexander McQueen's Givenchy debut, 1997](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313164025I67Nv30UkLE2cBzP.jpg-w600)

The third British designer, Julien MacDonald, also lasted only two years. He was just 28 when appointed, and public opinion still could not understand why this designer — dubbed "the Welsh Donatella Versace," who liked to work sequins and miniskirts into his designs — had been hired to run Givenchy, a house whose image was embodied by Hepburn's little black dress.

Other potential candidates had seemed far more suitable, including Stella McCartney, who would later take the helm at Chloé, Alber Elbaz, who had just left Yves Saint Laurent, and Nicolas Ghesquière of Balenciaga (now women's creative director at Louis Vuitton).

MacDonald's final show for Givenchy was even more dismal — no media were invited, and the cramped venue held only 80 guests.

It was not until Riccardo Tisci took over in 2005 that Givenchy enjoyed its second golden age. His secret was to abandon opulence and add street-style prints.

In the fall/winter 2012 collection, one could find dark gothic looks inspired by the Jurassic era; and at the fall/winter 2015 show, the models' styling resembled British singer FKA Twigs (real name Tahliah Barnett), known for her individuality — perhaps this was Riccardo Tisci's new definition of cool for a new era: compared with Holly, the gold digger daydreaming about American middle-class life in *Breakfast at Tiffany's*, FKA Twigs better fits today's climate of attention to diverse values and the freedom to choose.

Another highlight Riccardo Tisci brought was menswear. Since he began overseeing the Givenchy menswear line in 2008, elements still uncommon in luxury goods — basketball, the American flag, street graffiti — attracted more attention. The Rottweiler T-shirt launched in fall/winter 2012 finally gave Givenchy a new hit.

In leather goods, he also created new handbag classics that caught on slowly but endured, including the Nightingale, Pandora, and Antigona.

Riccardo Tisci has now gone to Burberry as creative director, and the mark he left on Givenchy is indelible — at least in the minds of younger consumers, Givenchy is a luxury brand with a street-style edge. Elegance? Not so present anymore.

### Givenchy the Brand, and Givenchy the Aristocrat

According to WWD, between 2005 and 2017 Givenchy's total market value grew sixfold, headcount rose from 290 to 930, and revenue reached $540 million. This also allowed the brand to reduce its dependence on suppliers: directly operated stores grew from an initial seven to 72, and most recently it successfully bought back its direct-operation rights in two important luxury consumer markets, Dubai and Singapore.

What has the greatest presence in the public mind today is beauty — according to BoF, the brand's annual sales hover around $400 million, of which the beauty line alone reaches $350 million.

After years of trials, Givenchy's biggest problem remains a lack of stylistic continuity and an unclear market positioning.

Mario Ortelli, a senior analyst at Sanford C. Bernstein, told BoF: "Today, when people think of luxury, few think of Givenchy first. Fashionable people follow Gucci, Balenciaga, and Dior, while traditional customers remain loyal to Louis Vuitton and Fendi."

In an interview with BoF, Givenchy's current CEO Philippe Fortunato said he plans to bring the design style back to the elegance of the brand's founding era and to focus on leather goods — the GV3, priced at $790 to $1,790, is about to hit the shelves, set to compete with Gucci's Dionysus and Saint Laurent's classic handbag, the Sac de Jour.

The person entrusted with this task by the CEO is Clare Waight Kellers. Her first collection received a lukewarm response, but her second collection, shown during Paris Fashion Week this year, drew wide attention for its use of faux fur and its reimagining of the elegance of the Givenchy era, making it the most-discussed luxury brand on Instagram and Twitter.

![Givenchy fall/winter 2018](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180313172128n7LSAR3Qthf15kpu.jpg-w600)

As for Hubert de Givenchy, after stepping down in 1995 he no longer commented much on his own brand, mentioning it only once, in a 2007 interview with WWD: "I am distressed; everything that happened is rather unpleasant. After all, everyone wants to be proud of their name."

For most of the time, he took on an identity that had little to do with fashion — antiquities expert for the auction house Christie's and the Louvre.

But [New York Times reporter Eric Willson believes](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/03/12/obituaries/hubert-de-givenchy-dies.html?mtrref=undefined&auth=login-email) that people's impression of the designer will always be frozen in a single image.

"Two meters tall, with a mop of fair hair, so impeccably polite as to be unsettling, robust of build and handsome of face — the very image of the French aristocracy."
