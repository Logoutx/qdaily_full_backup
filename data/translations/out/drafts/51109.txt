What the organizer kept asking was, "Isn't there one thing that could get everyone talking to each other?"

The 2018 edition of Singapore Design Week struck a different pose from its previous iterations.

The citywide design celebration, organized by the DesignSingapore Council, was marking its fifth year. Until 2025, Singapore Design Week would carry a lofty-sounding goal: let design drive Singapore's economic innovation and make the city more lovable.

Under that goal, this edition added quite a few things beyond its traditional core events, SingaPlural and the International Furniture Fair. For the first time, it launched Brainstorm Design, a three-day forum inviting creative figures from every field around the world to speak; there were community-building activities in Holland Village, as well as an urban walking design tour co-created with the National Heritage Board. On top of that, this year's event drew more participants and a more diverse range of content. More than 90 organizations and individuals across the city held events in conjunction with Design Week, and the Italian embassy even held an Open House on March 5–6.

For all its buzz, though, Singapore Design Week still left "Singapore design" a fuzzy concept for many people. This country, independent for only a little over 50 years, has no long-standing cultural tradition and has failed to develop a distinctive style that draws admirers.

Agens Kwek, the DesignSingapore Council's current executive director, likens Singapore design to "a unique blend mixing creativity, business and public policy." Asked how Singapore could achieve design-driven growth, her answer was, "I hope to see everyone equipped with basic design literacy."

At "District & Design Dialogue — Holland Village" (DDDHV), the largest community event of this year's Design Week, this area — originally tucked behind the Singapore Botanic Gardens and surrounded by university campuses — became a miniature of Singapore's ambition to truly weave design into the city.

Over the ten-day Holland Village mega pop-up, what you saw was not just designers and artists but, even more, the participation of ordinary residents. On one street in Chip Bee Garden, ten idle shophouse homes were converted into pop-up stores with a rich mix of content: food, exhibitions, streetwear labels, art, live music and workshops. In the free, vibrant atmosphere, strangers chatted warmly over ice cream cones, as if art, creativity, fashion and design had always been part of everyday life.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231039jiH9esxQl63tmanY.jpeg-w600)

Mention Holland Village to many Singaporeans and what first comes to mind is the excellent multinational cuisine, the raucous bars, and shops selling all manner of finely made knickknacks.

As its name suggests, the Dutch were the first to come here and establish a community. In fact, however, the name Holland Village honors one of the area's early residents — Hugh Holland, a locally respected architect.

But the neighborhood's existing architectural style was not set by the Dutch. In the 1950s, the British military built the area's signature black-and-white semi-detached terrace houses in Holland Village. Gradually, Holland Village began to attract more expatriates coming to Singapore, most of them from the upper classes. Over the past thirty years, the area became known as an upscale residential district, and all kinds of businesses flourished to cater to the well-off. In its shops you could find expensive imported gourmet products and international school uniforms.

People who live in Holland Village say that without diversity, the place is nothing. As Western culture poured in, Holland Village became the cradle of Singapore's trendy lifestyle. Its first batch of stylish bars and cafés went up here, and a new wave of upscale vegetarian restaurants rose in the 1990s.

Later on, the story of Holland Village became the same as that of every community that loses its way amid crowded modernization. The Holland Village MRT station opened, the "Bohemian Street" reputation grew louder, the windmills that once stood among the village houses were replaced by shopping malls, and wave after wave of tourists arrived.

Like an enormous magnet, Holland Village as a tourist destination grew ever busier. But the attraction among the people within the community weakened; the familiarity that had long held the whole community together quietly dissolved, and the distance between its more than 300 households kept widening.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315230853B1UDiRaWV5K8muJo.jpg-w600)

![Holland Village Neighborhood](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315230923kHdzfRUo6lD5gacB.jpg-w600)

Justin Long, who has lived in Holland Village for 12 years, found this puzzling. He watched his two-year-old son happily playing with the neighbor kids all the time, while he himself had little contact with his neighbors. It seemed adults were always held back by unfamiliarity, while children had a magical power to forge friendships instantly.

He wondered, "Isn't there one thing that could get everyone talking to each other?"

Last October, the moment Justin saw the DesignSingapore Council's open call for community design events, the idea struck him. The call, meant to accompany the following year's Design Week, had the Council acting as a supportive platform, openly recruiting communities, designers or organizations from all over Singapore, with no fixed theme or format required.

Justin decided to use Design Week as the occasion to hold an event in Holland Village — bringing design into daily life and creating opportunities for neighbors to connect.

The "District & Design Dialogue — Holland Village," curated by Justin Long, opened early on March 9. That day, each of the ten houses lining both sides of the street was painted a different color, with a specially designed little pennant hung at the door to welcome visitors. The designers who took over the rooms carefully styled the small front courtyards, giving each one its own personality.

![Say Hi To_House](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231100qe32mZBygN1hpsCI.jpeg-w600)

The first house visitors enter in the event zone is called Say Hi To_House. It was put together by Say Hi to_, a creative agency from France that runs an online magazine of the same name. Curator Kristen de la Vallière arranged the house as a small gallery, showing the work of ten designers — including the black steel-tube geometric furniture of Dazing Feels Good (DFG), which had appeared at last year's SingaPlural, and rugs by Singapore designer Olivia Lee that had previously been shown at Milan Design Week, among others.

In a second-floor room of Say Hi To_House, Joanna Chen, founder of the travel clothing-rental company Gibbon, was enthusiastically introducing the company's business to visitors. By signing contracts with clothing companies at travel destinations, it provides travelers with local clothing rental. Gibbon aims to relieve the burden of luggage and thereby make travel simpler. Its rental service has now expanded to Amsterdam, where it costs 22 euros a day, with a maximum charge of three days. Gibbon mails a full set of clothes to an address booked in advance, and the customer simply packs the clothes into the bag Gibbon provides before leaving; someone will come to collect it.

For this trip to Holland Village Design Week, Joanna Chen devised some playful "notices" — an airport discovers a crowd of naked people, and it turns out they all used Gibbon. Through displays like this, Joanna hopes to gather more people's ideas: how do you want to dress?

![Furniture design on the first floor](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231243wvPa8IDocKnjAsb6.jpeg-w600)

![Gibbon's "notices" on the second floor](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231243aV5EsT6DSrZhF9LA.jpeg-w600)

The second house held an exhibition made just for children. It was curated by the children's magazine *eyeyah*, founded by Steve Lawler and Tanya Wilson, who moved to Singapore more than ten years ago. The magazine collaborates with numerous artists, aiming to teach kids about real-world issues — the harms of phone addiction, environmental and social problems — through lively, charming artistic illustrations.

At the exhibition, *eyeyah* showed works by more than 30 artists, framed to decorate the house and also printed onto children's T-shirts, hung row upon row.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231415DUxvL17Zo3q0RgfG.png-w600)

![House of Eyeyah](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231500mINnpX7A8VfjD19R.png-w600)

Sole Superior, a homegrown street-culture event now in its fifth edition, opened its first-ever pop-up store at Holland Village Design Week. In a house named Sole Superior's Party stood sneakers, skateboards, baseball caps, and special collaboration pieces Sole Superior had made with brands such as XLarge, FIN and Novesta over the past five years.

![Sole Superior's Party](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231551xI9DSH2TmpLZoMPl.jpeg-w600)

Another house, called Runway, became a showcase platform for Singapore's emerging designer brands. It gathered the womenswear labels Waiyang and Ametsubi, the menswear label biro, and the eco-focused accessory brands Heads of State Millinery and Just Gaya. During Design Week, these brands also held more interactive workshops; Singapore up-and-coming illustrator MessyMsxi, for instance, ran two sessions on painting plants and natural landscapes with water-based paints.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231719tEXH6n1Nl0Gw5Msj.jpeg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231623kSUAbXJo5eYHjncT.jpeg-w600)

![The Runway](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803152316321F3uY79APsIHjnQG.jpeg-w600)

After the streetwear and designer fashion, The Lounge wanted to show "how to have a better life." It brought in lifestyle brands created by Singapore designers — for example, The Gentle Label, a British home-care brand actually founded by a Singapore designer, and Candles of Light, a botanical fragrance brand. The Fragment Room on the second floor, meanwhile, was designed to let people release stress to their heart's content: at the entrance sat a crate of glass bottles and clay pots; pay one Singapore dollar, pick one out, and take it into the wooden room inside to smash it to pieces.

![The Lounge](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231809Q12HE7oM8nif304G.jpeg-w600)

The Library was a somewhat quieter house, curated by Sing Lit Station — the organizers of another Design Week exhibition, Out of Print. Its ground floor teamed up with the well-known local independent bookstore Basheer Graphic Books to set up a reading room where you could read or buy books. The second floor was the "writers' room": every day during the event, a different writer was invited to live there, and visitors could chat with them and ask anything.

![The Library](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231843YijMWxfwuOTkXDIt.jpeg-w600)

The Playground was probably the kids' favorite house. Before entering, visitors had to pass through a small maze made of string called "String Maze," sized just for children — if you accidentally touched a "trap" along the way, you'd get tangled up.

Various themed workshops were held here too. Saturday Kids, an organization in children's education, used games to teach kids about programming, and Inez Designs brought a hands-on workshop welcoming parents and kids to play together.

![The Playground](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315231904plNo4OLmqB7Z9CWA.jpeg-w600)

Finally there was The Stage, a stage for local music bringing together Singapore's musicians and artists, with live performances every day. Its second floor held a very special restaurant: knives and forks were laid out on the tables, but the plates held headphones, cassette tapes and MP3 players instead.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315232042u6PCl8Wj9FfbOS3T.jpeg-w600)

![The Stage](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315232045APDpir7mRsIwYZxT.jpeg-w600)

The Kitchen, true to its name, was a delicious house — with pop-ups of local street snacks and homegrown foods, plus participation from some chain brands. Häagen-Dazs, for instance, decorated one of the second-floor rooms with flowers and sold its newest ice cream flavors there. A Häagen-Dazs brand representative on site explained that the company chose to run its event here to promote its new season of ice cream and showcase packaging designs from different designers.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803152321180pne9mYh1ZvwfB4S.jpeg-w600)

![The Kitchen](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180315232119dw6ZQ1Yq7KCnjaOv.jpeg-w600)

The first weekend after opening, the street welcomed 9,998 visitors. Justin partnered with more than 30 merchants around Holland Village, distributing promotions and discounts during the event and making custom coffees, cakes and desserts. What surprised him was that 16 other households spontaneously joined in: the neighbors' kids sold lemonade, cakes and baked goods at their own doorsteps, while others sold artwork out of their garages.

The results exceeded expectations, and Justin called it a "bottom-up" grassroots movement. His reasoning: although it was part of Singapore Design Week, the content, the organizers and the participants had all come together spontaneously — the DesignSingapore Council set no rigid rules.

It was also the first time Justin had staged an event of this scale. Founder of a creative agency, he and Jerry Goh started HJGHER in 2006, a company whose main business covers brand design, curation and interior design, among other creative work. When he first entered the design field, Justin describes Singapore design as still in its "infancy." Like most Singapore designers, they were learning about their own culture and figuring out what more they could do in the future.

In 2009, HJGHER launched a magazine called *Underscore*, showcasing art, culture, fashion, design and the creative industries from around the world. Through *Underscore*, Justin got to know more than 500 creative workers from all over the world — and the participants in this event came almost entirely from his circle of friends.

In the Holland Village plan, Justin's biggest challenge was time. From winning the open call to the event's opening was only three short months, in which he had to conceive, plan, implement and execute everything — from negotiating and confirming the plan with the government to arranging exactly how many tables, chairs and how much food the site would need.

Though most of the work had to be done by himself, Justin would rather credit the event to the collaboration of many forces.

When Justin first took over the ten houses, they were completely empty — not even air conditioning. Although the houses were rented for only a month and the event lasted just ten days, he had to pay out of his own pocket for a batch of air conditioners to fend off the heat. Later he went to the landlord — the Singapore Land Authority — to negotiate, and was told the air conditioners wouldn't have to be removed when the event wound down; the cost would be covered by the government.

The approach to planning the Holland Village event was similar to making a magazine. Justin first found ten idle houses on a single street in Chip Bee Garden within Holland Village, then went door to door asking the other 79 households on that street for their consent.

The next question was how to fill each house with content, giving every one a different theme, like the different sections of a magazine. Justin wanted the activities to be approachable and rich — ideally "with things to eat, drink and play" — so that everyone could truly enjoy themselves.

"It's as if we brought everyone together to one big party," he said. "That's all it is."
