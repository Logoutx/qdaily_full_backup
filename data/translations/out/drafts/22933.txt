## About the Author

Richard Preston (b. 1954) is an American nonfiction writer and a contributor to *The New Yorker*. He earned his PhD in English at Princeton University, where he studied under the renowned nonfiction writer John McPhee. Preston specializes in treating scientific subjects with literary nonfiction techniques. In 1984 he published his first nonfiction work, *First Light*, a book about astronomy that won the American Institute of Physics science-writing award. A decade later, Preston produced another classic of science writing, *The Hot Zone*. This account of the origins of the Ebola virus was a huge success, spending 61 weeks at the top of The New York Times nonfiction bestseller list. For it, Preston received the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's "Champion of Prevention" award — and he remains the only person in history to win it as a non-physician.

Charles Monet was a loner. The Frenchman's solitary cabin sat within the private land of the Nzoia Sugar Factory. The plantation lay in western Kenya, along the Nzoia River, with a view of majestic Mount Elgon. Mount Elgon, a massive extinct volcano standing alone beside the Great Rift Valley, rises fourteen thousand feet. Monet's past was unknown. Like many outsiders who end up settling in Africa, it is hard to say exactly why he came. Perhaps he had gotten into trouble in France; perhaps Kenya's beauty drew him in. He was an amateur naturalist who loved birds and beasts but did not much care for people. He was fifty-six years old, of medium height and medium build, with a head of smooth, straight brown hair — a reasonably handsome man. His only close friends were women in the villages around Mount Elgon, but when doctors came to investigate the cause of his death, they could not recall much about him. His job was to maintain the sugar factory's pumping machinery, the equipment that drew water from the Nzoia River to irrigate the sugarcane fields stretching for miles. He reportedly spent most of each day in the pump house by the river, and he seemed to enjoy watching the machines run to the roar of their engines.

In cases like his, the details are often hard to pin down. The doctors remember the clinical symptoms, because once you have seen what a Biosafety Level 4 organism does to a human body, you can never forget it; the symptoms pile up one after another until, in the end, they swallow the person crushed beneath them. The Monet case contains both icy clinical data and horrific firsthand imagery — startling enough to make us step back and blink, as if we had glimpsed a strangely discolored foreign sun.

Monet came to this country in the summer of 1979, when the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) that causes AIDS was finally breaking out of the rain forests of central Africa and beginning its long campaign of tormenting humankind. The shadow of AIDS had already fallen over humanity, but at this point no one knew it existed. It spread quietly along the Kinshasa Highway, which crosses the African continent from east to west, passing within sight of Mount Elgon and by Lake Victoria. HIV is a Biosafety Level 2 organism — highly lethal, but not very contagious. It does not pass easily from person to person, and it cannot travel through the air. You do not need a biohazard suit to handle HIV-infected blood samples.

On workdays, Monet toiled in the pump house; on weekends and holidays, he wandered the wooded areas near the sugar factory. He would bring food and scatter it on the ground, watching the birds and beasts come to eat. He would sit perfectly still, observing the animals. People who knew him recalled that he was especially fond of wild monkeys and had a special way of getting along with them; he would sit holding a piece of food and wait for a monkey to approach, and the monkey would eat right out of his hand.

In the evenings, he always stayed at his quarters. He had a housekeeper named Johnnie, who kept the place clean and did the cooking. He was teaching himself to identify African birds. A group of weaver birds roosted in a tree near his home, and he spent a good deal of time watching them build and repair their pouch-like nests. One day around Christmas, the story goes, he brought home a sick bird, and the bird died in his house — perhaps even in his hands. It may have been a weaver bird — but no one can be sure; it may have died of some Biosafety Level 4 virus — again, no one can be sure. He had also befriended a crow, an African pied crow, a black-and-white bird that Africans often keep as a pet. The pied crow was friendly and smart; it liked to perch on the roof of Monet's quarters, watching him come and go. When the crow was hungry, it would land on the veranda, walk inside, and Monet would feed it small morsels from his table.

Charles Monet returned to work at the sugar factory's pump house. Walking through the charred cane fields every day, he no doubt lifted his head to take in the view of Mount Elgon; even when heavy clouds hid the summit, he could still feel its pull, like the gravity of some invisible planet. At the same time, something was replicating itself inside Monet. Using Charles Monet's body as its host, it was multiplying furiously.

Normally, seven days after exposure to this virus, the patient begins to get a headache. Seven days after his New Year's Day visit to Kitum Cave — January 8, 1980 — Monet felt a dull, throbbing pain behind his eyeballs. He decided to take the day off and rest at home. The headache grew worse. His eyeballs hurt, then his temples began to ache, the pain circling inside his skull as if looking for a way out. Aspirin did nothing, and then his back started to hurt badly. His housekeeper Johnnie was still away on her Christmas holiday, so he found someone to help out temporarily. She did her best to look after Monet but had no idea how to help. On the third day after the headache began, he developed nausea, a high fever, and vomiting. The vomiting grew worse and turned into dry heaves. By now he had become, strangely, docile. His face lost all vitality and turned into an expressionless mask, his eyeballs staring fixedly as if paralyzed. His eyelids drooped slightly, as if the eyeballs wanted to pop out while the eyes themselves were about to close. The eyeballs themselves seemed to have frozen in their sockets, turning bright red. The skin of his face turned yellow, marked by conspicuous star-shaped red spots. He looked more and more like a zombie. The sight terrified the temporary housekeeper. She could not understand why the man had become like this. His personality changed along with his appearance, turning ever darker and more irritable, and his memory seemed to have vanished altogether. He had not lost his mind and could answer questions, but he no longer seemed to know where he was.

Monet had not shown up at work for several days, and his colleagues began to worry, so they went to his home to see if he was all right. The black-and-white crow watched from the roof as they entered the house. When they saw Monet, they decided he had to go to a hospital. He was too sick to drive, so a colleague drove him to a private hospital in Kisumu, a town on the shore of Lake Victoria. After examining him, the doctors could not explain what had happened to his eyes, his face, and his mind. They guessed it might be some kind of bacterial infection and gave him an injection of antibiotics, but it did no good.

The doctors decided he should go to Nairobi Hospital, the best private hospital in East Africa. The telephone system did not work well, and besides, there was no point going to the trouble of telling them a patient was on his way. Monet could still walk, and traveling alone seemed no problem. He had money, and he understood that he had to get to Nairobi. Everyone saw him into a taxi to the airport, where he boarded a Kenya Airways flight.

A dangerous virus from the rain forest can reach any city on Earth within twenty-four hours by plane. Air routes link all the world's cities into a network. Once a virus enters the network, it can arrive in a single day in any city a plane can reach: Paris, Tokyo, New York, Los Angeles. Charles Monet had carried the organism inside him into this network.

He was flying on a propeller-driven Fokker Friendship, a commuter aircraft rated for thirty-five passengers. The plane started its engines and flew over Lake Victoria, its deep blue water glittering, dotted with fishermen's dugout canoes. The Friendship turned east, crossing green hills covered with tea plantations and small farms. African commuter flights are almost always full, and this one was most likely no exception. The plane flew over belts of forest, over clusters of round thatched-roof huts, over villages with corrugated-iron roofs. The terrain dropped sharply into ledges and gullies, and the color shifted from green to brown. The plane was crossing the Great Rift Valley of East Africa. Passengers looked out the window at the cradle of humankind, seeing scattered thatched huts amid rings of thorn scrub, cattle trails radiating outward from the huts. The propellers droned as the Friendship passed through the cloud streets — the fluffy cumulus clouds above the rift — and the fuselage shuddered and shook. Monet got airsick.

The seats on a commuter flight are cramped and crowded; whatever happens in the cabin, you notice it. The cabin is sealed, the air circulating. If there is an odor, you smell it immediately. You cannot fail to see a desperately sick man. He is huddled in his seat. Something is wrong with him, but you cannot say exactly what.

He covers his mouth with an airsickness bag, coughing from deep in his lungs, and vomits something into the bag. The bag swells. Perhaps he glances around, and you see his lips smeared with a red slime flecked with black, as if he has been chewing coffee grounds. His eyes are the color of rubies, his face utterly expressionless, covered in bruises. The star-shaped red spots that appeared days earlier have spread and merged into large purple masses: his entire head has turned black and blue. The facial muscles droop; the connective tissue is dissolving, and the face seems to hang off the underlying bones, as if it is slowly detaching from the skull. He opens his mouth and vomits into the bag, endlessly — the vomiting truly does not stop; his stomach has long been empty, yet he keeps bringing up fluid. What fills the airsickness bag is known as "vomito negro" — the black vomit. The black vomit is not entirely black; the fluid has two colors: black granules like asphalt mixed into bright red arterial blood. This is internal bleeding, and it smells like a slaughterhouse. The black vomit is laden with virus, highly infectious and highly lethal — enough to terrify military biohazard specialists. The smell of the black vomit fills the cabin. When the airsickness bag is full, Monet closes it and rolls the top over. The bag is swollen and soft from soaking, on the verge of bursting, and he hands it to the flight attendant.

When a dangerous virus multiplies inside its host, the virus particles infiltrate every part of the body, from the brain to the skin. Military experts would later say that the virus had undergone "extreme amplification." This is no common cold. At the peak of extreme amplification, a single drop of the patient's blood the size of an eye-dropper dose contains hundreds of millions of virus particles. In other words, the life form that has occupied the host's body is attempting to transform the host into itself. The transformation does not fully succeed, and the result is a mass of liquefied flesh mixed with virus — a kind of biological accident. What happened inside Monet was precisely this so-called extreme amplification, and its outward manifestation was the "black vomit."

His whole body has gone stiff, as if moving might snap something inside. His blood is clotting: the bloodstream carries the clots, which lodge throughout the body — the liver, kidneys, lungs, hands, feet, and brain are all stuffed with congealed blood. In short, his entire body is having strokes. The clots pile up in the smooth muscle of the intestines, cutting off the blood supply. The intestinal muscle begins to die, and the gut starts to turn black. He can no longer feel pain fully, because the clots accumulating in his brain are blocking blood flow. The brain damage erases his personality. This is the so-called depersonalization, in which vitality and character traits drain away. He is slowly becoming a robot. Small regions of brain tissue are liquefying. The higher functions of consciousness are the first to be ground away; only the deep regions of the brain stem — the primitive "rat brain," the "lizard brain" — remain alive and working. One might say it this way: Charles Monet's soul is already dead; only his body lives on.

The vomiting seems to have ruptured the blood vessels in his nasal passages, and his nose begins to bleed. Bright red arterial blood without clots streams from both nostrils, dripping onto his teeth and chin. The bleeding will not stop, because the clotting factors are exhausted. The flight attendant hands him a wad of tissues, and he plugs his nostrils with them, but the blood will not clot and the tissues are quickly soaked through.

When you're on a plane and the passenger beside you suddenly falls ill, you certainly won't call attention to it — you don't want to embarrass him. You tell yourself this person will be fine. Maybe he's just not used to flying? He's airsick, poor fellow, and besides, people get nosebleeds on planes all the time, the air is so dry and thin... You'll lean over and quietly ask if he needs help. He doesn't answer, or mumbles a few words you can't make out, so you decide to look the other way — though the plane seems in no hurry to land. The flight attendant may have asked him too whether he needed help, but patients infected with this kind of deadly virus undergo behavioral changes that leave them unable to respond to kindness. They become hostile and do not want to be touched. They seem unable to speak properly. They can state their name, but not what day of the week it is, nor explain what is happening to them.

The Friendship passes through the clouds and glides along the Great Rift Valley. Monet slumps in his seat, apparently dozing... Perhaps some passenger wondered if he was dead. No, no, he's not dead. He's moving. His red eyes open, and the eyeballs shift slightly.

It is evening now; the sun has dropped behind the mountains west of the rift, casting shafts of light in every direction, as if the sun had shattered on the equator. The Friendship banks slowly, heading toward the eastern wall of the rift. The ground rises higher, the color shifting from brown back to green. The Ngong Hills appear beneath the right wing, and the plane begins its descent, skimming over savanna where zebras and giraffes can be seen. A minute later, the plane lands at Jomo Kenyatta International Airport. Monet stirs. He can still walk. He stands up, blood dripping. He walks down the stairs and onto the tarmac. His shirt is soaked with blood. He has no luggage. His luggage is all inside his body — countless multiplied viruses. Monet has become a human virus bomb. He slowly walks into the terminal, crosses the building, and arrives at the curve where taxis gather. The taxi drivers swarm around him — "Taxi?" "Taxi?"

"Nairobi... Hospital," he mumbles.

One of the drivers helps him into the car. Nairobi taxi drivers like to chat with their customers, and this one most likely asked whether he was feeling unwell. The answer was plain to see. Monet feels his stomach settling a little. His stomach feels heavy, numb and swollen, as if he has just finished a big meal rather than burning with empty pain.

The taxi pulls onto Uhuru Highway, heading into downtown Nairobi. It crosses savanna dotted with acacia trees, passes factories, goes around a roundabout, and enters Nairobi's bustling streets. The shoulders are packed shoulder-to-shoulder with people; women walk along dirt roads, men stroll idly, children ride bicycles, a man by the roadside repairs shoes, a tractor hauls a load of charcoal. The taxi turns left onto Ngong Road, passes a city park, climbs a slope, drives past rows of blue gum trees, turns into a narrow lane, and goes through a gated entrance with a guard post — Nairobi Hospital at last. The car pulls into a taxi space beside a flower kiosk. On the glass door is a sign reading "Outpatient Department." Monet takes out money to pay the driver, gets out, opens the glass door, and walks toward the reception desk, gesturing that he is desperately ill. It has already become very hard for him to speak.

This man is bleeding — wait a moment, a doctor will see you right away. He has to wait for a doctor to become free, but a doctor will see you immediately, don't worry. He walks into the waiting room and sits down.

The waiting room is a small room with padded benches. Clear, intense, ancient East African light pours through a row of windows onto a table stacked with grubby magazines, casting square patches of brightness on the gray stone floor, with a drain at its center. The room smells faintly of wood smoke and sweat, and is full of patients with dull, vacant eyes — Africans and Europeans sitting shoulder to shoulder. The outpatient department always has people waiting with cuts that need stitches. They are patient, holding towels to their scalps, bandages wrapped around their fingers; you can see blood seeping through the cloth. And so Charles Monet sits on a bench in the outpatient department, looking no different from the other patients in the waiting room — except for his expressionless, blue-black face and his red eyes. Notices on the wall warn patients to beware of pickpockets, and one notice reads:

**Please Keep Quiet**

**Thank You for Your Cooperation**

**Please Note: This Is the Outpatient Department**

**Emergency Patients Are Seen First**

**In Such Cases, Please Wait Patiently to Be Called**

Monet sits quietly, waiting to be called. Suddenly, he enters the final phase: the human virus bomb goes off. Military biohazard specialists have a term for this. They say the patient has "crashed and bled out" — or, more politely, that the patient has "gone down."

He feels dizzy, utterly weak; his spine collapses, limp and slack, and he loses all sense of balance. The room spins. He goes into shock. He bends forward, head on his knees, and with a spasmodic moan a huge volume of blood surges from his stomach and splashes onto the floor. He loses consciousness and pitches forward onto the ground. The only sound in the room is the gurgling in his throat; he has passed out, but he keeps vomiting blood and black matter. Then comes a sound like tearing bedsheets — the large intestine sloughing open, blood jetting from the anus. The blood is mixed with intestinal tissue. He is expelling his own gut. The intestinal wall has come loose and is expelled with the gushing blood. Monet has crashed, and his blood is running out.

The other patients in the waiting room scramble to their feet, backing away from the man on the floor, shouting for doctors. The pool of blood around him spreads fast. The deadly virus has destroyed its host, and now, busy burrowing out of every opening of his body, it is "trying" to find a new host.
