Between wet markets, big supermarkets, fresh-food e-commerce and convenience stores, why is there still room for fresh-food convenience stores to survive?

"Our store has been open for two years and it's still losing money. None of the fresh-food convenience stores around here make money," said a clerk at the Yonghui Life store on Shanghai's Anguo Road. By "fresh-food convenience stores," he meant the Suning Xiaodian and Li Xiaonong less than 50 meters away.

Tough profitability hasn't stopped more fresh-food stores from moving in. On November 25, one street over, the Qingmei Fresh Food store on Baoding Road was about to open. Next June, the first Shanghai store of 7FRESH, the Hema-style fresh-food supermarket under JD.com, will open at Shangbin Life Plaza — right across from Yonghui Life.

At the other end of the neighborhood, competition among fresh-food retailers on Tianzhen Road may be even fiercer: next to Yonghui Life sits a Sandan Fresh. Twenty meters further on there's a Suning Xiaodian, and ten meters past that are the front warehouses of the fresh-food e-commerce players Dingdong Maicai and Liangxian. Ten minutes north, Xingang Road has a CP Lotus hypermarket, with Laodu Chongming Ecological Produce across the street.

Residents here seem to have an unusually wide range of options for buying fresh food.

Taking Linping Road metro station as the center, there are already eight fresh-food convenience stores within one kilometer. That doesn't count the two wet markets, or the Hema Fresh, which is bigger and has dining areas.

![The competitive landscape of fresh-food convenience stores near Linping Road](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181126023611yD8oWLUdZrJFB9PC.jpg-w600)

A similar competitive landscape — Suning Xiaodian opening right next to Yonghui Life, Li Xiaonong gazing across several streets at Kangpinhui — also shows up along Changshou Road–Zhenping Road and Yanggao Middle Road–Century Park. It may play out in more Shanghai neighborhoods in the future.

More than 300 fresh-food convenience stores have appeared in Shanghai; counting wet markets converted into fresh-food supermarkets, the number may exceed 350.

As a relatively new retail format, the "fresh-food convenience store" is not yet a clearly defined retail category — it blends traits of the community supermarket, the wet market and the convenience store.

We visited more than 15 fresh-food convenience stores. If we were to distill their common traits into a rulebook, it might read as follows.

### The Rules of Fresh-Food Convenience Stores

**1. More than half of the merchandise is fresh food**

The most important thing in a fresh-food convenience store is, of course, fresh produce — vegetables, fruit, chilled fresh meat, river and seafood. In these stores, fresh-food SKUs (stock-keeping units) can account for up to 50% of total SKUs. More than half of the merchandise is fresh food.

Big-box hypermarkets chasing a wide range of categories (appliances, bedding, daily necessities, personal care, household care, packaged food, etc.) won't have such a high fresh-food share. Walmart's fresh-food share is around 10%; RT-Mart's is about 20%. Judging by the total number of fresh-food SKUs, those big supermarkets may not have fewer than fresh-food convenience stores — they may even have more.

Precisely because fresh food is the most important and most richly varied product, fresh-food convenience stores display it at the entrance to imprint it on passing shoppers. For a street-front store, the entrance is the most valuable display position, usually reserved for flagship, most competitive products. At the entrances of FamilyMart, Lawson or 7-Eleven, you'll generally find the chains' own-brand bread and pastries, along with some featured promotional items.

At Kangpinhui, Yonghui Life, Suning Xiaodian and Mishu Fresh, the entrance is a flat spread of fruit and vegetables, with wall-mounted coolers around the sides holding chilled fresh meat, fresh milk and drinks. Non-food items (paper, sanitary napkins, personal and household care products) sit at the back.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181126023807uV6qMWPR3lSJjA7r.jpg-w600)

Not only do vegetables or fruit get the most important shelves — they also take up more display space, meaning they're spread wider. Yi Huairen, operations director at Mishu Fresh, told QDaily that more than half of its shelves are used to display fresh food. Products less important to a fresh-food convenience store (say, toilet paper and hand soap) are packed more tightly — taking up a single slot, for instance, displayed in depth.

**2. More refined selection and packaging — "one-stop shopping" for the dinner table**

Compared with wet markets or the older generation of community supermarkets (Nonggongshang, Dia), fresh-food convenience stores offer a better environment — newer premises, brighter lighting, probably better upkeep, and no unpleasant smells.

![Packaged vegetables at Yonghui Life (Photo: Dong Zhifei)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181125215535fy9hSVMe8F6jsKr3.jpg-w600)

The products, too, are more "refined." Yonghui Life, CP Fresh Mart and Easy Carrefour weigh and pre-pack leafy greens in plastic bags. This is a form of standardization for vegetables; compared with loose produce customers pick over themselves, packaged vegetables cut the shrinkage rate. Skipping the weighing and picking also makes packaged vegetables faster and more convenient to buy — which appeals to younger consumers.

Kangpinhui and Li Xiaonong, among others, keep loose vegetables. Zhu Xiaobin says this better fits people's traditional buying habits: older shoppers want to be able to pick and choose when they buy vegetables. Through operational tricks — such as having staff clear out the less presentable vegetables after peak hours and sell them at a discount — the produce looks more attractive after the cleanup, while shrinkage falls and gross margins rise.

Beyond fresh ingredients, these stores also sell other at-home products — grain and cooking oil, beverages, condiments, frozen goods, packaged food (such as instant noodles and self-heating hot pots). These are also items consumers grab on impulse while buying fresh food. Like fresh food, these products are consumed at home: the second step after purchase is taking them home to prepare. This distinguishes them from convenience stores, which mainly sell on-the-go food — bread, rice balls or bento boxes bought at a convenience store are generally eaten right away.

Yonghui Life, Kangpinhui and others also sell imported foods (such as craft beer) and imported daily-use products, with a more upmarket selection than the older generation of community supermarkets.

**3. Street-front community stores of around 150 square meters**

Compared with wet markets or supermarkets, fresh-food convenience stores are relatively small — closer to the mid-sized stores of FamilyMart, 7-Eleven or Lawson.

The mainstream size is 100–150 square meters. According to Yonghui's financial reports, the average Yonghui Life store in Shanghai is 146 square meters. Some fresh-food convenience stores reach 300 square meters — CP Fresh Mart stores, for instance, are on the large side; compared with Yonghui, their displays are more spacious, and they even have fish and butcher counters.

Whether 100 or 300 square meters, the smaller fresh-food convenience stores have far more options for street-front space near residential communities than big supermarkets or even wet markets, and can expand more flexibly.

![CP Fresh Mart's Yuyao Road store (Photo: [Triad](https://www.triad.de/zh/xiang-mu/CP_Fresh_Mart/))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181125215926m59k7ZVNoDh6UtRr.jpg-w600)

![CP Fresh Mart (Photo: [Triad](https://www.triad.de/zh/xiang-mu/CP_Fresh_Mart/))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181125215819H4bR2TcEuPsVhdXO.jpg-w600)

![CP Fresh Mart (Photo: [Triad](https://www.triad.de/zh/xiang-mu/CP_Fresh_Mart/))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181125215822NSyWBMUuY8ihqkKC.jpg-w600)

All these fresh-food stores like to open at the gates of residential communities — in short, high-traffic spots people pass on their way to and from work. That's why you see fresh-food convenience stores clustering on a single street, such as Anguo Road and Tianzhen Road in Hongkou District.

Proximity to the residential compound is an important site-selection principle. Zhu Xiaobin, operations head at Li Xiaonong, says locations should be "in densely populated residential areas, ideally at the gates of several communities."

Whether it's fruit, vegetables or meat, everyone wants to buy close by — the closer the better. Among the 13 shoppers we randomly interviewed at fresh-food convenience stores, "on my way" and "close by" were the most common phrases. "Convenience really matters," said Wei Jianli, head of East China commercial property services at C&W. She shops for groceries and cooks herself: "If I can buy it somewhere closer, I won't walk ten extra steps."

The "close by" and "on my way" principles for fresh food exist for two reasons. First, freshness — many families buy groceries every day, and such a high-frequency behavior has to happen nearby; going far is inconvenient. Second, fresh products (especially vegetables and fruit) are low-differentiation goods, so for many people it makes little sense to walk 20 minutes to a bigger wet market.

Unlike convenience stores focused on ready-to-eat food, fresh-food convenience stores generally don't choose spots near commercial districts, where rents are higher. "Even if consumers go to the shopping district, when they buy fruit and vegetables they usually still come back to their own neighborhood gate," Zhu Xiaobin said. Like convenience stores, fresh-food convenience stores avoid sites on arterial roads and elevated highways, choosing smaller streets instead. Big roads cut them off and block them from view.

**4. More add-on services than a wet market**

Nearly all fresh-food convenience stores offer extra services — product pre-ordering, home delivery, returns and exchanges, and other after-sales services. These are things individual vegetable vendors in wet markets can hardly manage.

Most fresh-food convenience stores have community WeChat groups — Li Xiaonong, Kangpinhui and Suning Xiaodian, among others. "Limited pre-order of Yunnan black tomatoes," "Last few boxes of long-stem strawberries, grab them now" — every day, clerks post similar group-buying or promotional items in the groups.

Proximity is so important in fresh-food consumption that a store's regulars are a relatively fixed group — nearby residents. WeChat groups have become one way community fresh-food stores manage customer relationships. Consumers can order fresh products directly in the WeChat group (most stores offer pickup or delivery), give feedback on products, and even arrange returns.

Hema, which the whole industry watches, has also built a "Hema Community" feature into its app to push promotions and maintain customer relationships. CP Fresh Mart, Yonghui Life and Easy Carrefour do not operate community groups.

Delivery is an almost indispensable value-added service for every fresh-food convenience store. Except for Suning Xiaodian and Li Xiaonong, which run their own delivery, all the others have connected to third-party food-delivery platforms. Three kilometers is a common delivery radius.

At some stores, regulars can even pre-order food by phone and pay when they pick it up later. None of these services are common at wet markets.

Meanwhile, many new fresh-food stores have become more "generous" with returns and exchanges. Li Xiaonong, Mishu Fresh and others support no-questions-asked returns. Partly that's because these stores have negotiated certain return ratios with distributors and brands.

### Between wet markets, big supermarkets, fresh-food e-commerce and convenience stores, why is there still room for fresh-food convenience stores to survive?

**1. Office workers' time constraints**

Fresh-food convenience stores meet the needs of office workers, who have no time for the wet market's early-morning session and find it closed by the time they get off work in the evening.

Compared with the young consumer base of online fresh-food retail (Hema's customers are mainly post-80s and post-90s — that is, people under 40), fresh-food convenience stores draw from a broader age range. Our offline observations confirmed this: shoppers ranged from young people around 25 to retirees over 70.

A clerk at the Yonghui Life store on Anguo Road told me the main customer group is people around 40. These middle-aged shoppers are actually a clientele rarely seen at wet markets.

Compared with the past, when most jobs ran nine-to-five, Shanghai residents now work longer hours and spend longer commuting.

According to the Department of Employment at the National Bureau of Statistics, in the first half of 2018 the average weekly working hours of employees across all types of enterprises was 45.9 hours; ten years ago the figure was 44.6 hours. Shanghai has long been one of the cities with the longest working hours in the country.

Ten years ago, Shanghai residents' one-way commute was about 36 minutes. Today, whether you use data from the Shanghai Municipal Human Resources and Social Security Bureau (43 minutes) or from companies like Jiguang (54 minutes), commute times have increased. Moreover, analysis by DT Finance found that the younger a Shanghai resident is, the longer their commute.

Wu Xiaojun lives in an old residential compound near Linping Road. She's a surgeon; her husband works at SAIC. Neither of them goes to wet markets or supermarkets; they generally buy groceries at Yonghui Life or on Hema. "By five or six p.m. there's nothing left at the wet market... probably just whatever's left over." Fresh-food convenience stores or fresh-food e-commerce generally stay open until around 10 p.m., meeting the needs of working people who shop for ingredients after work at 6 or 7 p.m. If a fresh-food convenience store happens to be right at the neighborhood gate, whether for pickup or delivery it's likely faster than a hypermarket or fresh-food O2O.

"Faster" has indeed become an important factor in everyday consumption decisions.

The development of e-commerce and delivery has "spoiled" consumers. Young people are the IWWIWWIWI (I want what I want, when I want it!) generation, but older post-70s and post-80s are changing too. "On the whole, consumers are becoming increasingly impatient — they want to be able to buy anytime, anywhere," said Derek Deng, a Bain & Company global partner and senior leader of its Greater China consumer products and retail practice.

**2. Not enough wet markets; no desire to go to supermarkets**

There aren't enough wet markets, and that gives fresh-food convenience stores an opening.

According to the *Shanghai Planning Outline for the Wholesale and Retail Markets of Edible Agricultural Products (2013–2020)*, Shanghai needs about 1,350 standardized wet markets of 1,500 square meters each, but in 2017 the whole city had only 869 standardized wet markets. That was even slightly down from 880 in 2012 (though the total inched up if "non-standard" markets are included). Over those years Shanghai also added roughly 400,000 permanent residents.

Derek Deng told QDaily that 70–80% of China's fresh-food consumption still happens in traditional channels like wet markets and produce shops, while modern channels represented by supermarkets and fresh-food convenience stores contribute only 20–30% of fresh-food sales. He believes fresh-food convenience stores mainly steal business from wet markets.

![Li Xiaonong (Photo: Li Xiaonong's Weibo)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201811252200340x8gC1NIDl3Xmi9J.jpg-w600)

That consumers increasingly dislike going to big supermarkets is no longer news.

According to Bain and Kantar data, since 2015 sales of fast-moving consumer goods at hypermarkets (supermarkets over 6,000 square meters) have been declining. In *China Shopper 2018*, jointly published by the two firms, the hypermarket channel continued to shrink, with shopping frequency, average annual household purchase volume and penetration all falling.

At 6:30 on a weekday evening, in the fresh-food section of the CP Lotus at Youdian Xincun, I counted five young people under 40; the other dozen or so were middle-aged and elderly. Cheng Jiayi, 30, said her last visit was more than a week ago. "Day-to-day necessities can all be bought online, so I don't come to the supermarket much."

Because of their size, hypermarkets are limited in site selection and can't always be near residential communities. And while hypermarkets may offer a rich selection in some fresh-food categories (eggs and milk, for instance), fresh food is generally not near the entrance, which lengthens the time consumers spend hunting for goods. When it comes to buying fresh food, condiments and packaged food in one trip, fresh-food convenience stores may be replacing the hypermarket's function. Wei Jianli, head of East China commercial property services at C&W, told QDaily in an interview that fresh-food convenience stores are hitting hypermarkets hard.

In 2017, supermarket and small-supermarket sales grew 4.8%, which Bain and Kantar attribute in part to the role they play in O2O delivery services. "One important trend in retail is hypermarkets getting smaller," Derek Deng told QDaily. Yonghui Life, Easy Carrefour (stores near residential communities) and CP Fresh Mart are small-format experiments by retailers that traditionally excelled at hypermarkets.

**3. Retailers are willing to run fresh-food convenience stores because they see an opportunity to integrate the supply chain**

On one hand, people's lifestyles are changing; on the other, the retail companies that shape our lifestyles also have a (profit) motive to open fresh-food convenience stores.

Fresh food is a high-frequency purchase and a staple need for most Chinese households. The entire fresh-food market is worth 5 trillion yuan in annual sales — even larger than the 4 trillion yuan catering industry.

Meanwhile, modern channels account for a small share of total fresh-food sales — only 20–30% — and e-commerce penetration is even lower, around 3%. So the category has room to grow. Starting with fresh food, retailers can ride along into the broader business of the dinner table — semi-prepared ingredients, ready-to-eat dishes, condiments, packaged food. In short, the business of eating can be very big.

![Photo: Xu Tao](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181125220919UrD78NZki2v0qaIO.jpg-w600)

Fresh food sold through wet markets passes through several tiers of distribution, and costs grow with each first-tier, second-tier handoff. If a retailer can buy directly at source and integrate the supply chain, its profit margins keep growing. In fact, not only Yonghui, which grew out of the "farm-to-supermarket" conversion, and CP Group, which has long invested in agriculture — other retail companies (online and offline alike), such as Alibaba and JD.com, are all trying to integrate the fresh-food supply chain. The parent companies of Li Xiaonong, Kangpinhui and CP Fresh Mart all have upstream agriculture businesses or investments.

Although there is room in the market for fresh-food convenience stores to survive, most retailers are still feeling their way with fresh food. Because fresh food has low gross margins (Yonghui's financials show fresh food at only around 15% gross margin, below the 19% for food and general merchandise), high shrinkage in transport and sales, and a longer, more complex supply chain, genuinely profitable players are few. Derek Deng said: "Either you sell those high-price, high-margin categories (note: such as imported seafood), or you build very large scale and connect online, offline and the supply chain — only then can you make money."

Suning Xiaodian, whose operations are still immature, hopes to overtake Yonghui through prime locations and scaled-up store counts. This year it acquired Dia's China business and is still accelerating store openings; it already has 77 stores in Shanghai. From January to July this year, Suning Xiaodian's revenue was only 143 million yuan, with losses of 296 million yuan and debt as high as 653 million yuan. In October, Suning transferred its controlling stake in Suning Xiaodian to an affiliated company, taking Suning Xiaodian's results off Suning.com's books. It seems Suning plans to let Suning Xiaodian burn money for a while longer.

Another big piece of fresh-food retail news: Yonghui has lowered its store-opening target for Yonghui Life. Early this year it planned to open 1,000 Yonghui Life stores this year, but as of the end of the third quarter it had opened just over 400. The Yun Chuang business, which includes Yonghui Life, is still losing money. In the first half of 2018, Yonghui Cloud Ventures recorded revenue of 905 million yuan and losses of 387 million yuan.

"Middle-aged and elderly people are extremely price-sensitive — they generally buy at the wet market up front first, then top up at Yonghui on the way back," a clerk at the Yonghui store on Anguo Road told me.

Wan Qi, 76, comes to Yonghui Life after dinner for a stroll, picking up some discounted fruit or meat. What he said couldn't be more ordinary, yet it captures the complexity of the competition: "I don't buy leafy greens here — I go to the produce shop on Dong Yuhang Road up ahead; it's cheaper there."
