Drawing on three days of forum talks and QDaily's ongoing coverage of the food and beverage industry, we've distilled 12 insights that could prove genuinely instructive for the industry.

What flavors do consumers actually want these days? Passion fruit or okra, or maybe some turmeric thrown in?

Growth in first- and second-tier cities has stalled — how do we sell chocolate into third- and fourth-tier cities?

With the economy slowing, how can the food industry actually make money?

How is advertising on e-commerce different from before?

So you hear people don't like sugar — well, how much sugar are we supposed to put in, then?

……

These were essentially the questions preoccupying the 1,300-plus industry professionals gathered last week at the FBIF Food and Beverage Innovation Forum — and the subjects explored across 65 talks by 79 speakers.

The forum, which focuses on R&D, marketing and packaging innovation in food and beverage, held its fourth edition this year. In the words of Bella, chairman of FBIF: "In the past, domestic food companies all liked going to trade fairs, where everyone looked for distributors. Things have changed now — distributors alone won't get the job done."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426001938cYnsGtEfWKHNI5kM.JPG-w600)

The food and beverage industry has never agonized over "how to grow" the way it does today. Across 2016, you could count on one hand the major companies whose annual revenue grew more than 5%: Nestlé and Hershey each grew just 3.2%; Coca-Cola and PepsiCo fell 5% and 0.2% respectively; and alcohol didn't fare any better — AB InBev's revenue ticked up a mere 0.2%, while its profit nearly halved from 2015.

A month earlier, Coca-Cola announced it was eliminating the chief marketing officer (CMO) role — a position that had existed for 24 years — to be replaced by a new figure: the chief growth officer (CGO). The CGO has to worry about marketing and the company's strategic growth alike.

Business is harder than it used to be. In the past, if you were a marketer for a century-old brand like Coca-Cola, the challenges you faced were far gentler than today's. Ma Ruchao, president of Mondelēz China and a fellow speaker, noted that the advantage of traditional channels is eroding — "marketing in third- and fourth-tier cities is getting harder and harder."

More and more, it's the small companies setting the trends for "what to eat and drink" — as for the big companies, the 707 M&A deals in the food and beverage industry in 2016 speak volumes. Ferrero acquired premium chocolatier Fannie May for US$115 million; AB InBev bought a Shanghai craft-beer bar; PepsiCo and Coca-Cola bought the probiotic drink maker KeVita and the soy-based beverage AdeS, respectively; and Kraft Heinz even tried a cross-sector bid for Unilever — which Unilever rebuffed.

![Image from Marketing Week](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426002207Ykp13igCTaWrQPwu.jpeg-w600)

And more of the difficulty really does lie in the product itself. As QDaily noted in our year-end piece ["15 Findings About Eating"](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/36019.html), consumers today are increasingly resistant to processed food. Americans seem to want to eat nothing at all, Chinese consumers can't seem to get enough, and the products of food giants have lost their charm across the board. This sets new demands for product R&D, marketing, and even design in the food and beverage industry.

Over the three short days from April 19 to April 21, food and beverage professionals traded views on the anxieties they share. Combined with QDaily's ongoing coverage of the industry, here are the insights we distilled.

### "It's not big companies crushing small ones anymore — it's the fast crushing the slow."

Fast, first of all, means reading consumer demand before anyone else does.

Among the top five sodas sold on Amazon in the US in 2014, alongside the old standbys Coca-Cola, Pepsi and Dr Pepper, a new brand appeared for the first time: Zevia. Launched in 2007, the soda brand became the fastest-growing "natural" product because it was the first to print "no artificial sweeteners" on its can.

Greek yogurt — high in protein, low in fat — is another fast-growing example. Chobani, which went on sale in 2007, took just five years to become the No. 3 non-frozen yogurt brand in the US, with annual sales of US$745 million.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425234433T7wAUuaSm0GokhWy.jpg-w600)

"We launch new products every six months, a dozen or twenty at a time." At his April 21 forum talk, Zhou Jin, Chobani's former global R&D director, cited Drink Chobani as an example of the company's development speed. Drink Chobani is a high-protein, drinkable yogurt; the team settled on the product concept in just a few weeks, and the finished product "doesn't make you feel the 14 grams of protein inside, nor does it taste like it has 40% less sugar than comparable products on the market."

The big companies, ironically, have become the followers. Yili and Danone both launched similar products — Yili's Ambrosial yogurt, in particular, is now the company's fastest-growing brand, with 2016 sales exceeding 8 billion yuan.

Beyond the new pecking order of who leads trends, when a category rises quickly, big and small companies make different choices. Take NFC juice: Zhilinguo Fang will pick cucumber or okra, while Nongfu Spring will most likely stick with the big categories like apple and orange.

### "When I was a kid, I'd never heard of a milkshake or a meal-replacement energy bar."

Three meals a day are becoming a thing of the past.

"Snacks have now become your staple food, and that is gradually changing how we think about snacks. Thirty percent of European consumers drink at least one meal-replacement shake or eat an energy bar. I had never heard of any of this when I was a kid."

In his talk on food consumption trends, Nathan Gray, senior editor at NutraIngredients, observed that convenience foods are increasingly changing the way people eat. "People are eating fewer proper meals and want to eat on the go."

A 2013 United Nations report showed that at least 2 billion people worldwide were eating protein bars made from insects. In 2015, the number of smoothie varieties worldwide grew 23% year over year — in contrast to the declining juice market in the US. According to Mintel, 15% of Americans buy smoothies for breakfast.

More people are snacking than ever, but that doesn't mean spring has arrived for the food giants. The fact that industrially produced snacks are "not nutritious" has become the single biggest problem food majors must solve.

First, the more snacks you eat, the more sugar you consume. Nestlé's recent news on this front was a formula that keeps the same taste while cutting sugar by 40%, aimed at boosting the competitiveness of its products down the road.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425234552G4fB0sVeX5xNoO9q.jpeg-w600)

Campbell Soup — the American maker of canned soup — is meanwhile pondering how to "add nutrition to snacks." The century-old brand now derives only 40% of its business from canned products; cookies like its famous blue-tin shortbread are among its offerings. According to Craig Stephen Slavtcheff, the company's vice president of R&D for global biscuits and snacks, speaking at FBIF (drawing on US Department of Agriculture statistics), half of American consumers eat four to six different snacks a day — or as many as seven — meaning a quarter of people's calorie intake comes from snacks.

"Our daily intake of trace elements such as vitamin D is extremely low in these snacks. This is a very urgent problem — the big food companies must change the formulation of snacks."

### "Everyone recognizes the function — so why doesn't it sell?"

Food or drink, nearly every company mentioned in its talk that consumers today expect more from food than "tastes good."

As Campbell's Craig put it, people now demand that snacks "meet nutritional needs and meet the need of the moment."

The so-called "functional food" is hardly a new concept. Kellogg's already set up a functional foods division in 1998 and launched the Ensemble line, said to lower cholesterol.

Functional foods of the past were eager to advertise their health benefits, creating the illusion of eating a better-tasting dietary supplement.

Danone's Activia ad claimed that "drinking Activia every day can regulate your digestive system within two weeks"; Kellogg's Frosted Mini-Wheats claimed the product was "clinically proven to improve children's attention by 20%." In the end, US regulators ruled both ads false and misleading.

But the "function" the food and beverage industry talks about today has changed from the past.

The rise of superfoods is one illustration. Google data shows that searches for the word "turmeric" grew 300% between 2011 and 2016, making it one of the most watched functional ingredients in recent years. In various articles of varying reliability, turmeric is described as a spice capable of improving everything from depression to cancer.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425235208UQ2j5RtJGm8BFL3h.png-w600)

![Organic juice with turmeric added](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426002330QHJWkmsG8wz0DFaf.jpg-w600)

Sophia Nadur, one of the April 20 speakers, brought along several drinks "fortified with turmeric" that day. "A lot of people believe turmeric helps you relieve stress, and many products containing it are selling in Europe now."

Food companies no longer present clear-cut data to persuade consumers; instead, they add ingredients carrying a "healthy" label — turmeric, kale, avocado — so consumers feel the products are good for them.

The question that immediately follows, though, is: once a company decides to use these "mysterious ingredients," how do you guarantee the taste?

We heard two views that converge by different routes.

First, Sophia Nadur and Nathan Gray, senior editor at NutraIngredients, both argued that you can't design a high-sugar product just to make some ingredient taste good. "It has to be low-sugar first," Sophia said. Nathan cited Red Bull: for certain function-driven products, "a slightly different — or frankly bad — taste can actually convey the message you want: this stuff works."

### "Awareness of probiotics reaches 70% in first-tier cities, but only 23% have actually eaten them."

The other view came from Dr. Jin Su, business director of Nutrition & Health, Asia-Pacific at DuPont, a probiotics supplier.

For now, probiotic products on the market are mainly probiotic yogurts. Jin Su hoped to tell the audience that fermentation and probiotic fortification is a way to improve taste and differentiate a product, one that could apply to many more categories — fermented plant protein, fruit and vegetable juice, tea drinks, and so on.

"There are lots of fermented products in Japan and Korea. In Japan, everyone recognizes the benefits and functions of soy — so why doesn't soy sell well in Asian countries? We've been discussing this for years. Image aside, it's also about taste, and Japan has done very well on that front, smoothing out some of soy's unpleasant flavors."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426002731Kf0tblXSPqxo2wE6.jpg-w600)

Jin Su shared a piece of consumer research in her talk: awareness of probiotics in first-tier cities reaches 70%, but when consumers were asked "have you ever taken a probiotic dietary supplement?", only 23% said yes. In other words, if a food company wants to enter the probiotic food market, it no longer needs to educate consumers.

In a way, this resembles the equation of superfoods with health. Data shows that [global retail sales of probiotic-related products had already reached US$41 billion by 2015; products containing probiotics grew 8% year over year from 2014 and are expected to grow another 37% over the next five years](https://www.naturalproductsinsider.com/articles/2016/09/the-new-market-profile-of-probiotics-consumption.aspx).

Some probiotic-labeled companies have indeed won the market's favor.

KeVita, a California beverage company founded in 2009, started with KeVita probiotic sparkling water and saw sales climb quickly after launching kombucha, a fermented tea drink; sales were around US$60 million in 2015. Then last November, Coca-Cola — a company that has never much liked acquisitions — spent US$200 million to buy KeVita.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426002813zbNvknFGxeWIZR71.jpg-w600)

Coca-Cola is plainly interested in probiotic drinks: beyond the acquisition, its Tropicana juice brand announced last year the launch of Tropicana Essentials Probiotics, a Tropicana juice with probiotics added.

Some food companies are also making fermented products. For instance, Farmhouse Culture, an American startup founded in 2008, makes chips from fermented vegetables and has already raised US$6.5 million across two rounds, with investors including General Mills.

### "The world of bottled water has changed enormously."

When beverage companies describe their consumers in the currently hot "alternative water" category, they tend to put it this way:

"They want a natural beverage with no additives, something low-sugar and low-calorie, with functional benefits to boot."

— Aloe water aids digestion and weight loss, cactus water contains antioxidants and electrolytes, and birch sap is a "blood cleanser."

Just last month, maple water company DRINKmaple raised US$5.36 million. Zenith, a market research firm in the food and beverage space, predicts that by 2020 the maple water market will be twice its current size.

![Tapped birch sap water — the packaging is even shaped like a birch tree](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003032GFfAOzUubBhZViKX.jpg-w600)

Charlie Hoare, founder of Tapped, a British birch sap brand, spoke at the forum about the state of alternative waters, flagging two problems the category needs to guard against right now.

First, beyond the genuinely nutritious ones — aloe water, maple water, cactus water, artichoke water — more and more quasi-plant-water products are muddying the market.

Take, for example, WTRMLN WTR, the watermelon water company Beyoncé invested in last year, whose product contains only watermelon juice and "a drop of organic lemon juice." It easily comes across as juice, but WTRMLN WTR mostly promotes itself as natural, electrolyte-replenishing, and suited to replacing sports drinks and sodas.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003125t4GqlhaJzgWkPjLD.jpg-w600)

The US has even seen banana water: Steuben Foods mixes banana juice with water, rather than being pure pressed banana juice. In Charlie Hoare's view, such products damage the image of "alternative water" in consumers' minds.

Second, among all these "XX waters," only coconut water has so far managed to scale up. Ten years ago coconut water was a product nobody cared about; last year the global coconut water market was already worth US$2.2 billion.

Charlie said that when peers do business, they shouldn't compare their own products to coconut water.

"Grabbing share by attacking a growing category is pointless." Some alternative water brands compare their calorie counts with coconut water's — "so what do we do next, drink tap water?"

### "Focusing only on young consumers is actually a double-edged sword."

The UN Population Division estimates that by 2050, the share of the global population over 60 will rise from 12% in 2015 to 22% — a huge opportunity for the food industry, and, of course, if mishandled, a source of enormous social problems.

Major companies are already investing in this. Last April, Nestlé announced it was investing in R&D for foods that "fight disease," claiming this mainly senior-facing market could be worth US$15 billion. In a February interview with Bloomberg last year, CEO Paul Bulcke said the company would "expand and acquire in skin care and health nutrition to reduce dependence on packaged food."

Zooming in on China, demographic shifts are equally worth watching. Yu Jian, general manager of Kantar Worldpanel China, said in his talk: "If you only focus on young consumers, China's demographic structure makes that a double-edged sword." On one hand, thanks to the long-standing one-child policy, the number of Chinese consumers aged 25–29 will decline over the next five years; on the other hand, in absolute terms those 300 million free-spending people outnumber the entire US population.

NutraIngredients editor Nathan Gray read the question on a longer horizon. "Over the next 30 years, the world's population will grow by another 2 billion, straining resources. Food prices will rise, and there will be supply-chain challenges."

### "We need to get into third- and fourth-tier cities."

Mondelēz — the owner of Oreo, Chips Ahoy and Halls — has been in China for 33 years, and now it wants to talk about how to get into third- and fourth-tier cities.

Ma Ruchao, president of Mondelēz China, shared a set of Nielsen data at the forum showing how multinational food giants are faring in China: between 2012 and 2016, the share of total consumption coming from first-tier cities declined, while spending power in third- and fourth-tier cities and townships rose, and shops and supermarkets in lower-tier cities expanded.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426152151dfYlyV7uWGR4e5TH.JPG-w600)

When most multinationals entered China in the 1990s, they started from first-tier cities — which means their sales teams and channel resources are concentrated there as well. For distributors in big cities, costs rise 2% for every 100 kilometers beyond their base. On top of that, companies like Mondelēz built their first- and second-tier channels on Walmart and Carrefour — and once you get to third- and fourth-tier cities, those channels stop working. "In Weihai, Shandong, a major local retailer is Jiajiayue — there's no Carrefour or Walmart locally, only one RT-Mart, and in Weihai that local retailer basically dominates the market."

Compared with 2000, Mondelēz's cost of sales in China has actually tripled, while the company's brand penetration fell from 65% to 56%.

"Consumers in third- and fourth-tier cities don't know our brands. It's not that they don't eat Oreos — they just don't know about them."

Local competing brands are also blocking Mondelēz's path into lower-tier cities. Dali Yuan, a biscuit brand from Fujian, holds less than 3% of the so-called "key urban markets" but more than 7% of third- and fourth-tier city share.

Put a Carrefour shelf in Shanghai next to a supermarket shelf in a third- or fourth-tier city, and the most obvious difference is that the former has entire rows of big-share-pack Oreos, while in the latter, Dali Yuan may well be the mainstream.

But for Ma Ruchao, none of this is the hardest part. The hardest part is "headquarters' expectations," because HQ won't "give you more money."

The solutions discussed afterward all revolved around saving money. First: deeper distribution. In China, Mondelēz will add 300 to 400 distributors and build a sub-distributor system.

Second, Ma Ruchao focused on how to "upgrade distributors" — using training to help them optimize efficiency and thereby increase their loyalty to Mondelēz. To reduce sales-staff costs in lower-tier cities, Mondelēz chose to partner with e-commerce platforms such as Alibaba to reach those markets through their platforms. "In some small shops you can also try vending machines — a small-scale way to sell gum."

Advertising has to save money too. Using national media like CCTV to cover third- and fourth-tier cities has become a bad deal; on a limited budget, mobile media is the better choice.

### "Stop spending on TV ads — and if you must, test it online first."

On TV advertising versus digital media, Mariano A. Bosaz, Coca-Cola's global senior digital director, and Ma Ruchao may hold similar views.

This year, a 30-second Super Bowl ad sold for US$5.5 million — and after the ads aired, both Budweiser and Audi, which skirted political themes, reaped volumes of negative comment.

Curiously, the most-shared ad in Super Bowl history also came from Audi, in 2011: a boy dressed as Darth Vader using "the Force" to start an Audi. Because the ad had been uploaded to YouTube well before kickoff, it built enormous buzz ahead of the game.

"The world we live in today is a digital one. Spending that much money on a 30-second ad without knowing whether consumers will even like it — that's an unnecessary risk," Mariano said.

### "The Coke tastes especially great at one place — guess where?"

Advertising is, at its core, about conveying information — but with the average attention span today just 8 seconds, how do you say more in 8 seconds?

Two case studies shared at the forum offer something of an answer: either get consumers to search for you on their own initiative, or tell your story where people are more patient.

The first case also came from Mariano. Not long ago, McDonald's ran this ad in the US:

"There's a place where the Coke tastes especially good. It's ___? Go Google it!"

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003612MNhq5wm2ZDxlLytI.jpeg-w600)

The ad helpfully added that Coke at "this place" was now US$1 for any size! Of course, the place you already know is McDonald's, Coca-Cola's partner.

If you actually Google "the place where Coke tastes especially good," you'll find several articles McDonald's placed on Google's homepage shortly before the ad went live, along the lines of "6 Reasons McDonald's Coke Tastes Better Than Everywhere Else."

The second case came from Tencent, from a talk by Luan Na, vice president of its Online Media Group and general manager of its advertising customer division.

On March 7, the mobile game Honor of Kings released a limited-edition skin, "Zhao Yun – Engine Heart," priced at 888 vouchers (roughly 88.8 yuan) — which was in fact an ad placed by BMW's new X1.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003632lYO56z71pjhSqmPo.png-w600)

More of the brand story was hidden inside the skin's description: "The perfect ride, betraying its own car family, becoming a duty that protects only the driver, only the person behind the wheel……"

According to Luan Na's figures, the skin has sold 180 million yuan so far.

### "Product upgrading isn't just about meeting needs — it's about creating desire."

When a traditional food's sales growth stalls, food companies might try "upgrading" it.

The "upgrade" here doesn't mean changing the recipe — it means associating the existing food with a new scenario and reaching new customers.

One example mentioned at the forum was Snickers. Snickers ads have featured the weakling Tang Monk, the frail Lin Daiyu, the heroine of a Korean drama, and other characters — but they were always aimed at a broad audience. To attract younger consumers, in April 2016 Snickers invited TFBOYS to shoot a series of ads called *The Exam-Season Hunger Demon Conquest*, associating the product with the gaokao college entrance exam; the ads ultimately notched over 100 million views.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003645YKnVhTPf3Az6ZNwB.jpg-w600)

Another category that may reinvent itself through "new consumption scenarios" is frozen treats. As Luan Na noted, Yili's Zhenxi ice cream was plugged on the web variety show *Please Take Care of My Refrigerator* in all sorts of ways, with guests treating the ice cream as an everyday dessert — an attempt to break the limitation that ice products only sell in summer.

In Taipei, beer brands built around the 24 solar terms have already appeared, likewise hoping to break the "beer = summer" mindset.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003800KVNUqZgtwlfHoeRu.jpg-w600)

### "Packaging used to be a cost to me — now it's a tool for communication."

"If we see packaging as a cost, we make it thinner, smaller and worse, to squeeze the cost down," Yu Jian quoted a client representative as telling him not long ago, in his talk. "Now more brands treat packaging as a tool of communication — to me it has become an investment."

How do you convey more information on packaging — even complete an interaction, like "my drink is safe" or "time to take your medicine"? People's pursuit of food safety has indirectly driven the rise of smart packaging design.

Ong Wongawa, Motorola's chief designer, mentioned in his talk a packaging case he completed in 2014. Nutrilinx, a supplement, counts automatically each time you pour out a tablet, and the paired phone app reminds you which vitamins to take that day.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704260038222tYQFuGml7cqsk3T.jpg-w600)

Benton N. Sauer, Asia vice president at LPK — the firm that designed Pringles' cylindrical can — shared an example of "Tmall's Little Black Box" in an interview with QDaily. The conceptual "virtual packaging" pushes different brands' new products to users based on their preferences — perhaps L'Oréal's new lotion, or Adidas's new sneakers. For Tmall, the so-called packaging has actually become a marketing platform.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003831RnGWaPNculiFkYTs.jpeg-w600)

### "Packaging will be like the fashion industry going forward."

Speaking about the future of packaging, Chen Peiqi, a partner at British design firm JKR, said: "Change is the only constant. Going forward it will be a world of Living Editions — just like the fashion industry, packaging will change frequently."

Brands are repackaging faster and faster, largely to keep up with e-commerce's rapid rhythm.

AB InBev, for instance, launched a limited-edition Year-of-the-Rooster gift box set on Tmall during Chinese New Year, hoping young people would buy it for their parents — "high looks" was directly adopted as the tagline for the limited product.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003850YvnuWeItwcjAl4mr.png-w600)

Another similar example: in December last year, Mengniu's Chunzhen yogurt rolled out a special e-commerce edition, with the brand hoping meme-style elements would attract young online shoppers.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426003858QbDefad71zJ8m2iN.jpeg-w600)

Compared with traditional sales channels, KA channels, closed channels and specialty-store channels, online sales channels are more fashionable, convenient and entertaining. Packaging design suited to e-commerce therefore generally follows the characteristics of online sales: first, the more change the better, the faster the updates the better.

Second, in the words of Qiu Zhuoqi, a project manager at LPK: "Put the special designs online — that way consumers can find them more easily and make a purchase decision very quickly."

From a packaging-design technique standpoint, e-commerce-exclusive packaging also differs from ordinary offline packaging. Changing e-commerce packaging helps improve warehouse operations and the customer experience, thereby growing online business. After Unilever, for example, switched to an e-commerce version of its pump head, product return and damage rates dropped significantly.

A similar thing happened with Perrier: when the sales channel moved online, they switched the glass bottle to a cheaper plastic bottle that is easier to ship.
