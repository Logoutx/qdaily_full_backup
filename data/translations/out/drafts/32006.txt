Although It's Called the "China International Furniture Fair," Judging by This Year's Show, It Remains a Long Way From "International"
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
If you want to be in the lifestyle business, you can't keep running an export-turned-domestic trade fair.
@@QD_BODY@@
If you want to be in the lifestyle business, you can't keep running an export-turned-domestic trade fair.

In its 22nd year, China's biggest furniture fair is trying to cram in far too many concepts.

On September 8, the "China International Furniture Fair" opened — except that this year the name is unusually long: after the regular part comes "Modern Shanghai, the Fashion & Lifestyle Expo." The organizer, Shanghai UBM Sinoexpo, has been trying to expand the fair horizontally from the furniture industry into the broader home-furnishing industry and lifestyle showcase, which is why it built this themed expo alongside the original furniture show.

Put more plainly, they want to display not just furniture but everything that could possibly appear in a home; not just the big pieces but all kinds of small ones.

For many furniture companies, this "China International Furniture Fair" essentially functions as a trade fair. You can tell from the setup: on day one the show is open only to trade visitors — so-called manufacturers, buyers, dealers, designers and media. Most of them come bearing orders, and the factories from Zhejiang, Guangdong and elsewhere get down to business. The core message of a show like this is: look, these are the styles we can make this year.

Against the backdrop of China as the "world's factory," such a fair inevitably takes on an "export-oriented, high-end for the domestic market" coloring. You see large numbers of near-identical pieces repeated across different exhibitors. A Hans Wegner circle chair, for instance, might appear at the fair in more than 20 colors and 10 materials, at remarkably low prices — much as it's displayed in Taobao shops. These exhibitors' displays don't resemble an exhibition so much as an exercise in calculating how to cram the most merchandise into a limited space.

Beyond that, wandering these booths feels a bit like wandering a Red Star Macalline store. Higher-end brands such as KUKA Home and "HC 28" also exhibit. After walking through a few zones you get a rough sense of what European, American and New Chinese styles each stand for. Compared with the export-oriented exhibitors, the big brands tend to pay attention to display, and their booths are often enclosed — they try to give you the feeling of a genuine model room.

Only from 2010 did China's home-furnishing industry begin to pay attention to original design, and UBM Sinoexpo set up the "DOD Exhibition (Design of Designers)" to showcase designers' work in one place. To a degree, it was the furniture fair that created visibility for the independent furniture brands that have grown up in recent years, bringing more independent design brands and independent designers to market. Brands including Bentu, Ziinlife, Fnji, Yiwai Design and Benlai Design usually save their new-product launches for the fair. And independent labels like Zhou Chenchen's Frank Chou Design Studio, or Ruyi — the personal brand of Yuan Yuan, former design director at QuMei Furniture — have both gone to exhibit at the Milan Furniture Fair, which carries a whiff of simply showing up to build brand recognition. Apart from products they custom-make for brands like HC28 and Weixi, neither of the two designers' independent labels currently has formal sales channels, online or off.

On top of all this, the 2016 fair expanded into a new area — the "Modern Shanghai, the Fashion & Lifestyle Expo" at the World Expo Exhibition & Convention Center. But if you're a well-traveled consumer, we'd advise against making the trip.

More than 300 home-accessory exhibitors fill halls N2, N5 and E7 of the Shanghai New International Expo Centre, but the quality is generally low — mostly small craft and accessory companies and workshops from Guangdong, Zhejiang and Jiangxi. Rather than showcasing a lifestyle, it has become a giant craft market.

Fenyate Art Co., from Shenzhen's Dafen Oil Painting Village, ran a 40%-off special during the fair, and Yihe Ceramics, which came all the way from Jingdezhen to exhibit, offered fair pricing at 12% off. Yihe's head told QDaily that Jingdezhen ceramic merchants rarely come here "to attend such a high-end fair," and that the purpose of making the trip was to find more soft-furnishing designers to connect with, or gift-customization channels, to broaden the brand's sales avenues.

As for the curator, UBM Sinoexpo describes this year's "from furniture to home" curatorial approach as "combining the strengths of the Paris home-decor fair held every January and the Milan Furniture Fair held every April." In the words of Zhong Beihong, its deputy general manager and director of the furniture business unit, "one becomes the Shanghai 'Milan Fair,' the other becomes the Shanghai 'Paris Fair.'"

Judging by the scene on the ground, such positioning is clearly concept-first. Perhaps out of anxiety about keeping up with the times, this year's fair also enthusiastically discussed concepts like makers and smart homes, and invited 10 designers to do themed presentations — all collectively dubbed the Home Plus lifestyle concept pavilion, which is plainly a notch above the dining-table-and-chair hall, the classical furniture hall, the office furniture hall and the outdoor furniture hall.

Because it tries to say too much, the fair as a whole ends up looking mediocre and messy this year. From a charitable angle, the curator is trying to express the concept of "lifestyle," but the exhibitors that can truly express it still cannot fill the 350,000-square-meter exhibition space. For the vast majority of them, their lives seem to have no style to speak of.

### Here is our rough summary of the 2016 China International Furniture Fair, in 10 points:

1. Exhibitor numbers doubled at the popular "DOD Exhibition" zone — more of them, but quality declined. Brands that surprised in past years, like Ziinlife, Bentu, Xishan Workshop and Yiwai Design, were almost nowhere to be seen; the newcomers were mostly craft, lighting and furniture brands with cookie-cutter designs, nothing new or distinctive;

2. Brands that used to be the heart of the "DOD Exhibition" — like Muzhi Workshop, Lost & Found and Fnji, which put care and originality into their booth setups — had their original three or four booths compressed down to one or two, small ones with no room to express themselves, at excessive booth density, losing their display character;

3. This year's flagship "Modern Shanghai, the Fashion & Lifestyle Expo" turned into a handicraft-products show, a shaky support for the so-called "whole-home" concept;

4. Apart from the "DOD Exhibition," the W4 brand design hall and the W5 original design hall, the overwhelming majority of halls remained steeped in the export-trade atmosphere, the exhibits mostly familiar-looking replica, imitation and lightly reworked versions, densely packed into the halls — the distinct feel of a factory outlet;

5. Among home brands focused on original design, wood is still the most-used material for the vast majority of brands, but the pure solid-wood or solid-and-panel wood furniture of the previous two years was less in evidence; what there is more of now is fusion with other materials — mirror stainless steel, resin, acrylic, fabric, cowhide and sheepskin, and metal;

6. The two themed zones represented by the Home Plus lifestyle concept pavilion and the China Style · New Chinese Classics Design Exhibition worked fairly well, mostly presented as home-living scenes; each curator had a different style, bringing some variety to the booth setups;

7. Big brands like QuMei, Pingze and U+ didn't break much new ground with this year's products compared with previous years — mostly small tweaks in material or form. Unless you specifically asked sales staff, it was hard to tell old from new; they mostly follow their own rhythm for releasing new products and barely reflect it in their booth displays;

8. The "makers zone," joined by teams and studios in smart home and home innovation, was a highlight. The zone's own construction was full of innovation: all the partitions were made using digitally manufactured furniture and architecture, divided roughly into a smart-home experience area, a digital-home experience area and a maker-space experience area, gathering a good deal of smart-home black tech;

9. The children's furniture category got attention. Not only was a children's furniture hall added this year, but in the DOD design zone two design brands — Suyuan and Shangxing — showed new lines devoted specifically to children's furniture;

10. The proportion of design events increased. Examples: the "China Home Design Conference — Golden Dot Talks," which invited Peter Zec, founder of Germany's Red Dot Design Award, to give a keynote; a collective pop-up by 12 Shanghai shops including The Mix Place, both MUSIC&BOUTIQUE, Zhiguan Coffee and Liangshe; the "Find China's Good Sofa · Find China's Good Fabric" selection series; and the "Heritage · Stand Up" Milan homecoming exhibition, among others.

### In addition, we've picked out a few worthwhile new pieces from independent brands, shown below

1. Ruyi

Buyers constantly reach out to touch Ruyi's sofas, because they look so rounded. Along with last year's two China Red Star Design Award–winning classics, "Guan Shi" (Viewing Stone) and "Qing Shan" (Light Fan), Ruyi's more interesting product this year is "Wan Shi" (Playing with Stone).

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909035321DE51nNdGMPTZ30aL.jpg-w600)

The body of the "Wan Shi" sofa is nothing particularly special; the highlight is the pebble cushions in various sizes. Their look imitates the shapes and tones of riverbank pebbles, and the range of sizes lets people of different builds sitting on the same sofa each freely choose the way that makes them most comfortable.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609090334172MOrLxJzd7bmyfB9.png-w600)

But designer Yuan Yuan has another purpose in calling the piece "Wan Shi": to let people use these cushions less solemnly, not merely as backrests but for carefree play with family and friends.

2. United Strangers

United Strangers is one of the more unconventional offerings among the exhibits. It mixes leather and metal to convey an industrial style: an industrial-style desk with deep-brown leather straps webbing one side of the drawers, or similar treatment applied to a bed's headboard. This style might strike people as "special" — and relative to "chic," it really only qualifies as "special" for now.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909033706uG5Ntl6VI1fZwdxJ.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909033727rqRwBAD6ei0uhKT1.jpg-w600)

3. Shangxing

Shangxing's focus this time was children's furniture. Among the exhibits, the piece that prompted countless buyers to pull out their phones and take pictures was a bed shaped like a biscuit. Designer Yu Qing says the bed was made specifically according to his own child's wishes. His little one had long been pestering him to design a biscuit-shaped piece of furniture, so this time he built this small bed out of ash wood.

Shangxing's on-site display also had a childlike playfulness: colorful pennants hanging on the walls, rabbit-ear cushioned mini-chairs on the floor, bedside tables with rounded corners — all of it made the zone feel lively.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909033749ILnShTac2iEDU10M.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909033811fXhJaF6tkHwjGE4q.jpg-w600)

4. Tiwu

Many of the lighting fixtures at the fair were built to be elaborate. Although the most common pairing is still glass and metal, compared with designs that wrap the bulb in layer upon layer of hollowed-out metal shades, Tiwu's simplification actually makes its products stand out. The suspension cord of its teardrop pendant lamp uses brass in place of the usual material, but the knot-like treatment above the shade softens the hardness of the metal.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909033848bg8yh3wor0Zna24T.jpg-w600)

5. FRANK CHOU DESIGN STUDIO

This is the personal brand of up-and-coming original designer Frank Chou, who emphasizes a modern, minimalist style. Every season he launches new products themed on a different material, not confining himself to any single material or element. This season's theme is wool felt. FRANK CHOU STUDIO built the chair frames in metal and paired them with wool-felt seat pads, conveying a neither-cold-nor-warm sense of distance.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609090727250oYC7MSihNyeUkTJ.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909072806wgDhS7VtMLJPyc8E.jpg-w600)

6. PINWU (Pinwu Liuxing)

QDaily has profiled this design team before; they specialize in modernizing design through traditional craft. At this year's China International Furniture Fair, PINWU's booth space was not large. It brought its few new products: a floor lamp made of bamboo paper, and a small table with a ceramic top.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909072253RBlMv5VxGC9j74HS.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909072303UbSu1vxNEKdhmlnG.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909072311sErtjfLYHxnlcd0g.jpg-w600)

Bamboo paper has the natural grain characteristic of its plant fibers. The shade of this floor lamp is entirely handmade and can be unfolded and smoothed flat; the wooden frame uses beech, and that part can be disassembled. Because it uses natural bamboo paper, every lamp's shade has a unique texture and natural deckled edges.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909072551pA0vdwNkZsrObCPI.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016090907292592sjc8fp4BOoiNzr.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160909072933U4vVID2uEtL3CrWy.jpg-w600)

High-hardness ceramic paired with beech gives this small table the coolness of ceramic and the warm tactility of beech. The ceramic's high hardness and easy-to-clean properties add to its practicality.
