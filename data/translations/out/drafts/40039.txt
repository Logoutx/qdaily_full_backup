In the 2000s, Taiwan's "idol dramas" flourished, but their limited, repetitive production quickly exhausted itself in Asia's far larger market for "young hearts."

Sixteen years later, it was "Shan Cai" who announced that *Meteor Garden* would be remade.

On April 11, Barbie Hsu posted on Weibo a photo of herself with the original producer, Angie Chai, along with an emotional pitch: "In a flash, *Meteor Garden* is already 16 years old! Shan Cai is the mother of two kids! Chatting with Sister Chai about the old days feels so good... She knows I'm an honest person, yet she still told me a secret — let her tell you all herself!" Chai reposted and replied, "...Yes! *Meteor Garden* is being remade!" adding the classic tagline from Shan Cai back in the day: "Shan Cai is the indestructible spirit of a weed."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170421210028lMbNoK7uFhVDzxiL.png-w600)

More detailed news soon followed: as producer of the new version, Chai had secured from Yoko Kamio, author of the original Japanese manga, the rights for a second screen adaptation. Compared with the old version's 60-minute episodes and 27-episode run, the new *Meteor Garden* would run 48 episodes, with total production costs as high as NT$720 million (about 160 million yuan).

Although the new cast has yet to be announced, the old leading lady and the producer's "nostalgia" pitch succeeded in drawing the attention of some longtime viewers. Within days, Barbie Hsu's Weibo post racked up more than 20,000 likes and over a thousand comments.

Exactly 16 years — April 12, 2001, was the day the Taiwanese idol drama *Meteor Garden* premiered. In fact, like many other commercially successful pop stories, the manga was subsequently remade in Japan, South Korea, and by Hunan TV, in 2005, 2009, and 2009 respectively. But for many mainland viewers, the version that can be filed under "collective memories of youth" is, and remains, the Taiwanese one.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170421210141M9fUeJPKmiW3yGFk.jpg-w600)

Sixteen years on, if you're starved for idol dramas, *Meteor Garden* is still worth recommending

On Douban Movie, there are 145 long "drama reviews" of *Meteor Garden*, 74 of them published after 2010. Most of the commenters' posts are commemorations of "the youth I lived through" together with Taiwanese idol dramas.

*Meteor Garden* made its impact on the mainland in the year after its Taiwanese broadcast, airing on provincial and municipal TV stations across the mainland from early February 2002 and quickly causing a sensation. For many viewers at the time, it had the significance of an "initiation into idol dramas." A user named "Die Wu Bing Qing" wrote: "The first Taiwanese idol drama; the first time I understood what being handsome meant, and what fangirling meant."

Today, "female-oriented" Mary Sue idol dramas have become the most common genre in mainland-produced TV. But on the barren mainland screen of those years, young viewers still had very limited choices. Throughout the 1990s, the most influential romance genre remained the period-style melodramas of Qiong Yao, which had already been popular for twenty or thirty years.

The craze for *Meteor Garden* brought a fresh shock — and the censors' reaction now serves as proof of its "sensational" impact. Only a few days after the drama began airing, on March 8, 2002, the State Administration of Radio, Film, and Television (SARFT) ordered *Meteor Garden* suspended immediately, on the grounds that it was "liable to mislead young people."

Setting aside the gimmick of the "ban," *Meteor Garden* today retains a respectable 8.1 rating. As an adaptation, its success was built on the success of the original manga. Long before the live-action version, the story's setup had already been tested by popularity: Yoko Kamio serialized *Hana Yori Dango* (《花样男子》) in the shojo manga magazine *Margaret* from 1992 to 2004 (the Taiwanese Tong Li Publishing edition used the title 《流星花园》). According to Wikipedia's "List of best-selling manga," the 37 volumes of *Meteor Garden* had sold 60 million copies as of 2014, ranking first among Japanese shojo manga.

In fact, long before "web fiction" took off after 2000, Japanese anime and manga had been exported overseas since the 1970s, and the popular stories in cartoonists' hands brimmed with creativity. Shojo manga magazines including *Margaret* and *LaLa*, aimed at girls aged 6 to 25, produced the delicate feelings and beautiful romances that interested them and provided rich inspiration for film and TV — they were the first to use all the themes now in vogue: "time travel," "gender swapping," and the category of its own, "danmei." And of course, they also included what *Meteor Garden* tells: the most hackneyed, yet also the most enduring, story of the "campus Cinderella."

The story takes place in a campus society with a rigid class hierarchy. A girl from an ordinary family, Shan Cai, originally has the plain life that belongs to her, but by accident she arrives at the elite Yingde Academy, "a school founded to cultivate outstanding offspring." Here she meets boys of her own age from "upper society" — four of them: Daoming Si, Huaze Lei, Ximen, and Meizuo. They are the heirs of "the four great families," known around school as "F4" (Flower Four, the flower quartet), and they enjoy every privilege.

At first, while enduring constant humiliation, Shan Cai keeps her two braids and tries to stay healthy and lively, living up to the "indestructible spirit of a weed." But soon the "Cinderella" arc turns from suppression to triumph. Shan Cai nurses a secret crush on the "prince-like, mysterious" Huaze Lei while playing a cat-and-mouse game of bullying and being bullied (noticing and being noticed) with the "leader of the F4," Daoming Si. Yoko Kamio had originally written Huaze Lei as the male lead, but as Daoming Si's popularity surged over the course of the serialization, she ultimately had Shan Cai end up with him — letting them one day suddenly realize that, well, maybe he/she isn't so annoying after all... wait, how is he/she even a little cute?

QDaily previously examined why Jane Austen's [*Pride and Prejudice*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/37567.html), written by an Englishwoman 200 years ago, remains popular in modern society: the plot is dramatic and full of "satisfying moments," the characters are vivid and varied, and the romance progresses beautifully, like a fairy tale, without floating free of female readers' real-life desires — and if you compare, you'll find that *Meteor Garden* checks all of these boxes. In fact, Shan Cai and Daoming Si are like a modern teenage version of Elizabeth and Darcy: Shan Cai holds the prejudice (self-respect), Daoming Si the pride (immaturity), and the foundation of the love that spans rich and poor is a shared set of values (simplistically speaking, "truth, goodness, and beauty").

Despite the "ban," *Meteor Garden* stayed hot through pirated discs. "F4" and *Meteor Garden* topped Baidu's 2002 year-end "Top Ten Hot Keywords" in the "celebrity" and "TV series" categories respectively.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170421191438Weph3wBcXJsLkPnr.jpg-w600)

Taiwanese idol dramas of the 2000s

After *Meteor Garden*'s success, the producer who steered it earned a flamboyant nickname — one she has since written into her Weibo bio — "the mother of Taiwanese idol dramas." The compliment does reflect a fact: in Taiwan, Chai was a pioneer of the "idol drama" genre.

The drama was shot and aired episode by episode on Chinese Television System (CTS) in 2001. Initially nobody believed in it, and it was slotted into a late-night time period. The TV station gave it a budget of just 150,000 yuan per episode; the sports cars were borrowed by Chai from friends, and the actors wore T-shirts and track pants bought from wholesale markets, with no connection to fashion whatsoever. It had no stars: at the time Barbie Hsu was known only as a "comedy" TV host, and the "F4" were total newcomers. (Fans love to retell [Chai's casting process](http://www.chinanews.com/yl/2011/04-02/2949917_2.shtml): "I met Ken Chu at a barbecue shop; Vanness Wu was spotted by a company colleague at the gym; Zai Zai, Vic Chou, came to audition with a friend; and when Jerry Yan was filming *Spicy Teacher* (《麻辣鲜师》), I already felt this boy was special.")

But after *Meteor Garden* aired, it set a new Taiwan TV ratings record with an average rating of 6.99, a record that held until 2006, and it also established "idol drama" as a genre in Taiwan's TV industry.

[Wang Weizhong](http://www.books.com.tw/products/R030038018) once explained the logic of *Meteor Garden*: "(Chai) picked the thing she felt most strongly about to work on at exactly the right moment, and that's why she succeeded."

Although Chai had no film or TV production experience, she knew the TV market well. Born in 1962, she entered Fulon Production Company (the predecessor of Golden Star Entertainment, home to *Kangsi Coming* (《康熙来了》) producer Wang Weizhong) in the 1980s to work in variety-show production, and by the 1990s she had risen to general manager.

In 2001, after the talk show *Love Lectures* (《恋爱讲义》) she launched on Formosa TV (FTV) failed in the ratings, she turned to trying drama, adapting her beloved *Meteor Garden* into a romantic idol drama.

In fact, around 2000 the Taiwanese TV industry desperately needed a genre turning point. Taiwanese TV drama had gone through the boom years of the 1980s and 1990s with Qiong Yao, Jin Yong, and other traditional genres; but in the 1990s, as the economic environment changed — especially after the 1997 Asian financial crisis hurt exports —

On the other hand, after Taiwan liberalized "cable TV licenses" in the 1990s, the number of stations jumped from just three with party backgrounds to more than a hundred. Competition among stations turned fierce, and they also had to face a shortage of production resources.

FTV, a fast-growing upstart station, began producing long-running "homeland dramas" (*Stars Know My Heart* (《星星知我心》), *Wealth in Heaven* (《富贵在天》), *Life Between Heaven and Earth* (《人生天地间》)) to fight for the local adult-viewer market. Other stations bought Japanese and Korean dramas in large quantities, which battered the existing market.

Feng Jiarui, another producer of *Meteor Garden*, recalled the environment at the time: "Taiwanese TV drama was already declining... the road was getting narrower and narrower, gradually unable to satisfy audience demand, and was ultimately defeated by Japanese dramas with their polished production and brisk pacing. Taiwanese TV stations had to seek change if they wanted any chance of development."

In fact, the genre label "idol drama" originated in Japan's "trendy dramas" (トレンディドラマ, trendy drama) — those "fashionable romance dramas" aimed at young viewers in the late 1980s to early 1990s, which the internet has since crowned classics: 1991's *Tokyo Love Story*, adapted from a manga by Hinako Ashihara (the breakthrough work of the screenwriter behind this year's hit *Quartet*); 1996's *Long Vacation*, in which Takuya Kimura and Tomoko Yamaguchi ran in a wedding dress; and 1996's *Mischievous Kiss* (《一吻定情》), which established Takashi Kashiwabara as the "end-of-century beautiful boy."

![The 1996 Japanese TV drama *Mischievous Kiss*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170421191458yfp4JnZWKojwz3ea.jpg-w600)

And *Meteor Garden*, appearing as an imitator, demonstrated a workable path. It opened overseas sales for Taiwanese serial dramas: as of 2005, the drama aired in 16 countries and regions around the world. It topped the ratings in South Korea, Indonesia, the Philippines, and Thailand; more than 15 Japanese TV stations broadcast it, and it also spawned a Japanese remake.

[Chai](http://www.qikan.com/article/4038D015-61F9-4691-B4EE-97F1752E032B) said her goal was simply "young people": "Most dramas on the mainland and in Taiwan were period costume pieces. So I thought at the time, if we made a drama for young people, someone should watch it, because young people and the older generation don't see things the same way. There were no dramas for young people back then, hence no young actors, let alone young handsome actors."

Off-screen as on, "F4" became a red-hot boy band. Although *Meteor Garden*'s revenue never reached the production team and the actors, it launched an "idol" career: in August 2001, Sony Music released F4's debut album *Meteor Rain* (recording reportedly took only 11 days), which sold 4 million copies across Asia in its first year. In 2002 and 2003, F4 held a four-month "world tour," with 19 concerts in 12 cities. As of 2005, Chai estimated at least 500 million people had seen F4 perform.

In 2001, Chai founded her own idol-drama company, Comic Ritz, which produced nearly 30 idol dramas between 2001 and 2010, including *Peach Girl*, *Mars*, *Devil Beside You*, and *Corner with Love*, earning Golden Bell Awards recognition for people like Show Lo and Rainie Yang.

Feng Jiarui split from Chai and restructured as Comic International in 2003, producing *The Rose*, *It Started with a Kiss*, the *KO One* series, and *Hana Kimi*. Following the F4 model even more closely, he launched another Taiwanese boy band, Fahrenheit (Wu Chun, Calvin Chen, Jiro Wang, and Aaron Yan).

Stations including Sanlih and GTV set up dedicated idol-drama time slots. From 2002 onward, roughly 20 idol dramas were produced each year, totaling more than 200 by 2010. Ethan Juan, Joe Chen, Joe Cheng, Ariel Lin... Taiwan's stars all came out of idol dramas.

However, almost as early as before 2005, Taiwanese idol dramas quickly fell into the trap of "formula" in terms of content and reputation. Most stories came from Japanese manga, and cost constraints kept them locked within the modern urban mold. It wasn't until 2005 that GTV's *KO One* series attempted to combine idol drama with fantasy — but the aim was to expand the audience below elementary-school age. After 2008, to win new viewers, the hottest idol dramas — *My Queen*, *Fated to Love You*, *Autumn's Concerto*, *Black & White*, and *In Time with You* — all tried a more realistic style.

Idol dramas, as the last "solution" propping up the Taiwanese TV industry, saw their momentum fade around 2010, along with music, variety shows, and the idol industry as a whole, as the mainland market rose.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170421210243IwWiAGO7Zt9Qbo8M.jpg-w600)

The mainland's counterattack

At the 47th Golden Bell Awards in 2012, *In Time with You*, starring Ariel Lin and Bolin Chen, took seven major awards, becoming the idol drama with the most "artistic recognition."

But also in 2012, Chai announced to the media that she was dissolving her idol-drama company, declaring that her future focus would be film and talent management.

Taiwanese idol dramas were on the way down. Lo Shih-hung, a professor in the Department of Communication at National Chung Cheng University, commented: "The situation in which Taiwanese dramas held the advantage gradually changed. The mainland's Taiwan-drama fever slowly ebbed, while mainland dramas caught up quickly, and the two began to show a seesaw pattern, even forming a kind of 'counterattack on Taiwan.'"

According to import-approval data published by SARFT, over the ten years from 2004 to 2013, a total of 163 Taiwanese dramas were imported, an average of 16.3 per year, accounting for 19.7% of all imported dramas. Mainland imports of Taiwanese dramas peaked in 2009 at 33 dramas and 695 episodes, but declined steadily thereafter, down to just 3 dramas by 2013.

By contrast, mainland TV dramas began to flow back into Taiwan around 2000 and grew steadily after 2010. Imported dramas now account for more than half of all TV dramas in Taiwan. In 2011, Taiwan imported 151 dramas from the mainland — far more than the 49 locally produced that year. After 2013, *The Legend of Mi Yue* and *Nirvana in Fire* set off successive ratings booms.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170421205708wVYscOQMzUgWZt9n.jpg-w600)

Behind the rise of the mainland idol-drama market, video websites had replaced traditional TV stations as the main buyers and investors of TV dramas, and young viewers' tastes received unprecedented attention. Meanwhile, by this point mainland web novels had also matured over several years on Qidian and Jinjiang, providing a basis for adaptation. Starting with Hunan TV's 2011 drama *Scarlet Heart*, the film and TV industry saw the appeal of "Mary Sue IPs." Production teams that had focused on historical dramas and "serious dramas" joined in too. Zheng Xiaolong's *Empresses in the Palace*, directed in 2012, was re-aired again and again on mainland satellite TV, and was imported by Taiwan's CTS in 2013, peaking above a rating of 3 and setting a new multi-year record for the 8 p.m. time slot.

The most fundamental force behind the reversal in cross-strait cultural-export numbers is Taiwan's economic malaise. Between 1951 and 1988, Taiwan's average annual economic growth rate was 9.57%; from 1988 to 2008 it was 6.1%; and from 2008 to 2015 it fell to 2.8%.

Lo Shih-hung commented: "Since 2000, the rising mainland TV drama industry has exerted a powerful magnetic pull, drawing many veteran Taiwanese screenwriters and actors to develop their careers on the mainland, and their mainland works flow back to Taiwan without any obstacles. Meanwhile, with Taiwan's economy in a prolonged slump, a weak advertising market, many parties dividing the pie, and government drama subsidies amounting to little more than a drop in the bucket, a vicious cycle has formed in which mainland dramas keep soaring while Taiwanese dramas keep sinking."

According to Taiwan's Directorate-General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics, 724,000 Taiwanese worked overseas in 2015, nearly 60% of them on the Chinese mainland. Experienced Taiwanese film and TV creators "went north" in search of bigger opportunities — "cross the strait, double your salary."

This was precisely Chai's public reason for leaving idol dramas: "Because so much of Taiwan's production talent has drained away, it's very hard to find screenwriters, directors, and actors."

In fact, Chai herself was one of those who "went north." She entered film first. The coming-of-age film *You Are the Apple of My Eye*, produced and supervised by Chai and directed by Giddens Ko, became Taiwan's highest-grossing film of 2011, and earned 76.5 million yuan at the mainland box office when released there in 2012.

In 2013, Chai became one of the supervisors and investors of Guo Jingming's coming-of-age film series *Tiny Times*. Despite the controversy over the series as trashy "money-worshipping" films (this time the rejection came directly from audiences), the four films rode the coming-of-age film wave to a combined box office of more than 1.8 billion yuan.

After F4, Chai's new idols focused on the mainland market. Kai Ko, the star of *You Are the Apple of My Eye* and *Tiny Times*, had endorsement deals with 19 brands before the 2014 "drug" incident made him vanish from public view. And in 2015, *Our Times*, a coming-of-age film by Chen Yushan (a former Sanlih TV supervisor and producer of *Fated to Love You*), took in more than 300 million yuan at the box office, making it the highest-grossing Taiwanese film in the mainland market. Its leading lady, Vivian Sung, is now the idol Chai is pushing hardest.

*Proud of Love*, a two-season web drama supervised by Chai and starring Vivian Sung, launched on Youku in September 2016 and January 2017 respectively. Adapted from the novel of the same name by Jinjiang Literature site author Sui Houzhu, and jointly produced by Youku Tudou and Mango Entertainment, it tells a story in which the male and female leads "swap genders."

Also supervised by Chai and starring Vivian Sung, the film *Three-Good Underachiever* has finished shooting. Adapted from the web novel *Peking University Underachiever* (《北大差生》) by "Po Naodai," its main investor is Alibaba Pictures.

The mainland market first brought Chai production budgets. Taiwanese dramas average about NT$2.5 million (roughly 560,000 yuan) per episode, while mainland dramas average more than 1 million yuan per episode, and hit costume dramas exceed 10 million yuan per episode.

And the new *Meteor Garden*'s budget of 160 million yuan includes not-yet-announced "mainland investors." In that sense, it is no longer a Taiwanese idol drama either.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2017042120573607peB8tUhWkVSXgn.jpg-w600)

But is the new *Meteor Garden* still worth looking forward to?

Under the Weibo posts in which Chai and Barbie Hsu announced the remake, even as netizens indulged in nostalgia, they also voiced fears of a "ruined classic."

Can the story of *Meteor Garden* still work? Judged on the story alone, its hackneyed elements have not entirely gone out of date. The 2014 Korean drama *The Heirs*, starring Park Shin-hye and Lee Min-ho, depicted campus hierarchy even more superficially and unabashedly, yet still became one of that year's hottest Korean dramas on the strength of its "campus Cinderella" outline.

But from the producer's perspective, the answer is not necessarily optimistic — the *Meteor Garden* of 16 years ago was Chai's first idol drama, and also the best TV drama she ever made.

Still, for Chai herself, that may not be a reason not to remake it. As for the older viewers, perhaps they don't even hope for a new version that better satisfies their "young hearts" — they have also passed the age of caring most. But 16 years on, romance remains the most popular subject matter, and a new wave of viewers has arrived.

On Weibo, Chai still says "a brand-new *Meteor Garden* that belongs to your youth," but what she truly needs to please is, indeed, brand-new youth.
