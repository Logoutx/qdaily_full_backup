HBO's new series *My Brilliant Friend* premieres on November 18. Adapted from the novel of the same name by the Italian writer Elena Ferrante, it is the first installment of the wildly popular "Neapolitan Novels" around the world.

Calling it "highly anticipated" would hardly be an overstatement. When the first two episodes premiered at the Venice Film Festival in September, the audience in attendance gave the series a ten-minute standing ovation. [*Variety*](https://variety.com/2018/tv/reviews/my-brilliant-friend-hbo-1202921018/) argued that it overcame the two hardest problems in adapting this novel: staying faithful to the original, and bringing to the screen the protagonist's inner emotional life, the most prominent feature of the source material. "It retains some of the literary qualities of the book and transfers the rest to the screen in a moving, unforgettable way."

The popularity of the original is, of course, the main reason the series has drawn such attention. According to [the *Wall Street Journal*](https://www.wsj.com/articles/elena-ferrantes-my-brilliant-friend-comes-to-hbo-1541603256), the "Neapolitan Novels" have sold 10.2 million copies worldwide, more than a third of them in English. The author's fans have whipped up "Ferrante Fever" — in 2017 there was even a [documentary](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt6136430/) dedicated to the phenomenon, featuring Michelle Obama and Pulitzer Prize winner Elizabeth Strout, among others. Hillary Clinton appears in the film saying: "You read the first one, and you cannot stop reading and thinking."

The novel's protagonist Elena (Lenù) is a writer, and her story unfolds in postwar Italy, a society in turmoil. She grows up as a child in the chaotic, squalid neighborhoods of Naples, where she has known her childhood friend, the other female lead Lila, for years. After Lila vanishes without warning at the age of 66, Elena writes down, in the first person, the story of what they lived through and their tangled bond with the city of Naples: from studying together as children to falling in love with the same man as adults, and then raising children together. It is indeed a story of female friendship, and what makes it special is that the emotion is complex, and Elena's attachment to it — and fear of it — runs exceptionally deep: she believes the drive and the pain of her later life are all built on the pressure, joy, and hatred Lila brought her.

This teasing-apart of complex emotion, combined with the first-person narration, gives readers an "immersive" experience. [The *New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/10/31/magazine/elena-ferrante-hbo-my-brilliant-friend.html) once gave an example: when Elena recalls the past, she seems to have no intention of "clarifying anything," instead sketching her memories in a deliberately hazy way, often resorting to utterly uncertain words like "perhaps" and "who knows." This makes Elena — both within the story and for the reader — an uneasy, "easily invaded" woman who, like many women, "has the desire to express herself but cannot truly do so." In the novel she is thus haunted by contradiction and control, and it is precisely this quality that has drawn many readers in through deep recognition.

The "Neapolitan Novels" introduced readers to Elena Ferrante — the author of these novels, who, like her protagonist, is named Elena. But this is only the writer's pen name; her identity, real name, and even gender remain a mystery to this day. Since publishing her first novel in 1992, Ferrante has attended no book launches and given no in-person interviews, accepting only written ones. This anonymity seems to have become part of "Ferrante Fever." In 2016, an investigative journalist, Claudio Gatti, wrote in [the *New York Review of Books*](https://www.nybooks.com/daily/2016/10/02/elena-ferrante-an-answer/) that he believed Ferrante was herself a literary translator whose real name was Anita Raja. His move was condemned by many Ferrante fans. [The BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/magazine-37556183) commented at the time: "Ferrante's anonymity gives her fans an indefinable thrill — it grants them the precious space to imagine her."

![Poster for the documentary *Ferrante Fever*. Image from [IMDB](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt6136430/).](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181114204716bU0gHzYnTVjtrNxL.jpg-w600)

Now that Ferrante's *My Brilliant Friend* has been brought to the screen, how does she communicate with the director?

The answer holds little surprise: they communicate through formal emails and the publisher's WhatsApp. Despite the intense discussion and instruction, Ferrante still uses the formal address "lei" (the polite "you") rather than the informal "tu" in their exchanges, "as if it were the 19th century," the series' director Saverio Costanzo said in an interview with [the *Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/nov/11/on-the-set-of-elena-ferrante-my-brilliant-friend-tv-adaptation). "I wouldn't dare say Elena Ferrante and I are friends — I'm just her working partner... Whether she's a she or a he, I don't know."

The 43-year-old Italian director Costanzo first approached Ferrante back in 2007, trying to adapt her 2006 novella *The Lost Daughter* into a film, but the project went nowhere. Over the ensuing decade, Costanzo grew into one of Italy's most promising young directors. Several of his films feature sensitive female protagonists who commit baffling acts of destruction, and intricate psychological drama — for example *Hungry Hearts*, which entered the main competition at the Venice Film Festival in 2014, in which a mother who initially wants to protect her child from harm ends up gradually starving the child to death.

Perhaps because of this similarity in perspective and approach, in 2016 he unexpectedly received a phone call informing him that Ferrante had chosen him to direct the television adaptation of *My Brilliant Friend*. His first reaction was ["feeling daunted"](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/10/31/magazine/elena-ferrante-hbo-my-brilliant-friend.html), because adapting such a bestselling novel into a series is no easy task: he had witnessed the scale of "Ferrante Fever," and had also directed *The Solitude of Prime Numbers*, adapted from a bestseller, where he received nitpicking reviews from the novel's fans. In the end, though, he accepted the assignment. "She (Ferrante) was reaching her hand out to me at that moment, saying to me: 'I've decided (to create this story with you) — why can't you?'" he recalled to [the *New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/10/31/magazine/elena-ferrante-hbo-my-brilliant-friend.html) while making this decision, grabbing the reporter in front of him with one hand and reaching into the air with the other, as if Ferrante were right there.

![Italian director Saverio Costanzo. Image from [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Saverio_Costanzo.jpg).](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201811142051551rmFRuS34ydZJsOI.jpg-w600)

Ferrante has formally become the "ghost" in his life: she never appears, yet she is extremely strict about every detail of the adaptation. In an interview with [the *Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/nov/11/on-the-set-of-elena-ferrante-my-brilliant-friend-tv-adaptation), Costanzo said: "Even when she's exchanging emails with you, what she pursues is very clear. So if you feel a little weak, she won't soften in the slightest because of it — she's just... commanding and demanding"; "It's really a bit like working with a ghost, a kind of nightmare. I'll tell you, it's especially terrifying, because you start dreaming at night, dreaming of faceless things, things with no substance at all."

Yet Costanzo has always believed Ferrante's suggestions are effective, and that the original work itself is rich enough to be worth poring over and respecting. A reporter for [the *New York Times*](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/10/31/magazine/elena-ferrante-hbo-my-brilliant-friend.html) described Costanzo this way: "When he speaks of Ferrante, the deference in his tone is something you rarely see from a director speaking about the original author of his screenplay." Despite being driven to nightmares, he told [the *Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/nov/11/on-the-set-of-elena-ferrante-my-brilliant-friend-tv-adaptation) that he doesn't care what kind of person Ferrante actually is — or rather, he doesn't feel he needs to know her in order to understand her work: the director believes Ferrante's descriptions in the book are extremely true to life, and fit the drama's characters and plot perfectly; "once moved to the screen, they bloom like flowers without much effort." He feels he can experience the emotions and psychological states described in the book — though it tells the story of two girls, it contains rage, revenge, and love: "This story belongs to everyone... I can see myself in the shadows of Elena and Lila."

He said Ferrante has already watched the series and pronounced herself "very satisfied" — he repeated those three words to the *Guardian* reporter several times, ease and disbelief mingling in his voice. ["Whenever director Costanzo speaks of Ferrante, the author of the original novel, his dark eyes look upward, as if searching for her in the cracks of the ceiling, or in some parallel plane above the hotel attic."](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/10/31/magazine/elena-ferrante-hbo-my-brilliant-friend.html)

![On the set of *My Brilliant Friend*. Image from [HBO](https://www.hbo.com/my-brilliant-friend).](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181114205804JDIobVsnYZu08kGa.jpg-w600)

This *My Brilliant Friend* has eight episodes in total, and is HBO's first original non-English-language series — in addition to Italian, it features the Naples dialect of the 1950s. The filming location was set in the city of Caserta, a 45-minute drive from downtown Naples; the site covers more than two hectares and is one of the largest shooting locations in Europe. The area used to be an old glass factory, abandoned since the 1980s, and over the past year it was transformed into a residential neighborhood: 14 dwellings, a church, and a tunnel were restored, and the rooms' furnishings, doors, and windows are all postwar-era pieces matching the period in which the story takes place.

Filming took eight months, with more than 9,000 people auditioning. In the first two episodes, the protagonists Elena and Lila are in their childhood, and later each grows up. The childhood leads are played by 11-year-old Elisa Del Genio and 12-year-old Ludovica Nasti respectively, while the teenage versions are played by 16-year-old Margherita Mazzuccoa and 15-year-old Gaia Girace. None of the four had almost any acting experience before.

Del Genio, who plays Elena as a child, is from Naples and of mixed Italian and Norwegian descent. She originally came to accompany her brother to the audition; her brother was not selected, but she was unexpectedly chosen. Nasti, who plays Lila as a child, is the livelier one. She [told](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/nov/11/on-the-set-of-elena-ferrante-my-brilliant-friend-tv-adaptation) the *Guardian* reporter that her favorite scenes are throwing stones at the boys, and the segment where she quarrels with her father and he throws her out the window. She herself is a child model, likes playing soccer, and is called "the boss" by her colleagues on set.

According to [the *Guardian*](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2018/nov/11/on-the-set-of-elena-ferrante-my-brilliant-friend-tv-adaptation), the actress playing Lila as a teenager is frailer and speaks softly, looking somewhat mysterious. "I fell in love with my character from the very first scene I shot... I love her wit, her perceptiveness. But she's not easy to play, because she's very complex and her thoughts shift suddenly. Only by playing her could I understand her. She gives off a very powerful feeling — perhaps after reading the novel you'd think she's a demon, but in fact she has hidden vulnerability." This also shaped her understanding of Lila and Elena's relationship: "The bond between them is like a mystery; they complete each other. Unlike Lila, Elena appears fragile but is hard inside. There is always distance between them, yet they keep getting closer, again and again."

Earlier this year, a Brazilian tourist traveled to the island of Ischia near Naples, hoping to find the scenes from the novel there — perhaps where Elena spent a summer vacation reading and swimming. One evening at dinner, she unexpectedly ran into the *My Brilliant Friend* crew, mid-shoot — "Elena," "Lila," and the "Sarratore family" from the novel were eating right in front of her. Director Saverio Costanzo found the scene amusing, because the Brazilian tourist told them that many people travel from South America to Naples to find the scenes Ferrante describes in the novels. In fact, "Ferrante trail" tours of all sizes have sprung up in Naples, promising to show visitors an authentic Naples.

What Costanzo wants to do is precisely to "present an authentic Naples" — "We don't hide any aspect of Naples; we're not making postcards." Violence and organized crime occur at every stage of the protagonists' growth, and the city scenes of the 1950s and 1960s could even be described as "decayed" and "oppressive." But these scenes are not the point Costanzo — or Ferrante — wants to make. What they care about is the relationship between the protagonists' inner growth and these settings, and the responses the girls make to their environment.

As Elena and Lila discuss in the novel: "Without love, not only people's lives become dreary, but the life of a city as well."
