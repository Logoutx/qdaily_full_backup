It sparked fast food's "go large" (supersize) craze, became a business-culture symbol, and — because of its association with obesity — turned into a social problem.

Few products enjoy as much acclaim and as much infamy as the Big Mac: seen as a symbol of America, even part of Western culture, yet also accused of being the culprit behind human obesity.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180224121229NlJoPL8xSQ6IsC2E.jpg-w600)

In the 50 years since its birth, the Big Mac has undoubtedly been one of the best-selling burgers in the world — Americans eat 550 million of them every year, and global sales run at roughly 1 billion. It is probably also the only fast-food product with a museum of its own.

Three layers of bun, two beef patties, lettuce, pickles, onions and a special Thousand Island-style sauce… these days you wouldn't find anything remarkable about eating such a burger, since you can buy one at any of McDonald's more than 30,000 outlets worldwide.

It is precisely because of this unmatched global reach, combined with a high degree of standardization, that *The Economist* invented the Big Mac index to gauge how various currencies are valued against the dollar. In the English-speaking world the index even spawned the term "Burgernomics." In this way, the Big Mac became entangled with a subject as grand as the world economy.

As McDonald's most core product, the Big Mac is often regarded as a synonym for McDonald's itself. It sparked fast food's "go large" (supersize) craze, changed the rules of the entire industry, became a business-culture symbol, and — because of its association with obesity — turned into a social problem.

*The Economist* recently released the 33rd edition of the Big Mac index, but the Big Mac itself no longer holds the appeal it once had for consumers; young people even consider it a boring burger. Still, its enduring vitality, and its influence on — and transformation of — human food culture, are worth examining.

### "The Big Mac came about because we needed to offer a bigger sandwich."

In 1967, Jim Delligatti (who died in 2016), a McDonald's franchisee, invented the Big Mac.

![Image: McDonald's China](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180224120927ImjNlY6EfKdG0iwV.jpg-w600)

At first McDonald's was not enthusiastic about the new burger; the company was content with the success of its cheeseburgers and fries. After all, before the Big Mac was born, McDonald's had already expanded rapidly across the United States on the strength of standardized production and franchising: in the eighth year after Ray Kroc officially took over, it had sold 1 billion burgers, and by 1965 there were already more than 700 McDonald's restaurants in the US.

What's more, McDonald's management believed [Delligatti's Big Mac was overpriced at 45 cents](http://www.nytimes.com/2016/11/30/business/michael-james-delligatti-creator-of-the-big-mac-dies-at-98.html) — McDonald's mainstream burgers cost only 18 cents at the time — and worried this would scare customers away.

Thanks to Jim Delligatti's insistence, headquarters agreed to let him test-market it. Soon, Delligatti's stores had the highest gross margins of any McDonald's franchisee at the time.

In 1968, the Big Mac officially went on sale at McDonald's restaurants across the United States.

Although McDonald's pioneered many of the fast-food industry's model innovations (especially strict standardization), the Big Mac was actually a market-follower product.

"The Big Mac came about because we needed to offer a bigger sandwich to compete with the various offerings of 'Burger King' (commonly translated as Burger King) and the franchise stores," he wrote in the autobiography of [Ray Kroc](https://www.google.com.sg/search?q=Ray+Kroc&stick=H4sIAAAAAAAAAOPgE-LSz9U3MMu2SM42UuIEsQ3zTFLKtGSyk630k_Lzs_XLizJLSlLz4svzi7KtEktLMvKLAJa2BSs3AAAA&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwi4rOnIm7fZAhXHpJQKHUBpBwgQmxMIlAEoATAP).

It is no longer possible to determine who first hit on the idea that "bigger sandwiches sell better." What is certain is that Burger King was one of the earliest to put it into practice. In 1958, Burger King's Jim McLamore noticed that competitors' bigger burgers were selling well. After improving the patty-grilling machine, the bigger (and slightly pricier, at 37 cents) Whopper was born. This big burger helped Burger King, then on the verge of death, grow rapidly in the 1960s. For a decade, Burger King had virtually no rival in the big-burger category. And the Whopper meant to Burger King what the Big Mac meant to McDonald's.

The Big Mac arrived a decade later, but thanks to McDonald's greater scale advantages and its fierce "advertising blitz" ([we covered this episode in our report on the ad wars between Burger King and McDonald's](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/33222.html)), it quickly became a best-seller. Within a year of launch, the Big Mac was contributing 19% of McDonald's total sales.

### The bigger the food, the better: the Big Mac changed the "rules of the game" in fast food

With its extra layer of patty, bun and cheese, the Big Mac's original ad copy always carried a line like this: this is McDonald's new sandwich, designed for appetites that are out of the ordinary.

The product changed the "rules of the game" in the fast-food industry: it opened the era of bigger-is-better food (or, more calories is better). As sociologist George Ritzer wrote in his book *The McDonaldization of Society*: McDonald's fast-food outlets have always emphasized that bigger products are better, and many fast-food chains share a "bigger is better" mentality.

In McDonald's early days, however, its burgers, fries and Coke all came in only one size. From the 1950s onward, especially after big burgers became best-sellers, food portions began to be graded: the original serving of fries — about 250 calories' worth — is now the large size of small/medium/large fries; in the 1950s, McDonald's only burger patty weighed 1.6 ounces. Today, a patty of that size is sold only in the children's Happy Meal.

Former US Surgeon General David Satcher said: "They (fast food) have supersized everything. When you walk into a fast-food restaurant, the staff are trained to push the larger sizes on you."

![A 1971 Big Mac meal advertisement](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180224115955lYVtAb7rNKG0oIz3.jpg-w600)

The craze for bigger food happened to coincide with changes in American society. After World War II, the US food industry developed rapidly; more women left the home for the workforce, household disposable income rose, and the time women spent on housework shrank — all of which created favorable conditions for the fast-food industry.

A host of fast-food brands — Pizza Hut, KFC, Domino's, Wendy's — were founded from the postwar years through the 1970s. Offering larger portions that looked like better value at relatively low prices was not only an advantage fast food held over traditional dining; from the very beginning it also became one of the key strategies in competition among fast-food brands. At scale, a company's marginal cost of adding portion size is not high (paying 50 cents to upgrade from medium to large fries), yet consumers get greater satisfaction and feel they've gotten a deal.

For a long time, consumers' mentality was to buy the biggest and most food for the least money (without considering their ideal intake). In fact, to this day, low-priced value meals remain a standard tool for fast-food brands to lift revenue and win market share.

Amy Bentley, a historian and food studies professor at New York University, said: "(After World War II) people's perception of food was quantity over quality. A tall, robust physique seemed to say: I don't need to do manual labor, I have plenty of food."

### But obesity followed, and the Big Mac was seen as the culprit

"What's the secret of the Big Mac's success? Lots of fat and salt!"

So said Andrew F. Smith, author of *Hamburger: A Global History* and a scholar of food history. Along with the Whopper's commercial success came the obesity and health problems it caused.

Nutritionists' criticisms of McDonald's have never let up. "The typical McDonald's meal — a hamburger, fries and an iced drink — doesn't give you much nutrition. This kind of diet clearly raises cholesterol and can ultimately lead to heart disease," said Harvard professor Dr. Jean Mayer.

In the documentary *Super Size Me*, filmed by Morgan Spurlock, he ate nothing but McDonald's for 30 days; as a result, he gained 25 pounds, his cholesterol rose 65 points, and his body fat went from 11% to 18%. Doctors said that if he continued this diet, it would soon trigger coronary heart disease and cirrhosis. The film won the directing award in the documentary category at the 2004 Sundance Film Festival.

![*Super Size Me* (image from Douban)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180224115706JM6gHBRav1TcpwCV.jpg-w600)

Marion Nestle, professor of nutrition and public health at New York University, believes "the Big Mac's influence on Americans and the world has been enormous — mainly on eating habits, and not all of those effects have been good. It became the benchmark and exemplar of junk food."

[In 1977, only 6% of Americans' total calorie intake came from fast food. By 2010, that figure had become 16%.](http://www.aicr.org/cancer-research-update/2016/11_02/cru-35-years-of-fast-food-calories.html?referrer=https://www.google.com.sg/) The share of the population that was overweight or obese also [rose from 42% to 65%.](https://www.oecd.org/els/health-systems/Obesity-Update-2017.pdf)

Although pinning all of this on the Big Mac is hardly fair, as McDonald's signature product it has probably borne the brunt of the attacks.

Michael Delligatti, son of Big Mac inventor Jim Delligatti, thinks blaming Americans' obesity on the Big Mac is unfair. "The lobster bisque you order at a high-end restaurant has far more calories than a Big Mac," he said.

But it is worth noting that amid today's ever-intensifying health trend, "bigger and more" fast-food products seem to be in vogue again.

In the Chinese market, McDonald's began promoting its "So Big" series in 2016. The series went on to become one of McDonald's best-selling lines last year (also one of the few new product series McDonald's has successfully launched in the Chinese market), although the draw of Kris Wu's fan base may have played a large role. Recently, the series has expanded from chicken cutlets and chicken wings into milk tea, ice cream and carbonated drinks.

And in the mature US market, because of enthusiastic customer response, McDonald's is rolling out $1, $2 and $3 menus nationwide this year. Its competitors — Taco Bell, Wendy's and Burger King — have all rolled out their own varied low-price meal deals.

### Why did the Big Mac become an economic index for measuring exchange rates?

In 1971, McDonald's went beyond North America and began its global expansion, choosing Japan as its first stop. In 1990 McDonald's entered the former Soviet Union, and in the same year opened in Shenzhen, China. The UK and France are both important markets for it (France was for a time McDonald's most profitable market outside the US). As it spread its signature golden arches across the globe, consumers eating the standardized fries, Coke and burgers at McDonald's also absorbed the American culture of efficient, modernized dining.

According to *Golden Arches East: McDonald's in East Asia*, when India's first McDonald's opened in New Delhi in 1996, it drew protests from Hindu leaders. A young Indian woman told *The Christian Science Monitor* in an interview: "I don't mind McDonald's serving beef in other countries and regions; I come here just to experience its culture."

McDonald's is the most globalized fast-food brand. Ray Kroc once said: "I don't believe in (single) market penetration. What we're talking and thinking about is the global market."

Today McDonald's has restaurants in nearly 120 countries or markets, and all of them sell the Big Mac (or a Big Mac variant). Its preparation specifications are roughly the same everywhere, with local McDonald's distributors responsible for negotiating ingredient prices. These two conditions make the Big Mac a decent benchmark for purchasing power parity (PPP).

Reportedly, *The Economist* initially considered using Coca-Cola as the basis for comparing exchange rates across countries. But it ultimately did not choose it, because Coca-Cola's price within the same country or region varies by channel (Coca-Cola at a convenience store, for example, costs more than at a supermarket). The Big Mac is sold only at McDonald's, with a uniform price within a given market, making price comparisons easier.

When *The Economist* launched the Big Mac index in 1986, it treated it merely as a humorous, simple indicator of exchange-rate levels and "purchasing power parity." Even now, more than thirty years later, *The Economist* still updates the Big Mac index every year. The index has been cited in more than twenty academic journals or studies.

The theoretical foundation of the Big Mac index is purchasing power parity. The theory holds that exchange rates will adjust on their own until a basket of goods sells for the same price in different countries. Except that this basket contains only one good — the Big Mac.

In four US cities, the Big Mac now sells for an average of [$5.28](http://www.tegbr.com/article/eed3445eeb6b897f). If a country's Big Mac price converts to more than $5.28, that country's currency is overvalued; if it falls below that number, the currency is undervalued. For example, in the eurozone the average price of a Big Mac is €3.95, which converts to $4.84 at current exchange rates — indicating that the euro is undervalued by 8.4% against the benchmark dollar.

![The latest Big Mac index (chart: Feng Xiuxia)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180224120720OdE8zF3ucS0HNCKf.JPG-w600)

The index has other uses too. Jonathan Perraton, a senior lecturer in economics at the University of Sheffield in the UK, said that when companies need to compare the cost of business travel across countries, the Big Mac index is a decent measure for judging exchange-rate levels. "But if you need something more precise and more in-depth, the Big Mac index has many limitations."

For example, it does not account for differences in labor costs between countries. As a traded good, the Big Mac, compared with non-traded goods (such as housing), cannot accurately measure exchange-rate levels and differences between developed and developing countries.

Still, the Big Mac index pioneered the "product index." Because it is easy to understand and spreads well, other media and research institutions besides *The Economist* — including *Bloomberg Businessweek* — have launched product indices of their own, such as the Starbucks tall latte, the iPhone, the iPod, IKEA's Billy bookcase and Coca-Cola. The products capable of naming an index are all uniformly produced, globally best-selling products, and the companies behind them are all globalized giants.

Of course, like the Big Mac index, most product indices can hardly reflect the whole picture — at the very least, they cannot be read along a single dimension.

### The Big Mac has become a boring burger in the eyes of the young generation — and that is the challenge McDonald's now faces

One sobering fact: [only one in five American millennials has tried McDonald's most famous food](http://www.wsj.com/articles/mcdonalds-knows-its-losing-the-burger-battlecan-it-come-back-1475769684#livefyre-comment).

After all, there are simply too many burgers to choose from now. Besides McDonald's old nemeses Burger King and Wendy's, there are many smaller burger chains that look more individual, more "human" (as opposed to McDonald's industrialized feel) — In-N-Out Burger and Five Guys are both more popular among millennials, and Shake Shack, the pricier boutique burger chain, was once such a high-growth brand that it became the hottest name in the restaurant business.

[In a 2014 burger review by the American Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/cro/magazine/2014/08/best-and-worst-fast-food-restaurants-in-america/index.htm), the Big Mac ranked last among 21 burgers. That was the verdict of 32,000 American consumers.

![Three sizes of the Big Mac (image from mcdonalds.com)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180224115549HFqnm91ThWM3XfEw.png-w600)

To boost its appeal, after piloting it at more than 130 restaurants, McDonald's rolled out two new Big Mac sizes nationwide in the first quarter of 2017 — the Mac Jr. (small Big Mac) and the Grand Mac (extra-large Big Mac).

This was almost the biggest adjustment McDonald's had made to the Big Mac in its nearly 50 years of existence. "We want to offer consumers choices for different lifestyles, to cater to their needs," McDonald's said in a press release — in fact, it wanted to capture both the big-bite and small-bite eaters.

Reportedly, the extra-large size was meant to counter the giant burgers of rivals such as Five Guys and Smashburger, while the small Big Mac took into account that arch-rival Shake Shack's burgers are a bit smaller; McDonald's also said the small Big Mac makes it more convenient for consumers who eat on the go.

According to McDonald's financial reports, [thanks to the timely rollout of the Big Mac's two new sizes to restaurants across the US (along with low-price drink promotions), US comparable sales grew 1.7% organically in the first quarter of 2017.](http://news.mcdonalds.com/news-releases/news-release-details/mcdonalds-reports-first-quarter-2017-results)

But its fortunes overseas do not seem so rosy. When the Grand Mac entered the UK market earlier this year, [one taste-tester wrote](https://www.somersetlive.co.uk/whats-on/whats-on-news/mcdonalds-grand-big-mac-review-1220236): "If you want a mind-numbingly boring burger, try McDonald's new Grand Mac."

![A Shake Shack burger (image from Shake Shack)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180224122232rhPkVp8xdJtnlwAX.jpg-w600)

Another major challenge: starting in 2011, [fresh-beef, made-to-order burgers gradually became popular among American fast-food and fast-casual brands](https://www.wsj.com/articles/mcdonalds-knows-its-losing-the-burger-battlecan-it-come-back-1475769684#livefyre-comment).

This consumer trend is not good news for a McDonald's famous for efficiency and standardization: rolling out fresh beef to more than 14,000 restaurants across the US would require changes to both McDonald's supply chain and its restaurant equipment. In addition, the speed at which restaurants serve this burger would slow.

It was only last year that McDonald's announced it would switch the Quarter Pounder's beef from frozen to fresh this year. Since McDonald's began testing fresh beef, some [franchisees expressed concern about the food-safety problems it could cause](https://www.qdaily.com/articles/35074.html): "If we don't handle the meat properly, bacteria could invade our products."

Beyond that, Americans seem to be eating more chicken. According to [US Department of Agriculture data](http://business.financialpost.com/news/retail-marketing/move-over-big-mac-mcdonalds-wants-to-become-the-new-chicken-king-in-transformational-poultry-push), in 2017 the average American consumed 92 pounds of chicken, versus just 57 pounds of beef. McDonald's has even copied chicken-focused fast-food chain Chick-fil-A by selling a Southern-style chicken sandwich.

The Big Mac's current predicament is, in fact, the challenge McDonald's faces today.

And McDonald's most talked-about product right now turns out to be [the Szechuan sauce that went viral because of the animated series *Rick and Morty*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/43644.html).
