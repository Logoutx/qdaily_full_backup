Solving problems is the hard truth — in packaging designers' eyes, how are problems discovered, and how should they be solved?

Think about it: when you stand in front of a shelf full of products trying to pick one, what do you base your choice on? Could it be the packaging's "looks"?

In fact, packaging is about far more than grabbing your attention. Apart from natural products that barely need any packaging at all, whenever you need to "use" something, you cannot escape its packaging. Picking up, putting down, opening, closing — we deal with all kinds of product packaging every single day.

![Image from: [Pinterest](https://jp.pinterest.com/pin/299841287667918888/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704241738350uLskvcato2EwNfy.jpg-w600)

And for designers, how a package is designed and produced has its own mysteries. Good packaging is never merely pretty, trendy, or cool — it must meet consumers' needs while also conveying a brand's beliefs and values.

In the past week, quite a few designers and senior executives from packaging design firms at home and abroad came to Shanghai to share their insights on design at the 2017 FBIF Food & Beverage Innovation Forum.

Their names may not be familiar to you yet, but their work is everywhere — in convenience stores, supermarkets, and online — and you have more or less come across it. For example: BETC, the French design firm behind Evian mineral water; LPK, the American firm that first put potato chips in a tube — Pringles; JKR, the British firm behind Domino's newly revamped minimalist packaging from last year; and L3 Branding, an emerging local design firm that created the packaging for Mengniu's *Hi! Milk* 《嗨！Milk》.

QDaily also sat down with them during the forum to talk about what good design is, how design language and design approaches are changing, and their thoughts on the consumer market as a whole. Based on their talks and interviews, we have distilled some interesting viewpoints. Perhaps they will give you a glimpse into what the designers behind good design are actually thinking.

### Back to essentials: packaging is getting simpler

For packaging design, the wave of minimalism has long ceased to be a novelty — you can see hints of it every year in the Pentawards winners, which are either fun, elegant, or simple.

Yet minimalist packaging is no easy feat. Its punchy simplicity lies in a highly distilled brand essence and in the pursuit of a simple, effective mode of interaction — which is precisely why it is a test of the designer.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424172553210a6fjlS3xFqX4L.png-w600)

In the eyes of Christophe Pradere, art director and founder of BETC — the French firm that designed Evian's water — water itself is an interesting product: colorless and tasteless, yet it can still be sold at a high price.

"Evian's brand and packaging are about emphasizing that what is inside is simply water. When you grab the bottle, what you are holding is water — the very essence of nature," he said. He believes food is likewise stressing essentialism — "stressing its own flavor and formula, stressing what the thing you have actually bought fundamentally is."

Last March, for the first time, Evian combined mineral water with fruit and plants, launching Fruits & Plants natural flavored water, and brought in BETC to do the design. The bottle was shaped like a drop of water, with vivid colors blended from fruit juice, and simple fruit patterns on the body indicating the flavors — patterns resembling three mountain peaks, an easy association with Evian's original logo.

This flavored water's design, while adding new elements, carries on Evian's sense of "purity." Through the packaging, you can see crystal-clear what it is actually selling — the "essentialism" Pradere stresses is fully on display here.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170423235917vb1YJMcTOFjsAXy2.png-w600)

![Image from: [BETC design](http://betcdesign.fr/front/we-are-creative)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424172603ZtjYHaxIBKsX3m6c.jpg-w600)

"Making things with less material and better resources is very much a trend of the moment," Peng Chong, art director at Pinzan Design, told QDaily.

In 2014, he won the Pentawards Platinum Award in the food category for a design made from the organic rice of *Qian·s Gift* 《黔之礼赞》. The piece rejected any industrial element: its paper came from handmade papermaking by local families in Guizhou, and the dyes used for printing were plant-based.

The emphasis on simplified packaging and environmental protection stands out especially in the rather special context of China's baijiu industry. Peng Chong's previous Pentawards-winning work — the sauce-aroma baijiu packaging he designed for Qian·s Gift in 2013 — was seen as a response to the enormous changes the baijiu industry is undergoing. Gone are the days of elaborate designs that spent up to 60% of a product's cost on packaging just to make the liquor look expensive; simplified packaging has become the new way of thinking.

### Consumers have changed, clients have changed, and the whole market has changed too

"Change is the only constant. From here on it will be a world of living editions — like the fashion industry, packaging will change frequently too."

Chen Peiqi, a partner at British design firm JKR, said in an interview with QDaily. Citing the fiercely competitive Japanese market — where the packaging of many everyday products is refreshed every three months — she noted that all brands are constantly making limited editions to respond to this ever-faster-changing market. In her view, the emoji-concept edition of Mengniu's Chunzhen yogurt launched at the end of last year is a good example of adapting to market change.

![Chunzhen yogurt "Honest Words for the New Year" emoji bottle. Image from: [Sohu](http://mt.sohu.com/20161219/n476337365.shtml)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424234521pmZcDR1eITH5x6hY.jpg-w600)

"They (the clients) now pay very close attention to young consumers. Their demands have shifted from what used to be grand and Western-flavored to cheeky, cute, and fun."

JKR had a fruitful year last year: the 140th-anniversary limited edition it designed for Budweiser and the Domino's pizza delivery box each picked up a Pentawards prize. From these two works, you can perhaps sense JKR's design language quietly undergoing certain changes.

When designing Domino's delivery box, the JKR team boldly discarded the motley elements of the original box, keeping only the brand's distinctive two-color mark — blue and red — paired with striking white dots. The brand's character and charm are presented to maximum effect, leaving consumers with a vivid impression.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424000009TDjuWKZnqdtmpXbH.jpg-w600)

![Domino's delivery box. Image from: Pentawards](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424000003CH7tx8eUQYWdRAr1.jpg-w600)

Budweiser's all-new packaging design, meanwhile, added a flat-style logo of the kind popular today — the biggest change since the first logo was born in 1936 — helping Budweiser's brand image better adapt to future communication across multiple terminals and platforms. A limited-edition can timed to ride the US election hype also became a hot topic for a while.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704240000195tS3QdIFUabRuzVX.jpg-w600)

![Budweiser. Image from: Pentawards](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704240000288krE9PjQeUA4tbsc.jpg-w600)

LPK, the American company that grew up alongside P&G and has more than thirty years of experience in packaging design, likewise feels that clients are trying every means to please young people — they now demand that packaging design also appeal to the millennial generation.

"When a big global brand wants to enter China, the truth is it doesn't really understand what the Chinese market wants," Benton N. Sauer, LPK's vice president for Asia, told QDaily. The label "millennials" is perhaps too broad; at the end of the day, because cultures differ, millennials in different regions cannot be lumped together.

The company once designed the innovative tube packaging for Pringles, and also handles packaging for daily-chemical products such as Pampers, Whisper, and Olay, with branches in North America, Europe, and Asia. Now they are turning more of their attention to early-stage consumer research: their Guangzhou branch is mainly responsible for research on the Chinese market, and every so often colleagues from the US office come asking, "Hey, what are Chinese people into these days?"

In their view, young consumers in emerging markets represented by China are more willing to accept new things. For international brands, this consumer group's preferences change quickly and are harder to figure out.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424174451ZAHfvNMEsLabeO8F.png-w600)

![Pringles. Image from: [LPK](https://www.lpk.com/work/pringles/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424174451seMzNY8iJ0Tf1yj2.png-w600)

Other changes in the Chinese market are also shifting LPK's design thinking.

A recent project of theirs was designing e-commerce-exclusive packaging for a daily-chemical brand. The brand hoped to give consumers a greater sense of freshness both offline and online. Nowadays, more and more brands put their more distinctive designs online, where consumers can find them more easily and make a purchase decision very quickly.

The shift of sales channels and of consumer attention has likewise changed designers' design paths. As online sales become ever more widespread, packaging design places greater emphasis on fashion and entertainment, and it demands a rapid shift in design thinking — it must capture consumers' attention within a much shorter time.

Jiaguwen, a firm specializing in liquor design, once partnered with Jiuxian.com to create "Shengjing Shanhe," a huangjiu made exclusively for sale on Jiuxian.com for Hunan Shengjing Huangjiu Company. It used a wooden gift box and a glaze-sprayed glass bottle, attempting to change people's fixed impressions of huangjiu with a distinctive new form.

!["Shengjing Shanhe". Image from: [Jiuxian.com](http://www.jiuxian.com/goods-30878.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425000138EszuVKPhc89a3QjW.png-w600)

Faced with a market environment that changes ever faster, how do you find your own positioning and identify consumers' real needs? NEOM, the Italian design firm founded in 2009, has its own method.

"We can test it on the shelf. Put the product on a shelf, then walk away and see whether you can spot it from ten meters off. Then move closer — five meters, three meters... It's the simplest test; in just five minutes you can tell whether a product's packaging design has problems," said Artem Movsesyan, a partner at NEOM.

Movsesyan believes that a design which cannot strike an emotional chord cannot attract people. He values the added value of emotion and values market research — he wants to use such research to awaken consumers' deeper needs.

"Our conclusion is A, but what we need to deliver is A+. You have to see further than what your research tells you — only then can you be number one in this market."

Back in 2011, a new infant-formula brand came to NEOM asking it to help with brand strategy. NEOM named the formula brand MAMO — a name so simple and accessible that people from Russia, Asia, or the Middle East can all accept it. For the packaging design, NEOM wanted to express a caring, joyful mother-and-baby relationship; they designed a cartoon character for MAMO and even wrote stories around the character. For babies across three different age groups, they designed three different MAMO packages — lively and cute, catching the attention of both babies and mothers.

"Actually, we do a lot of market research, and the results never match what we expected — they're eye-openers." Li Guanru, founder of L3 Branding, believes consumers come in many layers and cannot simply be summed up as "young consumers."

Li Guanru's company was founded four years ago. In its early days, he kept entering creative pitches and won projects from Mengniu and Tencent. Later, with *Hi! Milk* 《嗨！Milk》 designed for Mengniu, he won the 2016 Pentawards Gold, the 2016 iF Design Award, and the Best Dairy Packaging Design Award at the 2016 Global Dairy Innovation Awards, gradually making a name for himself in the circle.

Li Guanru feels that for brands like Mengniu, third- and fourth-tier cities are the real market. So packaging design needs to hit that "just right" spot — neither too artsy nor too plain — pushing just a bit above the middle of mass aesthetics.

*Hi! Milk* is the direct embodiment of this "aesthetic middle": its little cow shape perfectly matches the Tetra Pak diamond structure of the carton itself — pull up the folded corner and it becomes the cow's ear, and the cow's expression varies slightly from box to box. The cow shape made this milk stand out quickly among its peers: vivid and easy to understand.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425012734wLujPM146BchlE7f.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424180103uw1ybREBcFVTZ78O.jpg-w600)

"So when we talk about creativity and design, what we're really talking about is the business logic behind design — where the problem is, and what the solution is," Li Guanru told QDaily.
