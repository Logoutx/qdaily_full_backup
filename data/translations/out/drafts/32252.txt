Why did they choose to study animation? And where does their future lie?

The 2016 graduating class of the Animation Department at Communication University of China (CUC) held its graduation-project screening on June 10. That day, the auditorium in the university's main complex — which seats 1,500 — was packed on both floors, the aisles and corridors included. Altogether more than 2,000 people showed up, from inside and outside the campus.

With the screening approaching, Li Qiwei still had no confidence in the film he had made, *Tales from the Mountain* 《山中故事》. Half a month earlier, at the department's internal grading, their group's short film had only received a "Good." The music license came through the night before the screening, and the cut had to be redone yet again — they had been revising right up to the end.

After *Tales from the Mountain* screened, the applause in the hall lasted about 30 seconds — Li Qiwei's parents filmed the event on site, and that was the duration they recorded. Two other members of the group, Fu Yuxuan and Huang Zishan, were also seeing the final version for the first time, and were even more excited.

At the end of June, the Bilibili website featured a special page on "CUC graduation projects," which so far has racked up close to 360,000 views. *Tales from the Mountain* has its own page on Douban, where it holds a rating of 8.2 — one of the highest-scoring works among this crop of CUC Animation Department graduation films. One netizen's review reads: "Inevitably green, but it captures something of the flavor of *My Neighbor Totoro*. The music is especially praiseworthy — the flowers on the branches bloom one after another in time with the score, and the little dandelion's adorably dopey gaze is wonderfully vivid."

The graduation screening is like the "Spring Festival Gala" of CUC's Animation Department. Before graduating, students are grouped into teams to produce an animated short, and the grade it receives is their graduation grade. When people debate whether a school's animation department is "strong" or "weak," the evidence they usually go by is precisely the level of these student graduation works.

Beyond serving to spot talent, the "graduation gala" is also when animation companies come to "pick people." Among those 2,000 people at CUC were more than 40 animation-related companies. On the second day after the screening, Li Qiwei received a call from "October Media," the studio of *Monkey King: Hero Is Back* director Tian Xiaopeng. He accepted the offer and became a "storyboard artist" (a role in animation production analogous to a screenwriter).

Li Qiwei was quite satisfied with how things turned out.

But a Spring Festival Gala–style scene like this has its melancholy side. Among animation students at major schools there is a tacit understanding: after graduation, they may never again find the motivation to throw themselves wholeheartedly into making an animated short that belongs to them. There is a very good chance that this is the best work they will ever make.

*Tales from the Mountain*, by Li Qiwei, Huang Zishan, Fu Yin, Fu Yuxuan, Zhang Yubing

Four years earlier, Li Qiwei had come to Beijing from Henan to become a college student. The Animation Department's class of 2012 at CUC enrolled three classes with 81 students in total.

Those 81 students were a cohort of winners under the education system's selective gauntlet. More than 250 universities nationwide offer an undergraduate animation major, and each year more than 10,000 freshmen enroll in animation programs. Of these, typically only the most qualified get into the few best schools: Communication University of China, Beijing Film Academy, Zhejiang University of Media and Communications, China Academy of Art, Central Academy of Fine Arts, Guangzhou Academy of Fine Arts.

Most animation programs are very "young." CUC's Animation Department was founded in 2001. Around 2003, China began actively supporting the animation industry, and the great majority of schools only rolled out their animation programs in the decade after that. Some schools converted existing programs into animation majors, shifting over the faculty from former design and film/TV programs — the teachers were basically the same teachers, or young graduate students with little practical experience hired as instructors. Even the best schools have this problem.

Of course, Li Qiwei knew none of this. His reason for applying to the major was that, among art programs, the Animation Department sounded "fashionable."

Unlike regular academic majors, animation runs on the "art exam" system: the gaokao score counts only as a reference, and the independent professional-skills exam is what matters most.

Li Qiwei's grades in high school were poor, so he took up drawing. In early 2012, he sat the entrance exams for the animation programs of both Communication University of China and Beijing Film Academy ahead of schedule. Both exams covered art theory, basic drawing skills, and narrative ability — which, in fact, was exactly what he would mainly study after entering university.

He counted as "bold": he applied to only two schools. Some art students, playing it "safe," apply to as many as a dozen or more.

By contrast, Li Qiwei's classmate Kong Youyang took up animation on a momentary "impulse." He graduated from the high school affiliated to Yunnan Normal University, with grades good enough for an ordinary first-tier university, and had never deliberately learned to draw. But when thinking about his application preferences in his final year of high school, "not wanting to study liberal arts, not wanting to study science," he suddenly discovered there was a "great major" like animation. Moreover, he had seen *The Red Scarf Hero* 《红领巾侠》, a short film by Cheng Teng from CUC's class of 2007, on CCTV — and thought it was amazing.

![*The Red Scarf Hero*, by Cheng Teng, Li Xia, Li Yunfei](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609152143555JUzSAlbfYigsVvX.jpg-w600)

All those motives can now only be chatted about over casual meals. After starting college, they all discovered that the "making animation" they had imagined was not the same thing as the reality.

Kong Youyang says the normal state of his college life was "staying up late." The Animation Department's courses fill the entire week — required and elective, theory and hands-on practice — and many of them come with assignments. Take the freshman-year "2D Animation Design" course, which teaches Flash, the relatively low-barrier software of animation production. Students might have to work more than ten hours to produce just a few seconds of footage.

Kong Youyang actually liked this state. He felt he had come to the right place — "the classmates around me are from the same world as me" — and every day the conversation was about art, film, design.

But Li Qiwei didn't like it at all. When he entered the school he was one of the students with the strongest drawing fundamentals, but after four years, key animation was no longer his strong suit. He says he somewhat wasted his major courses from freshman through junior year — if you want to, assignments can just be gotten by with. For a while he shifted his interest to live-action film, devoting more energy to it and even sitting in on the directing courses of the neighboring department. During vacations he drew storyboards and worked as art staff for film crews, earning decent money that covered his own tuition and living expenses.

From the second semester of junior year, students stopped attending classes and began freely forming groups to make "joint projects" — likewise animated shorts, serving as practice for the "graduation project" of senior year.

In the summer before senior year, they began preparing for the "graduation project": choosing partners, choosing an advisor, thinking up scripts, thinking up styles. When the new semester began, they officially got started.

In fact, whether "joint projects" or the "graduation project," as mandatory academic procedures of CUC animation they were only established after 2010 (for the class of 2006). Ai Shengying, head of CUC's Animation Department, told QDaily that before that, students also had to make graduation works, but they might apply to graduate having made only a "trailer" that could hardly count as a complete work. Yet "watching animation and making animation are two different things" — without real practice it is hard to say you have truly learned anything.

Li Qiwei's process of making *Tales from the Mountain* was anything but smooth. He was fixated on making a lyrical work but unsure how to handle the plot. He wanted more dramatic storylines — for example, having the protagonist stumble into a mountain cave and encounter magical creatures — but in the end none of those plot points were used. In fact, it wasn't until the last two or three months that he truly figured out the direction and got into the zone.

The problem Kong Youyang ran into was workload. His graduation project with teammates Zheng Ligang and Lü Wei was called *Born ANIMA* 《生 ANIMA》 — a science-fiction animation on a cosmic stage, telling a story of racial battles. The characters use a realistic style, and the backgrounds are highly detailed. The short runs 12 minutes, one of the longest in this year's crop of graduation films.

*Born ANIMA* was one of the shorts awarded "Excellent." The production had a clear division of labor: Kong Youyang did the design work, Zheng Ligang had the best drawing skills, and Lü Wei excelled at backgrounds.

*Born ANIMA*, by Kong Youyang, Zheng Ligang, Lü Wei

The few shorts from this class that ultimately won praise have completely different styles. *Akemi's First Love* 《明美的初恋》 goes for a Japanese-comedy style, telling of a girl who is only into "human–beast romance" and falls in love with a gorilla teacher. The "male protagonist" of *The Old Song* 《老歌》 is a tape recorder, traveling through Beijing in 1997 and meeting a "music-box girl" with an even stronger vintage feel. There are also works completed by a single person. *Hot Hot Hot* 《烫烫烫》 is an experimental MV-like short focused on style, so its workload was not as heavy.

Animation needs more than visuals — it also requires voice acting and music. Every group has to spend a sum of money, anywhere from three or four thousand yuan at the low end to ten thousand at the high end, split evenly among the members.

![*Akemi's First Love*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160915222224hGwTvPi2Jg754O9H.jpg-w600)

In the end credits of *Tales from the Mountain*, *Born ANIMA*, *The Old Song*, *Akemi's First Love*, and *Hot Hot Hot*, a "116" logo appears. It refers to "Study Room 116" on the first floor of the teaching building of CUC's School of Animation. Roughly thirty students from about ten graduation-project groups spent their entire senior year in Room 116.

Kong Youyang says that in this room everyone could see each other's progress at any time, give each other feedback, and get quick help if they didn't know how to use a piece of software. On top of that, if one group's advisor came to "inspect," they would give suggestions to the other groups.

Some people became recognized "model workers." Li Ruijie of *Akemi's First Love* arrived earliest and left latest. She says her teammate Pan Jing could draw both fast and well, so she herself had to put in more time to reach her goals.

The building's access control kicked in at 11 p.m., but no one worried about "overstaying" — at worst they'd climb out through a fixed window. Whenever a deadline crunch came, someone would pull an all-nighter here. At its most crowded, the room held 10 folding beds.

That year they rarely had a moment alone. Besides people, Room 116 also had animals — a dog, a cat, two birds, a hedgehog, and two tree frogs. With the teachers' tacit consent, they were kept in the classroom. Walking the dog became a popular way to relieve anxiety; sometimes someone would just come back from one walk and get handed the leash again to go out for the next round.

After the June graduation screening, Li Qiwei left a WeChat message for the official account "Sir Movie," "recommending himself" on behalf of his class's shorts. The reply at first was: if you're a theatrical blockbuster, come find us. Li Qiwei then sent over the comments from Bilibili and Douban. It wasn't until August 15 that he found Sir Movie had pushed an article titled "Once Again Amazed by an Obscure Chinese Animation." The article racked up "100,000+" views.

Although the students won't earn box-office revenue from these shorts, "academy-style animation" has already become a genre of its own in China's independent animation scene, and animation fans eagerly await excellent new works. Ai Shengying, the department head, says this is one of the meanings of the graduation project: the more "sacred ceremony" the result feels like, the more willing students are to give it their all.

Ai Shengying is responsible for three classes of students at once, and is one of the "most reliable" teachers in students' eyes. Although every class has a homeroom advisor, in reality they rarely show up except for class meetings. Li Qiwei says that in his three-plus years of college he didn't have deep contact with teachers, but during the graduation project he could still get help from his advisor.

Not everyone values the process of the animation major. Some had long planned to switch fields and found ways to "freeload" onto a group, taking the last spot in the credits. Some shorts failed and the groups disbanded, their members redistributed into other groups. Ten students from this class were graded "unqualified" and will have to delay graduation and retake.

As for some students, their post-graduation destination was settled even before the graduation project was finished. The list of 10 students recommended for graduate school (without exam) was confirmed before winter break, and it included Cui Daiqi, author of *The Old Song*, and Li Ruijie, author of *Akemi's First Love*. They completed all their coursework, including public courses like politics and English.

At the graduate level, they too may join their teachers' animation projects. Of the 30 teachers in CUC's Animation Department, one-third work on commercial animation off campus. Li Zhiyong's "Jiang Jiang Jiang Studio" produced the animated series *Kung Fu Bunny*. Li Wei, nicknamed by students the "key-animation god," was one of the executive directors of *Big Fish & Begonia*; the pilot for his directed film *Legend of Deification* also drew praise online.

![*The Old Song*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160915222602gxkTHywouDVSeU2c.jpg-w600)

There are also the mavericks. Liu Nanli, author of the animation *Hot Hot Hot*, founded her own studio and took on MV and commercial work. She had decided as early as sophomore and junior year that she didn't want to be a company employee. Like everyone else she worships "animation gods" such as Satoshi Kon and Katsuhiro Otomo, but if you ask whose "state of life" she aspires to, she names the independent cartoonist "Men Xiaolei" — because she gets to make the things she loves, free and unbound.

Three people currently fund Liu Nanli's studio: her mother, her older sister, and the boss of Shanghai Liutang Animation. She had worked with Liutang since college, creating comics for the company on the U17 comics platform. Liutang is preparing to get into animation and gave her a script in an "American comic style," making her the director and having her pull together some classmates.

After graduation, Liu Nanli found a duplex apartment in Beijing to serve as both studio and home. Eleven classmates from "116" currently live here together.

![*Hot Hot Hot*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160915221342v9WCTQFzHufMIxpO.jpg-w600)

Kong Youyang's original plan was to study abroad. His "idol" is Cheng Teng, whose work he had seen on CCTV years ago and who now works at DreamWorks in the United States. Cheng Teng graduated from CUC's animation undergraduate program in 2011, then went on to spend three years in the animation program of the USC School of Cinematic Arts.

Among the class of 2016, about five people are confirmed to study abroad in places like the United States and France, and some others are still applying. Kong Youyang says going abroad means access to the most advanced animation education, and also to job opportunities overseas.

But Cheng Teng told QDaily that going abroad is undeniably a gamble. Arts majors rarely get scholarships, and his three years at USC cost US$150,000 in tuition. If you count the top animation film companies in the United States, there are only a handful — Disney, Pixar, DreamWorks — and they hire only a tiny number of people each year. He was the only student DreamWorks signed last year.

Another fact: even though animation students all love Japanese anime, almost none of them consider studying or working in Japan. That's because Japan's own animation talent has to endure long years of competition to break through, and it's hard for "outsiders" to be accepted.

Kong Youyang, though, is still in China — he wasn't ready for the TOEFL. He says four years in the Animation Department eroded his test-taking ability considerably. In July he went to Shenzhen and joined the animation division of Tencent Pictures. In September 2014, Tencent Interactive Entertainment announced the establishment of a film and TV business unit; Tencent Pictures was formally founded in 2015, and its first slate included an animated film adapted from the *Speed Drifters* game IP.

Kong Youyang's parents feel Tencent is a stable big company with better benefits. He also considered "Luo Xiaohei Studio," founded by the animator MTJJ. The web animated series *The Legend of Luo Xiaohei* 《罗小黑战记》, which the studio began producing in 2011, has a fan base thanks to its cute art style and is also preparing a movie version.

Four graduates from CUC's class of 2016 Animation Department entered Luo Xiaohei Studio, including Kong Youyang's teammate Zheng Ligang. Kong Youyang says that when you count the companies the industry agrees are "reliable," there are probably fewer than ten.

As for pay, it varies quite a bit from company to company. The low end is five or six thousand yuan a month; the good cases get more than 10,000. Lü Wei, another of Kong Youyang's teammates, is now with him at Tencent in Shenzhen but earns more, because he joined the most profitable game division.

![Lü Wei, Kong Youyang, Wang He (of this class), and Zheng Ligang at the graduation ceremony](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609152228418Qb4SecBY2HTjLRq.jpg-w600)

Li Qiwei says he once didn't plan to join an animation company, but the sense of enjoyment he got while making *Tales from the Mountain* changed his mind.

He says the praise from Bilibili and Douban netizens at first made him "a little full of himself." But when he arrived at October Media in August and saw the level of Tian Xiaopeng and the other veterans, he was thoroughly convinced. Li Qiwei says he only aspires to commercial films that reach the widest possible audience.

Maybe he is catching a good moment. *Monkey King: Hero Is Back* grossed 900 million yuan at the box office in 2015, and *Big Fish & Begonia* took in 500 million in 2016. New companies are being founded, new capital is flowing in. Enlight Media invested in October Media (of *Monkey King: Hero Is Back*) and B&T (of *Big Fish & Begonia*). At the end of 2015, Enlight announced the establishment of "Coloroom Pictures," with investments in 13 animation companies and a slate of more than 20 new films announced, including "IP adaptations" of hit games, web novels, and web comics.

Amid a pile of options, students must judge for themselves what is the future and what is a bubble. Kong Youyang recalls that a boss from Anhui called him after the graduation screening and said: "We're going to make a big movie — bring your whole team over."

Graduates of CUC's Animation Department are already the best cohort on the "talent market." [In an interview with AVG News, Wang Lei, vice dean of CUC's School of Animation, said](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s?__biz=MzAwNTc0MTY4NA==&mid=2650720356&idx=1&sn=7935000d8738fba8c56c84ec55538de8&scene=1&srcid=0822GHBfUPddTYWOYTMf6Z36&pass_ticket=qJrgs3LE574a1oyUqakz7YKA5idQNLqbJKq73t03FlrJ2fZsJNCvxnPbgG51mfJV#rd) that the animation industry as a whole has roughly 200,000 practitioners, and if every year newcomers equal to 1/20 of an industry keep entering, that is still a bit too many. He said: "The fever of scale expansion in the animation major has largely passed; we have reached the stage of careful accumulation and competing on quality. What matters is not chasing waves — what matters is respecting the basic laws of education."

Cheng Teng says that when he graduated in 2011, the companies he wanted to join were all in Los Angeles. But the project he is working on now actually belongs to the rights of Shanghai's "Oriental DreamWorks." He says he has also talked with Enlight's "Coloroom," but he feels he needs to hone himself on projects for a while longer.

Cheng Teng's 2011 graduation short *Happy Anniversary* 《纪念日快乐》 won the "Special Prize" at the 11th Tokyo Anime Award. As for whether a graduation film might turn out to be one's best work ever, he says, "That all depends on you."

Li Qiwei still remembers what his advisor told him over the "farewell dinner" on the night of the graduation screening: "In the graduation short you were the creative core, but once you go into a company, you need to be a 'cog' and cooperate with other people's work — that's the state of the industry."

He accepted this. After expressing that he was in no hurry, he added: "I'll probably think about becoming a director when I'm in my thirties."
