In ten years, Wang Shi's country and his company became unrecognizable — and so did his confidence

**Wang Shi began to "mature" at 57.**

**Before that, Wang Shi would say, "I'm a famous entrepreneur — I've even climbed Everest." To many people, 2008 was a turning point in China's fortunes: the Wenchuan earthquake, the Beijing Olympics and the great-power rise behind it — and, between the ebb and flow of those, a global financial crisis that only added to China's strange new confidence. That year, Wang Shi — 57 at the time — has said again and again in hindsight, was his darkest hour.**

**That year, his views on the Wenchuan earthquake and the charity that followed offended a great many people.**

**So-called maturity — like everything said about boys leaving adolescence behind — means no longer saying whatever comes to mind, learning to compromise, moving from what you "want" to do to what you "should" do...**

**One of China's most famous entrepreneurs has "matured." What does that actually mean? Ten years on, Wang Shi is still caught up in it, and may not yet have had the chance to examine it fully and calmly.**

**Some words have come to nothing. Wu Xiabo once wrote a preface for one of Wang Shi's books: "No longer seeing the self as an appendage and parasite of the government, and able to take part in social reconstruction in a more active, modern way... this is one of the most important public events in Chinese society."**

**By the time of the later Baoneng affair, that persona — and those hopes — had ended too.**

**"I'm a famous entrepreneur, I've even climbed Everest" — climbing up is one thing; coming back down is not easy.**

**An entrepreneur, public figure, and motivational icon of unusual achievement, plastered with labels of the strong — for him confidence had become instinct, to the point of arrogance.**

**In our conversation, he showed a powerful desire to express himself, though he hardly lacks opportunities to do so; a keen eagerness to voice his opinions, though his opinions are a little too correct. He falls often on the rhetorical question — the force is in him.**

**"Do you think I'm the type now who doesn't want to speak, who watches every word?"**

**"Say too much and you're bound to slip up; you get shot even lying down — am I that type?"**

**"****If I were that clever, would I have had to go through all that?****"**

**"****In conversation, do I seem pessimistic or optimistic? Do you actually still need an answer?****"**

**"****No worries — is that even life?****"**

**"****It's not that I can't adapt to retirement — how could I not adapt?****"**

**"****China's reform has always been top-down, so the future will be top-down too — why not be optimistic?****"**

**For this "Ten Years" series, we contacted several public figures who were active around the time of the Wenchuan earthquake, but nearly all declined to be interviewed; some shied away at the mere mention of Wenchuan.**

**Wang Shi did not deliberately avoid it, and is rather proud of his freedom of expression.**

**But the fact is, Wang Shi is no longer as "free" as he once was.**

**He tries to avoid talking about other people. About the future, he has many worries, but will not discuss their specifics; "cautiously optimistic" is the closest answer one can get.**

**On May 23, 2018, we met Wang Shi at Vanke's headquarters in Shenzhen, a 380-meter-long building designed by architect Steven Holl and known as the "horizontal skyscraper." Wang Shi, 67, sat in his office wearing a blue suit, a white pocket square accenting the breast pocket.**

**In ten years, Wang Shi's country and his company have become unrecognizable. So has his confidence.**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607220019Kv0JAYw1jl65IW8a.jpg-w600)

In 2005 I published a book. In the afterword I wrote this passage: "In 2004, when Vanke turned 20, Vanke would have no more stories. Why? Great companies have no stories. What story does Coca-Cola have? What story does M&M's have?"

Of course Vanke is not a great company, but it was close — and being close means no more stories. But in fact you only realize the story was just beginning. All I can say is I was arrogant, full of myself. Vanke is 34 years old now, another 14 years have passed, and looking at it today — damn it, we're still far from great.

These past ten years have been too meaningful. The film about Churchill during World War II is called *Darkest Hour*, and I think my darkest hour was 2008. Many people ask whether the Vanke–Baoneng battle of the past two years was my darkest hour. I say no — it was 2008.

I started my business in Shenzhen in 1983–84. That experience — worse than death, not knowing whether tomorrow would come. Having been through that year, nothing is more searing — but it was not my darkest hour.

Because if tomorrow doesn't come, it doesn't mean your life is over.

I might have failed in my startup, gone back home, even been unable to stay in Shenzhen and returned to Guangzhou — all possible. But I was not passive on that path. Before I came to Shenzhen at 33, my life had basically been chosen for me. Jobs were assigned; you didn't do what you wanted, you did what you were assigned. Even the army — you didn't know where you'd be posted. Everything was passive; society arranged it all.

But the first time I resigned and went to Shenzhen to start a business, that was my choice. If I chose it and failed, I had no complaints; if I tried my best and still failed, then so be it — I'd do something else. Even that hardship didn't make me feel life was over, because at least there was no moral condemnation. What is a darkest hour? It's 2008 — the whole thing was moral condemnation of me. So what if you've climbed Everest?

I remember at the end of 2008, a documentary filmmaker asked me, "What was your biggest feeling this past year?" I said: "Three thank-yous — thanks, not gratitude. First: during the Wenchuan earthquake, my performance drew down upon me the fury and abuse of millions of netizens, which made me see myself anew. To put it positively, I realized my personal influence was greater than I had imagined, and Vanke's influence was greater than Vanke's management team had imagined."

Since the influence was greater than imagined, and I had said something inappropriate, being condemned like that was only to be expected.

In the negative sense, it knocked me flat on the ground, back to zero, letting me know just who I was. I had been a famous entrepreneur; I had climbed Everest — and still I had to redefine myself. I did not say, "Ah, who would have thought society was like this." I did not.

But it also showed my stubbornness. During the Wenchuan earthquake, another famous entrepreneur said something similar after I did, and the torrent of abuse that had been aimed at me instantly shifted onto him. But he was not stubborn; his PR team put out the fire in three days, and then the abuse came whooshing back onto me. And I kept saying: "I didn't do anything wrong, didn't say anything wrong — why should I compromise? Why should I apologize? Why should I explain?"

You can also see how deficient Vanke's PR capability was — Vanke, after all those years, should not have been like that.

This was not a bad thing in itself that we wanted to cover up, afraid of being exposed — it wasn't like melamine being exposed. It was simply that I said things people didn't want to hear. It wasn't a question of right or wrong, but of timing. And with no emergency PR handling, the company looked very immature — especially in this internet era, where one careless move can lose you the whole game.

Looking back over these ten years, this matter had an even bigger effect on the growth of the Vanke team.

Although it was triggered by my remarks, the handling was done by the Vanke team, since I was already an executive chairman and didn't involve myself in many specifics. In the end we decided to focus on Zundao, in Mianzhu — from funding to earthquake-resistant construction — and built a very close relationship with the place.

With the Ya'an earthquake afterward, we moved from mere passion and disorder toward rationality and order; our response to natural disasters became much more mature.

If another earthquake like that happened, would I say something similar? No — because I've improved. Second, even if I did say something similar, netizens would not treat me with that same extreme, unforgiving attitude, because society has progressed.

Through this affair we also realized Vanke was too aloof, too self-righteous. But was the problem completely solved in 2008? No. The Vanke–Baoneng battle that followed showed it again: we were still too aloof, out of touch with the ground. So in a sense, you could say it wasn't a shareholding crisis so much as a crisis of the people involved — we simply didn't know how the outside world saw us, or how we should deal with it.

Despite all that — do you think I'm the type now who doesn't want to talk, who is cautious with every word? Or the type who thinks, ah, I'm so mature now, I'll just keep quiet — say too much and you're bound to slip up, you get shot even lying down? Am I that type?

On January 23 this year, after stepping down from Vanke, I gave my first public speech, at the Water Cube. The venue I chose was a flamboyant one, right?

The director asked if I could pick a song I liked to set the mood. I said "Blue Lotus" — the theme of "Blue Lotus" is longing and the pursuit of freedom, the blue lotus that never withers. That's the song I chose.

Tell me — is that a gesture of confidence, of arrogance, or a gesture of timid caution?

![In January 2018, Wang Shi gives his first speech after stepping down as chairman, at the Water Cube](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607220256AGBs9pUhMgteYr8a.jpg-w600)

My biggest change over these ten years is my understanding of freedom. What is "freedom"? Freedom is doing what you want to do. For many reasons you often can't do what you want, so the highest state of pursuing freedom is doing what you want to do.

But that is no longer my definition. For me, freedom is doing what you should do. What should you do? What society needs you to do — and that may be something you don't like doing. Take my philanthropy: society needs philanthropy to move forward, but there are many kinds. I can choose environmental protection, which I love — protecting endangered animals like the giant panda, the snow leopard, the Siberian tiger. I was born in the Year of the Tiger myself, and I love it.

What about garbage sorting, disability causes — do I like those? Frankly, not necessarily. But society needs you to do them, so I chose the "should." I made this decision entirely from the angle of my own growth, not of social or political change.

Reform and opening has been going for 40 years. I founded Vanke 34 years ago; I came to Shenzhen 35 years ago. All this time I have been in a state of longing for freedom without being free — struggling, struggling, pushing forward.

In that process, I always thought of myself, doing what I liked to do. That's why things ended up the way they did — because I was unwilling to compromise, which is why 2008 happened. If I kept doing what I wanted to do, it wouldn't be impossible, and it would be splendid — but wouldn't it be too selfish?

With China where it is today, am I a beneficiary of the system? Am I a very successful beneficiary? Does China need progress? Does it need reform?

If all I could see was doing what I wanted to do, wouldn't that be too selfish? Shouldn't I think more about what society needs? Moving from what you want to do to what you should do — that is my updated understanding of freedom. In other words, freedom and constraint are one and the same. If freedom has no constraint, there are big problems.

In 2008 I was 57 — no longer young. What did I feel at 50? At 50 I was extremely active, a regular on CCTV. I'm rather snobbish, you know — these days I don't go on CCTV for just anything, but back then, whatever CCTV invited me to, I went.

With one program, the moment I walked in I knew I'd made a mistake agreeing to it — it turned out to be the "Sunset Red" show. The audience was a sea of old men and old ladies. I thought, I've walked into the wrong studio — what does this have to do with me? I told the director I'd made a mistake. I said: one day I will be "sunset red," but not today.

It feels like yesterday, and this year I'm already 67.

![Vanke Center, Shenzhen](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018060722152439odBSqsO6nxc5JM.jpg-w600)

**In 2000, Wang Shi was 49. From that year on he published a book nearly every two years, about Vanke's founding and his own mountaineering journeys. The content and the covers all showed his daring, flamboyant side.**

**Cultivating his image this way made Wang Shi one of the rare entrepreneurs who endorse other companies beyond their own. He has endorsed no fewer than 10 brands, and each brand had to fit his established image — endorsements involving top-tier luxury were all turned away.**

**Wang Shi's personal heroism shaped his public image, and also determined most of the trajectory of his life.**

**Before 1983, every choice in Wang Shi's life was passive — an assigned job, conscription into the army — until, at 32, he resigned from his job in Guangzhou and went to Shenzhen to found Vanke. He began to pursue self-realization, and won his first battle. In 1991, Vanke became one of the first companies in mainland China to list publicly; in 1998, it became the largest real estate company among China's listed firms.**

**Wang Shi was labeled from the very start.**

**Then in 2008, Wang Shi ran into the donation scandal he calls his "darkest hour." "You might not make it through tomorrow — how could that not be a darkest hour?"**

**On May 12, 2008, Vanke announced a donation of 2 million yuan to the disaster area in Sichuan. Netizens agreed the figure was disgracefully small.**

**In the early hours of May 15, Wang Shi replied on Weibo: "Of course I admire companies that donate more than 10 million. But as chairman, I think Vanke's 2 million is appropriate — it is the largest single donation the board has authorized management to make. Even if the authorization were larger, I would still consider 2 million an appropriate amount. China is a country prone to disasters; relief charity is a normal state of affairs, and corporate giving should be sustainable, not a burden. In Vanke's internally organized charity drives, there is a notice: in each drive, ordinary employees' donations are capped at 10 yuan. The point is to keep charity from becoming a burden."**

**The Weibo post drew a flood of criticism and mockery, and Wang Shi quickly became a target of public outrage. Recalling that day in his book, Wang Shi wrote: "I was denounced by netizens across the country, condemned in words; then the intense emotions fermented and exploded, brewing the biggest public-opinion crisis in Vanke's history."**

**Wang Shi has always believed that the charity of the majority can turn into the tyranny of the majority. To this day, he does not think he said anything wrong.**

**But at Vanke's shareholder meeting on June 5, 2008, Wang Shi apologized nonetheless. The meeting began with his apology and ended with his apology, and it was decided to add 100 million yuan to the donation for the disaster area.**

**After 2009, Wang Shi began teaching business school courses at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and Peking University, while serving as chairman of the Alxa SEE Ecological Association; in 2011 he also became chairman of the One Foundation.**

**"Philanthropy" gradually became his third label, after Vanke and mountaineering. He now advises more than 40 philanthropic organizations; ten years ago the number was 4.**

**At this point his personal heroism slowly began to fade, and altruism appeared more often in his life. Wang Shi sees this as one of his biggest changes over the past ten years. "But altruism has its preconditions. To be altruistic as a matter of duty before you're famous — many people can do that; I couldn't. Back then I was a self-interested man who didn't harm others — within the premise of self-interest, I could be altruistic."**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607221532NfVxsurgilGUDR0H.jpeg-w600)

**When Wang Shi went from Guangzhou to Shenzhen in 1983 to start a business, he had no certainty about the new special economic zone's future. He treated Shenzhen as a "gap year," planning to go study in California in the United States two years later. But after founding Vanke, he never entertained the idea again.**

**Only at 50 did he realize his dream of studying abroad was still unfulfilled.**

**At a 2010 thank-you banquet for the Harvard China Fund, the fund's executive director invited Wang Shi to visit Harvard as a scholar, and he immediately accepted. But within Harvard there was no small controversy over whether a Chinese entrepreneur should be invited; three months later, after a small "interview," Harvard finally sent the formal invitation.**

**Before Harvard, Wang Shi had embraced Western culture almost completely. "My business and management philosophy was always 'take what works,' because modern industry and modernization are products of Western civilization," Wang Shi says.**

**Moreover, for Wang Shi there was no great barrier to accepting and learning from the West. There were no intellectuals in his family; the Cultural Revolution began when he was in the second year of junior high, and he never received an orthodox Chinese education — so there was no clash between two worldviews to speak of.**

**He even felt relieved about this. "I always took it as my advantage. In my subjective consciousness I had always felt distant from traditional culture," Wang Shi says.**

I embraced the West completely. But you find that all these years since the founding of New China, the industrial system we built and human civilization as a whole are consistent with Western industrial modernization. Even though we had a planned economy, a different system, much of it does not contradict. Manufacturing and craft are the shared wealth of humankind; only the distribution of wealth, the operation of markets, and modes of competition differ. The market economy built by reform and opening is also a market economy guided by the modern Western contract spirit.

So when I got to the US I didn't feel like a blank slate. I already had plenty of experience, because in many respects what New China built is consistent with the general direction of human civilization.

Second, compared with the West, we were obviously critical of traditional Chinese culture — and from today's perspective, that criticism was actually nihilistic. What is criticism? You have to know why something is bad. The education we received led to a nihilistic kind of criticism: we didn't understand it, yet we negated it. That is nihilism.

I was always rather smug about this nihilism; I considered it my advantage. Why? Because I was unpolluted. The Cultural Revolution started when I was in the second year of junior high, and after that I was barely educated at all. It was always "criticize Lin Biao and Confucius," "smash the Confucian shop" — from the May Fourth Movement of old right up to the present, my education was a blank.

So when I got to Shenzhen I devoured Western knowledge. Before that I read novels and literature, watched the limited imported films, and worshipped and yearned for the West.

But once I actually got to the West, my understanding of it was not the West for the West's sake — it was more about comparison, because I'm still Chinese, Eastern to the bone. As an engineering student I never received very systematic training in logic. At Harvard I absorbed the Western logical way of thinking; I would compare what was good and what was bad by the same logic — but once I compared, I found there was no way to compare.

I used to think I didn't understand the West well enough; it turned out I didn't understand my own culture well enough either. Even if our culture is bad — fine — but you have to know why it is bad. You can't nihilistically say it's just bad, it's feudal, it's dross.

Before Harvard I was already teaching at the HKUST Business School and Peking University's Guanghua School of Management, lecturing on corporate culture. The first lecture was on entrepreneurial spirit, and I said China has no entrepreneurial spirit.

At Harvard I talked with a professor who said China had the well-field system — egalitarianism. Each person got a plot of land, with a common plot in the middle; it was contractual in nature. Right after liberation there were still land title deeds, which were later burned. Is a title deed a contract? Of course. If there were no contract spirit, how could title deeds have continued through so many dynasties, right up to liberation?

China's contract tradition differs from the West's. It has its drawbacks, of course — for instance, in China liability is joint and several: when two of us sign a contract there's a guarantor in between; if something goes wrong with you, there's still the guarantor. It's more about joint liability. Why does China say "a father's debts are repaid by his son"? In the West this doesn't exist, because the West has limited liability — limited to the point that if you can't pay, your wife is your wife and your children are your children; it has nothing to do with them.

That is the modern Western contract spirit; such progress led to prosperity and development. But in China it doesn't work — you'll still find cases today where, clearly liability is limited and you don't have to bear responsibility anymore, yet the father's debts are still repaid by the son.

![Harvard University](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607222203phsaZkPqr1UYnAu6.jpg-w600)

Another important point in China's contract spirit is sympathy for the weak. What sympathy is there in the West? In competition, if you lose, you lose — no one pities the weak. China not only has guarantors, it sympathizes with the weak.

In an agrarian civilization, when land was distributed equally, who was the weak party? Certainly the one selling the land; the buyer was the strong one.

If you sold your field or house and want it back, or when the buyer wants to sell the land on, the original seller has the right of first refusal. Second, not only the right of first refusal — it must be sold back to him at the original price.

So you find China leans more toward egalitarianism; society then lacks the progress that competition brings — but at the same time, what it emphasizes is that everyone has food to eat.

With this understanding, I knew what principle guides the Chinese government in handling problems: sympathy for the weak.

When a conflict arises between a big company and homeowners, you have to be very careful. Don't assume that because you're in the right legally, society will side with you. Take a simple example: when the market is bad and you cut prices, homeowners make trouble, right? I'd think: you've already bought your apartment, it's yours; whether I raise or cut prices on new apartments is my business. When I raised prices you didn't complain, because you benefited along the way, right? Now I cut prices and you come to me saying, "Your price cut has hurt me, you have to compensate me" — do I need the government to stop this behavior of theirs?

Between the developer and the customer, who is strong? I am the strong one. So what do you do? You can't not cut prices — the market demands it — but you can't do it with righteous fanfare either. I'd say: "A holiday promotion, surely that's all right?" A disguised price cut, and they won't make trouble like that. That is Chinese culture.

My careless words in the 2008 earthquake also produced the "inflection point" thesis. Where did the inflection point thesis come from? Wasn't it just me cutting prices? That price cut instantly enraged everyone except the central government and people preparing to buy homes. The central government wanted price cuts; my peers didn't; local governments didn't; would-be homeowners didn't; people who had already bought homes didn't either.

Before Harvard, all of this made me furious. Those would-be homeowners who had bought apartments but not yet taken possession were furious too — they made trouble, surrounding Vanke's sales offices, blocking Vanke from selling homes, blocking the people who wanted to buy them. "Unless you compensate me, I won't let you sell."

Worried, we called in the police, saying you have to keep order — this is a market economy, a society ruled by law, you can't just ignore it. The police came. They did nothing.

Was I furious? The police said: as long as no one dies, as long as there's no brawling, we won't intervene.

Why? After I got to Harvard I understood: the principle of sympathy for the weak in traditional Chinese culture made me the strong party. But does that mean we abandon the market economy, abandon entrepreneurial spirit? No. The government handled it that way for its reasons. Only by understanding Chinese culture better, by comparing East and West, did I learn what I should do in China.

What we learn from the West now is mostly at the technical level — their methodology is more advanced than ours, so we take it and apply it. We rarely think from this other level.

![Wang Shi mountaineering in the United States](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607221636odHkKwb48JrcDpGl.jpeg-w600)

At Harvard I had a dual identity: on one hand I was a diligent student; on the other, I was imitating — imitating how professors teach.

What was my purpose in returning to school after a hundred battles? Because I knew my greatest influence would be education — to systematize the experience and theory I had summed up and exchange with students. In 2009 I accepted HKUST's invitation to teach.

I already had the posture of a teacher, so at Harvard I wasn't just sitting in class. I remember I picked a course on the Arab Color Revolution. I pushed the door open: one professor and one student. I thought I'd surely gotten it wrong. The next class I went again — I was the only one. I asked, "Are you the professor?" He said, "Yes."

Only at Harvard did I learn: required courses you must attend; electives are the ones you choose. Many professors run electives under a rule that if at least three students choose your course, you have to teach it.

He told me three students had signed up then; after the first class, the other two had fled, and I was the only one left. Meaning: even with just one student, you still have to teach. That impressed me deeply. I used to think: I'm a famous entrepreneur, I surely have face — my lectures would draw at least 50, and should really fill two or three hundred, so I cared very much about audience size. Only later did I realize: what truly shows your level is whether you can keep teaching as the audience gets smaller. That is the level.

Examples like that really opened a skylight for me.

I took a course on new energy — that one I remember very well. Twenty-odd students. The professor took out a deck of cards and dealt each of us two: one red, one black. He said, let's play a game. Of these two cards, pick one.

Pick red: you get three points, and if everyone else also picks red, you get one extra point. Pick black: you get one point, but for each other person who picks black, you get two more points.

Interesting, right? Because you don't know what the others picked, your choice of red or black depends on what everyone else chooses. Do the math: if you pick red, you score more up front — three points, two more than the others.

In the end I picked black, and it turned out black scored more.

The professor went on to explain two mathematical concepts: first the prisoner's dilemma, then Nash equilibrium. He said today's new energy policy is a prisoner's dilemma. Damn, how brilliant is that, right?

If you asked me to explain the math of the prisoner's dilemma, I'd make it very complicated. From the angle of new energy prices, it's about government subsidies — how much to subsidize, how the government thinks about it, why?

And I thought — damn, holy shit — what is "everyone"? Really brilliant.

After that he went on to derive the mathematical formulas — it didn't matter whether you followed or not. I'd long since forgotten my college calculus, damn it, so I went back and reviewed two months of calculus.

But the moment you feel the excitement, the agony begins right away, because I still had the pressure of homework. Of course, as a visiting scholar I didn't have to do the assignments, but I pressured myself — without pressure, how do you improve? I didn't copy classmates' homework; I borrowed their notes, because I couldn't understand what the professor was saying. My English was mute.

My way of buttering up classmates was treating them to meals. One classmate named Eric — I saw him in Beijing after leaving Harvard, and he asked me, "Do you know how many meals you treated me to? More than 200." Over my two and a half years at Harvard.

At Harvard everything about me was tense, the pressure enormous; I was in a defensive state, strung tight. Doing homework was truly tormenting myself in purgatory. When doing homework I felt even eating was a waste of time — 20 minutes to settle a meal, then back to work until two in the morning. I rarely felt anything was finished.

I am absolutely not that clever; I'm definitely a slow bird. If I were clever, would I have had to go through all that? What "top student"? I never was one, and it doesn't matter.

**Wang Shi first went to Japan in 1986. Subsequent visits began to fascinate him with the question of how an island country the size of China's Yunnan Province became a world industrial power. Vanke's management later drew on the model of postwar Japanese companies such as Sony, Panasonic, and Toyota.**

**But the question Wang Shi never figured out: China and Japan began top-down modernization drives at the same time — why did Japan succeed and China fail? What role did the merchant and industrial class of Japan's Edo period play in the movement? This became one of his research topics at Harvard, and also the subject of the two books due for completion this year.**

I spent two and a half years at Harvard, originally intending to finish my book on Japan — mainly on the Edo period, from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries to the end of the nineteenth, those 260 years. Three threads: first, universal education; second, the intellectual class's preparation for absorbing Western culture; third, a comparison of Chinese and Japanese commerce and industry, and what that period meant for the Self-Strengthening Movement and the Meiji Restoration. I had mostly finished it by 2011 and hoped to complete the book at Harvard.

Sorry about this — I publish a book about every two years, but what I really write is mostly casual reflections. *The Road and the Dream* (《道路与梦想》) chronicles my founding of Vanke, year by year, the annals method. There are books devoted to mountaineering, but they're mostly travel notes — often a week-long trip to Israel could yield a book. Why "sorry"? Because it's a bit shoddily made.

When I left in 2011, a new book had just come out, *Life at Its Heights* (《生命高处》), the third of the so-called soul trilogy. The first was *Let the Soul Catch Up* (《让灵魂跟上脚步》), the second *The Wandering Soul* (《徘徊的灵魂》), the third *Life at Its Heights*. See, there's still a logic to it, right? (laughs)

Damn it — after I got to Harvard, the books wouldn't come out anymore. I wanted this book to be a bit different — after the logic training, to be somewhat academic, more logical and more systematic than before.

But after Harvard I found I couldn't write books anymore. I wanted the standpoint to be different from before, to improve — but you find you can't go back, and what you write is neither fish nor fowl. Look, it's 2018 now and this book is already in its 11th draft, 130,000 characters. One draft won't do, the next won't do either — right up to now.

It's not dissatisfaction; I just find it unreadable. I'm aiming to finish both books this year. Though I can't get them out, it's a progressive kind of stuck — I still feel I'm improving every year.

I gained a great deal at Harvard. After leaving, my state of mind was wanting to go back but not daring to — I didn't return until 2016, and only then realized I cared about myself way too damn much. Why? I felt that after two and a half years away, I had to come back with an answer sheet — I kept wanting to finish that book of mine. Only later did I realize I shouldn't be so full of myself. Who the hell takes you that seriously? Only you.

Cambridge afterward was a completely different story. At Cambridge I stopped doing homework; I stopped forcing myself. With the change of environment, my tense, defensive state relaxed too. At Cambridge no one knew I used to speak in such a stammer — my English is still halting now, but at least those old barriers were gone, and I was less nervous.

At Cambridge I switched to researching the migration history of Jews in East Asia. Many people, including Jews themselves, found my choice very surprising: first, a Chinese businessman studying Jews is odd; second, Jews themselves study modern Jewish issues, so it seemed even stranger.

My logic for choosing it was simple. I had been studying Japan at Harvard, and in comparing Japan I found that Japanese and Chinese views of Jews are very different.

Historically, after their last state was destroyed by Rome 2,000 years ago, the Jews basically migrated across the world — a history of persecution, ending in the Holocaust, genocide.

Chinese people feel quite close to the Jews. First, because China has no history of anti-Semitism — Jews were rejected everywhere in the world, but not in China. Second, we're both pretty good at business. Third, we both care deeply about the extended family. Fourth, we both value education. And during World War II we sheltered and supported Jews, letting more than 20,000 Jewish refugees settle in Shanghai — it creates a sense of kinship.

But after engaging with the academic literature and this history, I found I couldn't agree.

Let me answer it with one story. Jewish communities in China go back more than 1,000 years, to the Northern Song dynasty — there are community records from that time. But what they recorded were not Jews; they were Hui Muslims. They had no idea these people were Jews — so how could you have anti-Semitism? The people did not allow churches to be built in China, but we didn't know Judaism existed either. When it came to destroying churches, can you say we spared the synagogues and destroyed only the Christian and Catholic ones? Was that our history? No.

**Wang Shi's visiting scholarship at Cambridge was interrupted by the Baoneng battle in 2015. In August 2015, Baoneng Group became Vanke's largest shareholder for the first time in 20 years. Four months later, Wang Shi announced that Baoneng was not welcome as a shareholder. Last year, the war wound down temporarily, with Shenzhen Metro becoming Vanke's largest shareholder.**

**The title on the door of Wang Shi's office changed from "Office of the Chairman and General Manager" to "Office of the Chairman," and most recently to "Honorary Chairman of the Board."**

**The story isn't over.**

**On the day he stepped down as chairman, Wang Shi wrote on WeChat Moments: "I've been at Vanke for 34 years, spent 17 of them as a hands-off boss, and now I'm beginning my new 17 years. In another 17 years, I'll only be 83!"**

**Wang Shi believes Seneca's poetry best represents his attitude now:**

**"Disaster is opportunity for the true man. We may rightly say that those dulled by too much good fortune are pitiable — people who could be said to live a tranquil, idle life on a calm sea, and who feel discomfited at the slightest thing."**

My biggest change from 2013 to 2018 still came in 2016. After the Baoneng battle erupted in 2015, more of my energy shifted back home, to handling company affairs.

My main energy used to go to lecturing at universities, to campus visiting scholarships — after 2015 that basically stopped.

Last July I stepped down as Vanke's chairman. My basic time split is 30-30-30-10: 30% philanthropy, 30% BGI, 30% Broad Group, 10% personal time.

Being chairman of BGI is not like Vanke, where I was only a spiritual leader. I have to play a role. The amount of time is different; the businesses involved are different. First I have to understand their industry; second, get familiar with the companies; third, contribute ideas as they develop and get deeply involved in handling matters.

I have to thank the era I was born into — we've entered a long-lived society, and lifespans are longer than before. In the old saying, at seventy one was already a rarity. At 60 you no longer had time to experience things; life was about to close its door on you — how could you perform, what role could you play? But now I still have the chance to experience, to improve my cultivation and understanding, so the visiting scholarships must continue.

This fall I plan to go to the Hebrew University, and afterward to Istanbul University in Turkey. I remember when I went to Harvard in 2011 I set a three-year plan, but I ended up staying two and a half years at Harvard, and one year at Cambridge became two. I'm planning half a year at the Hebrew University, but I estimate it'll take two years too — and then I feel I should spend a year in Iran as well. One mustn't be too greedy.

Also, now that I've retired from Vanke — as an entrepreneur, what am I going to do? I think there's a two-year adjustment period, and one year has already passed.

Leaving requires preparation. It's not that I can't adapt to retirement — how could I not adapt? I've long been the hands-off type, long been a spiritual leader. I do philanthropy, things that have nothing to do with Vanke. So my retirement from Vanke was a seamless handoff.

I can put it this way: from the moment I took up mountaineering and kept my distance from Vanke, I was already preparing for retirement. But the time I had originally planned for was not last year — I retired early. So what will Vanke do after my retirement? And as an entrepreneur, what will I do after leaving Vanke? I still haven't figured it out. But it requires preparation, and the preparation time is two years.

Being optimistic doesn't mean I have no worries. Optimism and worry are two different things. How could there be no worries? No worries — is that life? That's just your expectation. Worry is life. What the worry is doesn't matter; life will have worries no matter what.

To be completely candid: at the 30th anniversary of reform and opening, as a benchmark figure, my biggest takeaway looking back over the 30 years was that I had never expected such great achievements from myself, from my company, from the nation and the country — and I was uncertain about the future.

I remember at Alxa SEE's 2012 annual conference, my speech was titled "Facing an Uncertain Future, Correcting Myself."

This year marks 40 years of reform and opening, and it's the same line — especially for the past ten years, which I could never have imagined would be so full of ups and downs, so magnificent and sweeping.

Facing the future, I've gone from "uncertain" to "cautiously optimistic."

I am optimistic, but it's completely different from ten years ago. Ten years ago I was in trouble and I was uncertain; that uncertainty lasted until 2012.

But by 2018, as I sum up the second decade of reform and opening — what do I worry about? I worry about a great many things, which we won't go into; they're hard to explain anyway, right? Hence "cautiously optimistic."

Where does this optimism come from?

Personally speaking, the Vanke–Baoneng battle now has at least a rough outcome, but the story isn't finished for the future — I'm still watching, and I'm optimistic. Also, having thrown myself into environmental protection, on the international stage, on climate change, I'm one of the benchmark figures in a sense.

Nationally speaking — is our country a big polluter? We have the largest carbon emissions. Are we the ones talking economic development regardless of environmental damage? Environmental protection is now a huge issue — smog, for instance, food safety, and so on.

Before the Paris conference, Obama and Xi Jinping signed a joint declaration in Beijing that laid the foundation for the Paris Agreement. But after the Paris conference, Trump — then newly elected, or not yet elected — stated very clearly that he would withdraw as soon as he took office.

Everyone was stunned. Whom did all eyes turn to? China. China had reason to withdraw, but if China withdrew, the show would be over. Precisely because of China's attitude, many US state governments and many big companies have not stayed aligned with the federal government — tell me, shouldn't we be optimistic?

China's reform has always been top-down, so the future will be top-down too — why not be optimistic?

The poetry of the ancient Roman Seneca is my attitude toward life now. A soldier risks his life to complete a combat mission — facing death, threat, pain, struggle, what is his attitude? The general chose you to carry out this mission; that is your pride and honor. If not me, then who?
