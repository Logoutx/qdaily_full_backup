Francis Alÿs is an urban wanderer who expresses resistance through all kinds of absurd performance art

A tall, thin man appears on the streets of Mexico City. Under the blazing sun he is pushing a huge block of ice. At first he has to bend down low just to force it forward; as the ice melts and shrinks, he switches to kicking it along. The block does not need to be delivered to any particular destination—it leaves trails of water on streets all over the city, which quickly evaporate. After more than nine hours, the ice finally melts into a puddle.

This was a 1997 performance by the artist Francis Alÿs; the man pushing the ice was Alÿs himself. The nine hours were edited into a video of nearly ten minutes, the camera following him as it sweeps past Mexico City's streets, cars, storefronts, and passersby. Some people stare at Alÿs; others quickly look away, pretending they saw nothing. But no one steps in to interrupt him and ask what he is doing.

“In 1997 I had already lived in this neighborhood for more than ten years. The neighbors knew me, knew I was an artist, even if no one knew what kind of art I made.”

Alÿs's first solo exhibition in China recently opened at the Rockbund Art Museum in Shanghai. In an interview he recalled the performance from twenty years ago: nine hours of “labor” produced no artwork in the traditional sense—no painting, no sculpture; even the prop he had prepared before the performance dissolved into nothingness.

![The “ice pusher” in Mexico City, 1997 (Photo / [Francis Alÿs](http://francisalys.com/sometimes-making-something-leads-to-nothing/))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181129093048L3rZBikK7NbSqQ5d.jpg-w600)

The ice-pushing journey was titled *Paradox of Praxis I*, and its subtitle explains the paradox: sometimes making something leads to nothing (*Sometimes Making Something Leads to Nothing*).

The paradox of human action seems to be the enduring theme of Alÿs's work—sometimes all effort comes to nothing, and sometimes the process itself becomes the meaning.

Another of his works, *Rehearsal I*, is on view in the Shanghai Biennale, which opened the same month; in the half-hour video, Alÿs drives a red Volkswagen Beetle in a performance of “climbing.”

The hill is in Tijuana, Mexico. Alÿs's soundtrack is a brass band: when the melody turns urgent the car accelerates uphill; when it relaxes, the car halfway up rolls back down. After failure upon failure, the car exits the frame entirely.

Engels used the “five social formations” theory to describe human society's linear progression—but in Latin America, has society really progressed? This also resonates with the theme of this Shanghai Biennale, *Proregress*: perhaps “progress” in the narrow sense is humanity's greatest illusion.

![*Rehearsal I* — the red Beetle that never made it over the hill (Photo / [Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/whats-on/tate-modern/exhibition/francis-alys/francis-alys-story-deception-room-guide/francis-alys-9))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018112909354785GZI7O32vV0Ysdc.jpg-w600)

### Mexico City, That Belgian

Alÿs has lived in Mexico City for more than thirty years, yet people can still spot him as an outsider at a glance. “I'm too tall, too white, I look too much like a foreigner”—even though he has lived there longer than most of his neighbors.

In 1985, Mexico City was struck by a 7.8-magnitude earthquake. The next year, Alÿs arrived in Mexico as a volunteer with a national rescue team; volunteer service could count toward exemption from Belgium's mandatory military service.

His education up to that point included architecture and engineering. In his first two years in Mexico, his main work was repairing the city's earthquake-destroyed water system. When his “service” ended, he decided to stay and become an artist.

At first, the architect's career change did not go smoothly. To make ends meet, he painted apartments for others and worked as a billboard painter.

“Young people often ask me today how to create a style of one's own. I think that wasn't a question for me thirty years ago—there was no contemporary art in Mexico back then; nobody knew what contemporary art was, myself included. That was a good thing for a creator—no scruples at all. Many young people today are far more worldly than I was, but an excess of information can bring some obstacles,” Alÿs told QDaily.

The first presentable work was a homemade “toy dog” from 1991. Its construction was extremely simple: a rectangular block with a built-in magnet and four rubber wheels. For a stretch of time, Alÿs walked it on a leash, solemnly wandering the city every day as though it meant something. The toy dog was named *The Collector*; its collection, of course, was not expensive treasures bought at auction but all kinds of refuse discarded by modern life.

“This magnetic collector shuttles through the streets every day, and the metal objects it gathers quickly cover its entire body. It keeps attracting, until one day the collector is completely submerged by its own spoils.” In the edited nine-minute video, Alÿs offers this explanation.

![Alÿs with the toy dog named “The Collector” behind him (Photo /[Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/whats-on/tate-modern/exhibition/francis-alys/francis-alys-story-deception-room-guide/francis-alys-0))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181129095011DCd6OIn8VZ4rkSyR.jpg-w600)

In fact, the toy dog's urban walks did not yield much. [With limited magnetic power, its spoils amounted to only a few beer bottle caps](https://www.nytimes.com/2011/05/06/arts/design/francis-alys-a-story-of-deception-at-moma-review.html), yet this work of conceptual art still made Alÿs's name in the international art world. Around the end of the Cold War, the fate of many people was no different from these bottle caps—they all became waste products of modernity flying the banners of “development,” “progress,” and “globalization.”

Interestingly, this sits in some conflict with the architecture industry Alÿs once worked in. By the 1960s, modernism had become the mainstream of architecture; designers believed that well-functioning, well-planned spaces could shape a modernized way of life, but Alÿs chose to swim against the current.

“When I decided to leave architecture, my first impulse was not to try to add something to this city, but to look for things that were already there—to look for the ‘residue’ left after the city has digested something, or to look for those negative spaces, gaps, and in-between zones.”

### Wandering as a Political Resistance

Alÿs's home in Mexico City is near the main square downtown, the Zócalo (Plaza de la Constitución)—the square's transformations contain Mexico's colonial history. In ancient times this was sacred ground where the Mexica held rituals and ceremonies. When the Spanish colonizers invaded in the sixteenth century, the temples were destroyed and replaced with a Catholic cathedral. The “constitution” in the square's name refers to the Spanish Constitution of 1812. Today, the Metropolitan Cathedral (Catedral Metropolitana) on the square's north side remains the largest Catholic cathedral in all of Latin America.

The Zócalo is Mexico City's most important public space; at the fences along its edges, job seekers often gather, each displaying his or her trade—fixing pipes, painting, or unclogging drains. One day in 1994, Alÿs stood among this crowd; the sign he put out read “Tourist” (*Turista*).

![What can hiring a “tourist” get you? (Photo / [Stedelijk Museum](https://www.stedelijk.nl/en/collection/82250-francis-alys-turista))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181129100247u2oXvNwdmVGIeZ0S.jpg-w600)

In 1994, the North American Free Trade Agreement formally took effect, and the Mexican peso quickly lost half its value. People called the economic crisis the “Tequila Effect”; a presidential candidate was also assassinated at a campaign event.

In the midst of social and economic crisis, what service can hiring a tourist buy? An outsider's confusion, an “advanced perspective” standing in for the experience of the developed West, or secondhand knowledge from a travel guide?

Alÿs also portrays Mexican society through street photography.

Around 2000, for instance, he shot a series on street vendors (*Ambulantes*): their humble carts carried loads wildly disproportionate to their bodies, piled high and teetering. Alÿs called them a disorganized “circus,” saying the city as a place of exchange becomes interesting because of these ordinary people who perform “juggling” every day. Of course, as a stubbornly conservative folk theater, Mexico City has not only jugglers but also the municipal officers ever ready to regulate the streets.

Around 2011, he photographed another series, this one on street sleepers and stray dogs.

Compared with these somewhat plain street photographs, Alÿs's greater energy lies hidden in the modern fables he weaves.

Between 2003 and 2006, he captured a mirage in Patagonia, in southern Argentina. The camera pushes forward along the highway; viewers can see the wonder before them, but however fast or slow they go, it remains forever visible and forever out of reach.

At the Rockbund exhibition, Alÿs paired the piece with a short text explaining his inspiration: the Tehuelche, Patagonia's indigenous people, once hunted the rhea—known locally as the ñandú—by a particular method, simply walking behind it until it was exhausted. This hunting method has long been extinct.

But when what people chase is a mirage, who is it that ends up exhausted?

Alÿs connects this fable with Argentina's 2001 “Corralito” crisis. To prevent capital flight, the Argentine government at the time restricted residents from withdrawing cash at banks; the monthly withdrawal limit from checking accounts was 1,500 pesos.

The blueprint the government had sketched was like a mirage, unable to honor its promise of economic development; people were penned into the corral like cattle, unable to go anywhere. Alÿs titled the footage *A Story of Deception* (Patagonia).

Alÿs also spent ten years, over and over, chasing tornadoes across Mexico. At the Rockbund exhibition, visitors can lie down and watch the tornadoes he filmed with a handheld camera.

The climatic anomalies born of Latin America's unique geography often supply material for Alÿs's work, while Latin America's marginalized position in the global economic system is a topic he wants people to pay attention to: in these places where modernity has failed, how do people live with themselves?

### “Poetic Politics”

Alÿs's questioning is not an isolated case; unfinished modernity is a theme shared by much Latin American art, and the chaotic political and social realities spur people to reflect.

In recent years, these reflections on whether progress is real have also gained attention in the international art market. In September this year, for instance, [Art Basel extended its map of the art world to Argentina](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/56721.html). And in this Shanghai Biennale, the large contingent of Latin American artists likewise presented a range of social and political issues.

Alÿs's fables have always buried a political thread. Ten years ago, he held a solo exhibition at David Zwirner's gallery in New York: “Sometimes doing something poetic can become political, and sometimes doing something political can become poetic” (*Sometimes Doing Something Poetic Can Become Political, and Sometimes Doing Something Political Can Become Poetic*).

This year's solo show in Shanghai is themed *La dépense* (“Consumption”). The curator, Yuko Hasegawa—director of the Museum of Contemporary Art Tokyo—said in an interview that this concept, drawn from the French philosopher Georges Bataille, runs through Alÿs's artistic practice.

“In the globalized context of capitalism and the development of convenient technology, excessive ‘non-productive consumption’ is as important to human activity as ‘productive consumption.’ This idea happens to be tightly bound up with Alÿs's artistic practice: the courage to confront social realities head-on, to cast doubt on the visual senses, and to rethink the meaning of physical labor and the expenditure of time.”

In 2002, another Alÿs performance caused a sensation. On the outskirts of Lima, capital of Peru, Alÿs hired five hundred volunteers whose job was shoveling sand, in an attempt to move a sand dune together. In the end, the dune really did move several feet—but the moved dune was indistinguishable from the original one.

Critics said Alÿs wanted to express the relationship between the superpowers and Latin American societies: the former hands the latter rubber checks promising economic and social progress—but has real progress come? What did they expend?

![Francis Alÿs, *A Story of Deception, Patagonia, Argentina*, 2003–2006, 16mm film projection, color, silent, 4 minutes 20 seconds, on loop, in collaboration with Olivier Debroise and Rafael Ortega](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181129103938r2c7N5wk6dq0L94V.jpg-w600)

![*Reel-Unreel*, video still (Photo /[Francis Alÿs](http://francisalys.com/reel-unreel/))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181129104112DsqBhV95KiWCz3ed.jpg-w600)

In recent years, some of Alÿs's projects engage regional politics more directly, but their form of expression is no longer the fable, no longer wrapped in layers of symbols and metaphors—instead it has become more concrete and direct.

In 2010, after traveling through Afghanistan, he took part in the thirteenth edition of documenta in Kassel. The film he shot there, *Reel-Unreel*, is something like a documentary.

The work's inspiration came from a common street game—hoop rolling, which Afghan children also often play in the streets. In filming, Alÿs substituted a reel of film stock for the hoop. The children split into two groups, one going uphill and one downhill, running after the film with curiosity.

Afghanistan's war had transformed the landscape of the region; shantytowns and makeshift shelters were everywhere. But Alÿs's camera remains relatively calm: the children pass through the streets of Kabul, past the old city, the bazaar, the riverside docks, and the garbage heaps—where ruins of buildings destroyed by war coexist with rebuilt lives.

At the end of the film, a title card appears: on September 5, 2001, the Taliban confiscated thousands of reels of film from the Afghan Film Archive and burned them on the outskirts of Kabul; people say the fire burned for 15 days and nights. But the Taliban did not know that most of them were copies—reproductions.

In that 2007 solo exhibition, Alÿs posted a passage on the wall expressing some self-doubt: can artistic intervention really bring about unprecedented ways of thinking? Can a single absurd act really stimulate people to break out of habitual thinking, shatter their original assumptions, and search anew for the source of conflict? Can performance art like this really bring change?

Ritual, war, spectacle, game… what is the meaning of these acts? Alÿs reminds people that what can be done is to stay vigilant for the long haul.
