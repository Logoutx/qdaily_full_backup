"Sticker pack" illustrators are hugely popular on Chinese-language social media right now. Before we introduce WeChat sticker illustrators, let's first take a look at what this trade has been through in Taiwan. Below is a firsthand report from Taiwan's most popular illustrators.

Thanks to the internet and social media, it has become easier for illustrators to become famous in Taiwan.

On social media platforms such as LINE, some illustrators have created wildly popular original sticker sets — what we usually call "sticker packs." In May 2014, LINE launched its Creators Market, an open platform where anyone can sell the stickers they design. In two years, there were more than 570,000 creators worldwide; creators from Taiwan accounted for ten percent — roughly 50,000 — and [the annual growth rate of creators was 73%](https://linecorp.com/zh-hant/pr/news/zh-hant/2016/1416). In 2015 alone, the top five best-selling sticker packs in Taiwan averaged sales of about 7.76 million Taiwan dollars (1.55 million yuan) per set.

**Just how hot are these "sticker packs" now?**

Consider a set of numbers. Below are the Facebook fan counts of Taiwan's top five online illustrators, along with the well-known sticker characters they created:

1. Duncan: 3.17 million fans; famous character: Duncan

2. Bye Bye Jojo: 1.51 million fans; famous characters: Pointy Head, Michael the Cat, Ole Chicken

3. Mr. H.H: 1.44 million fans; famous character: Meimei

4. Rolling Eyes, Miss Undine: 1.09 million fans; famous character: Undine

5. Cherng: 1.05 million fans; famous character: the Malayan tapir

In short, each of these "illustrators" has more than one million Facebook fans. The most popular of them, Duncan, has been around for three years and ranks fifth among all Facebook fan pages in Taiwan — ahead of virtually every Taiwanese celebrity except Mayday.

People love following illustrators' updates, not only because the content they discuss is close to the lives of ordinary folk, but more importantly because these illustrations give them something to chat about with friends, or a source of healing, amid busy lives.

Duncan is a boy who wears glasses and shorts; he looks remarkably ordinary, a bit like Nobita from *Doraemon*, with a goofy air. But Duncan is actually full of wit — anything trendy gets a playful twist in his hands.

Made of simple black-and-white lines and modeled on the animal of the same name, it has a slightly slacker personality, and its motto is "accomplishing nothing." Though its appearance has a dumb-cute charm, its personality is often cheeky — a contrast that Taiwanese netizens find especially endearing.

Today, characters created by online illustrators have become a new crop of "2D stars" in Taiwan's merchandise market, changing a landscape once dominated by American and Japanese characters.

In June this year, the well-known Danish craft beer brand Mikkeller approached Taiwanese illustrator Cherng for a crossover collaboration designing a beer label, releasing a Taiwan-exclusive *Malayan Tapir's Beer Dream — Mikkeller's Floating Foam Beer* (《马来貘的啤酒梦之米凯乐漂浮奶泡啤酒》).

Small brands aren't the only ones hoping to borrow these illustrators' influence — big brands are too. In May this year, Coca-Cola also teamed up with four Taiwanese illustrators — Duncan, Bye Bye Jojo, Wanwan, and Lu's — to launch limited-edition illustrated bottles, sold in Taiwan's convenience stores.

**Why do brands favor these characters that haven't yet stood the test of time?**

[One illustrator, Yang Jianxiong, explained in a media interview](http://www.ccci.tku.edu.tw/index.php/tw/2012-05-11-06-17-26/interview/987-banna): "Foreign characters are well known, but they often come with licensing restrictions; on top of that, budgets are limited, so you have even less negotiating leverage, and there's relatively less flexibility in how the character can be used. Taiwanese characters, by contrast, have their own fan bases thanks to the power of social platforms. The influence of amateur (everyday) illustrators is on the rise — they're more cooperative, the scope of collaboration is broader, and prices are more flexible — so companies are relatively more willing to work with them."

For illustrators, turning their work into merchandise is certainly a good thing — but some more time- and labor-consuming hassles await down the road, such as negotiating terms with clients or handling legal matters. And because brands see commercial value in these "sticker packs," illustrators, just like performing artists, have signed with talent agencies.

Illustrators Cherng and Bye Bye Jojo, along with Song Song Meow, all belong to HIM International Music. In less than three years since joining, they have brought the company average annual output value of more than 200 million Taiwan dollars. This output comes from a wide variety of sources: image licensing, merchandise development, brand co-branding, publications, or digital content such as videos made in collaboration with brands.

The company originally focused on artist management, with well-known acts including S.H.E, Yoga Lin, and Aaron Yan; it only entered illustration management in 2014. "Song Song Meow also has a way with words — in the future he could try writing lyrics together with our artists. Jojo keeps challenging himself with singing, and Cherng is interested in travel and cultural observation. These are all spaces where they can stretch beyond illustration," HIM International said.

HIM International told QDaily that since it began managing illustrators, it has regularly received unsolicited applications from design and fine-arts graduates over the past two years — many of whom already run fan pages featuring their illustration work.

Compared with Jimmy Liao, Taiwan's representative illustrator of the past, everything has changed for illustrators: their platforms, their ways of working, and what kinds of illustrations and characters are more likely to catch on.

Most illustrators of Jimmy Liao's generation or earlier drew illustrations for newspapers and magazines on a part-time basis. They had no dedicated channel of exposure and relied on children's books or newspaper supplements. After Jimmy Liao published his work in picture-book form in 1998, the industry underwent its first change: illustration became the protagonist of a publication rather than decoration for the text. In 2000, Li Yushan founded Jimmy S.P.A. Inc., which elevated Jimmy Liao to the level of a brand; they began releasing licensed merchandise and pursuing crossover collaborations in film, stage musicals, and more.

Thanks to the openness of social media platforms, the illustration industry underwent yet another change.

Some people who were never in the industry at all have jumped into sticker creation. Yang Jianxiong created a sticker set called Banana Man, which became the top download in the very first month the LINE Creators Market opened. He wasn't originally an illustrator — he was an engineer.

However, these newly minted sticker stars should note that without a Facebook-style fan page, both illustrator and sticker packs may struggle to stay hot. (Correspondingly, WeChat official accounts are playing a similar role.) Duncan chose to create his fan page in 2014 precisely because he was influenced by the fan page of another illustrator, "Rolling Eyes, Miss Undine," when he suddenly realized there was a platform where he could properly manage his own work.

In hindsight, that proved to be exactly the right call.

Despite his high download numbers, Yang Jianxiong knows full well that stickers alone can't sustain a character's vitality. After creating his stickers, he set up a Facebook fan page and, like other online illustrators, began posting image-text updates nonstop; he even released a dedicated photo app — all to keep the character from being forgotten too quickly.

Hollywood studios long understood this trick: they paired their properties with animated films, TV series, theme parks, and merchandise. But the internet has plainly changed the path to creating that sense of being "everywhere."

**Q: Are lots of illustrators now treating the LINE sticker (sticker pack) platform as a springboard? Could you share your thoughts on why stickers have become so popular?**

> Cherng: It's great to have more channels for creators to emerge. Younger creators have come to me for advice, and I felt the mechanism of rising through Facebook might no longer work that well — after all, they've changed the algorithm — so I suggested they draw stickers.
> My first sticker set went on sale at the end of 2013; it was Taiwan's first paid sticker set (laughs), because back then Taiwan didn't have a single paid sticker set, so I pitched LINE myself, saying our popularity was already very high. LINE ultimately agreed because Wanwan (another Taiwanese sticker illustrator) had previously released a free sticker set in collaboration with a kiwi brand that was downloaded in huge numbers, which opened the door to cooperation, and they approached Wanwan and me to collaborate on stickers.
> For creators, being able to make stickers on LINE back then was a remarkable thing — like a star getting to perform at the Hong Kong Coliseum. I drew every single one carefully; it was an important milestone.
> Song Song Meow: Creating on the LINE platform is very much about the emotion of using the stickers — the emotion runs stronger, and it demands more innovation. But the turnover rate is also high, and a sticker that's handy to use isn't necessarily easy to print onto merchandise, so that's another challenge — after all, sustaining popularity is hard. And a viral sticker lasts a week at most; on Facebook, things last longer.
> Bye Bye Jojo: Let me add — I was Taiwan's first official voice sticker set (laughs). I think the sticker market lets creators enter the world of characters very quickly, because if a character is really going to make money, it's nothing more than licensing.
> When we write the chapters of a story, it doesn't always connect with what brands have in mind. But if a sticker's image is complete and its emotion is full, I think that's a fast route from original stickers into the character-licensing market. If a brand just wants to print one image onto a product, the fastest way is to look at stickers — I've looked at quite a few sets myself, and now I'll seek out younger-generation creators to collaborate with. It's a good direction.

**Q: Are stickers that get replaced quickly a good thing for illustrators themselves?**

> Cherng: Our creative focus is really on the character itself — we pay more attention to the character, because a sticker has a hard time emphasizing a character's personality, so the sticker's image becomes very important.
> Bye Bye Jojo: It depends on the purpose. Some very popular characters — at first we don't even know who the creator is. Of course, if it can be combined with the author's personality, that's a bonus; if not, you just have to maintain the character's quality.

**Q: In the early days, many illustrators were known more for their style, which differs from today's online illustrators, who all create strong, memorable characters. Do you think this is now the easier route to fame for online illustrators?**

> Song Song Meow: I think nowadays people look for illustrators on Facebook, but because Facebook's reading experience is so simple, nobody reads articles that are too long — the same goes for images that are overly artsy. That doesn't mean such illustrators have disappeared, though — for instance, there are still many amazing illustrators on Pinterest and Tumblr.
> Bye Bye Jojo: It depends on the subject. If an illustrator's image tells a story, it should still get shared. For a while, everyone was passing around an illustrator's work of the Facebook logo as a metaphor for smartphone addicts, and people shared that too. I think it mainly comes down to the presentation. A gorgeous landscape image, for a Facebook user, of course just gets swiped past — but paired with some text, people may share it. So what Facebook readers share is whatever resonates with them.

**Q: How do you think you differ from illustrators of the early publishing era?**

> Cherng: It's sometimes strange to be called an illustrator, because this industry is too new — so new there's no proper job title for it. I find it odd to describe myself as an illustrator; it's hard to define. For me, compared with earlier illustrators, we're a bit like the difference between pop music and classical music. But I think we're all creators — the difference is only in the name; fundamentally there's no difference.
> Song Song Meow: I like picture-book illustrators, like Jimmy Liao. I think the old way of publishing picture books and beautifully bound books was wonderful — they have a tactile quality and texture. Even better, they might spend a year or two focusing on a single book, and that book's emotion runs deep, with a strong worldview. On Facebook, what we offer is more of a sense of companionship. These two constructions are quite different: one is "I build a world and ask readers to accept it," the other is "we walk together."
> Bye Bye Jojo: It's not about making comparisons, but I think we see ourselves more as character designers than illustrators. Kai Xi of the early era — they emphasized backgrounds, then highlighted figures. But nowadays quite a few people call us image-text creators, which shows that the "text" part is a very important mode of expression for us.

**Q: At this stage, what do you think is the most important element for a character to succeed?**

> Cherng: Personality. Without a clear personality, people won't like it. Though actually, at first I wasn't that deliberate about crafting the character's personality. The early fan-page posts just leaned toward satire or slice-of-life narratives. Later I started drawing the Malayan tapir attacking Yuan Zai (the panda at Taipei Zoo), mainly because both are black-and-white animals, and I thought it was quite fun at the time. And the Malayan tapir has that lazy, laid-back personality; plus everyone's busy with life, so I often drew it lazing around at home — accomplishing nothing. Most characters on the market are fairly agreeable, so this attracted fans who want to be different from everyone else.
> Song Song Meow: I also think it's personality, but because it's through the Facebook platform, the creator's personality has an influence too. Creator and character can complement each other. I'm different from the other two — my personality is warmer, more healing, and Song Song Meow's creation was originally meant to encourage myself, but I unexpectedly found many exam-takers coming to read it, so the image was gradually established. Plus many Taiwanese people share the same anxieties — feeling they have to dutifully take exams and go to work — so Song Song Meow got attention because it voiced what was on everyone's mind.
> Bye Bye Jojo: For me, the most important thing for a character is its visual image. If you see a circle with two circles at the upper left and right, you naturally think of Mickey Mouse. A character's image can't be too complex — black-and-white coloring makes you think of the Malayan tapir; for me, you see anything pointy and you think of my character. That's certainly a crucial part of managing a character, and simple lines sit closer to cute.
> Precisely because these are simple-line creations, beyond mainstream image-licensing collaborations, it's also easier to combine them with physical forms.

**Q: What traits do you think draw fans to follow you?**

> Cherng: I founded my fan page in 2012, and within three or four months it had ten or twenty thousand fans. There were really very few people doing this kind of image-text work back then, so it grew fast. It's different now — competition is too fierce. Over the course of running it, tying posts to current events drove fast growth. The "Yuan Zai incident" took me from over 200,000 fans to over 500,000 — though it wasn't especially about pandering to fans, just about winning resonance. Keeping your own personality matters more.
> Song Song Meow: At first I ran Instagram, and through Instagram I got to know the Malayan tapir, who encouraged me to start a fan page. At first I worried no one would look, but it went quite smoothly — a thousand fans on day one — so I felt I could really start doing this.
> Because Song Song Meow gives people more of a healing feeling, it soothes them after a day of work and busyness. At events, the attendees were mostly exam-takers and nursing students, and mostly women. One Japanese mother was amazing: for Song Song Meow's third anniversary, she custom-ordered packaging with Japan's Thunder chocolate and mailed a whole batch from Japan to Taiwan.
> Bye Bye Jojo: Back in the Wretch days (note: an early Taiwanese blogging platform), the popular drawing style wasn't about finding a gag to draw — people drew slice-of-life narratives. I started with gag-finding from the Wretch era — stuff like teaching everyone how to avoid wardrobe malfunctions, very trivial things.
> My fans skew toward guys and "older sisters," because my work touches on gender issues; early on it was racier, which guys and women over 25 were more able to accept. Now it's because I started making videos. The male-female ratio is about 4:6. Some people used to complain my work was trashy, yet they'd keep coming back — and after a while they'd say, "Today's Jojo didn't let me down."
> Speaking of which, the turning point in running the fan page was starting to make videos. Our early cohort of fan-page founders all grew at about the same pace — around 500,000 two years ago — but once I made videos, my numbers jumped right up.
> The reason I make videos is that what I created on Wretch was long-form, while Facebook's browsing mechanism suits single image-text posts, so the storytelling changed. Videos let me keep a long-form rhythm. Most importantly, nobody was doing it back then. My creative process is: whatever everyone else is doing, I try not to do the same.

**Q: Many well-known online illustrators share a common trait — they create work tied to current events. Do the three of you feel this has become a formulaic move?**

> Cherng: I'm quite wary of that, so my line is: if someone else has drawn it, I don't draw it, and I also pick my subjects — if the tone doesn't really fit, I probably won't do it. I think I've already stopped chasing current events that closely now; I'd rather develop the character's own stories.
> Song Song Meow: I prefer things with feeling. For example, we have licensing in Japan — Japanese readers obviously don't know us, but we can collaborate with face masks or FamilyMart on the strength of a certain feeling. That said, I do observe online trends — it's just that Song Song Meow's way of observing the internet leans toward touching people's hearts. Plus I observe how users behave — many people are into handwritten lettering nowadays, so Song Song Meow re-creates through that approach.
> Bye Bye Jojo: If I engage with current events, I always combine them with gender issues — I turn the event into a backdrop. Sometimes when I'm thinking of gags, I imagine how other creators would draw them, and in the end I steer away from those directions.
> **Q: In the course of your work, have your ideas about the illustrator profession changed?**Cherng: I used to think illustration meant the small pictures attached to print publications, but after getting into this and encountering characters, I discovered so many directions it can develop — it can go from flat to three-dimensional.
> Song Song Meow: We used to lock ourselves in a room drawing. These past few years, Taiwan's illustration scene has been fascinating, with many incredible things happening — for instance, the three of us held an exhibition together, and the same goes for character licensing: it started with just drawing something, but because the illustration got noticed, we get to learn ways to make the thing more complete, creating characters through different media.
> Bye Bye Jojo: I think this field has changed a lot in the past two or three years, but we're all still walking the road Japan walked before — the profession of character management is only just beginning. In the future there may be regional associations — like how Ghibli makes you think of Mitaka City.
