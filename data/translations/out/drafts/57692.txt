“Everyone has their own marginality—I’m looking for that thing”

During the 10 minutes of club dancing, some people were really into it, some swayed in place to the music, but plenty of others looked stiff and refused to join in—anyone who walked in off the street would have assumed this was some nightclub, and it seemed impossible to connect it with theater. But this really was a theatrical performance, and the people who took part—and the people who refused—were ordinary audience members.

It was the evening of October 20, the third day after the opening of the Wuzhen Theatre Festival, and 《如鱼得水》 (*Like a Fish in Water*, the second piece in 《老妖精》/*The Old Goblin*) was being performed at the Shen Family theater. The traditional stage had become a sound console, the seats had been torn out, and the space had been turned into a dance club. A huge fish tank stood in the middle, curtains had been rigged up on either side of the space, and video played continuously: goldfish swimming in the water.

![A Wuzhen performance still from *Like a Fish in Water* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181025213515fhOAgyv71EIxZpkD.jpg-w600)

Two audience members couldn’t accept this kind of “doesn’t look like theater” performance, and shouted loudly for refunds near the entrance. Director Lyu Yuzhou handed them the microphone, hoping they would say why they objected.

Unfortunately, the two were too worked up to have an equal dialogue with the creators, and to make sure the rest of the performance went smoothly, Lyu agreed to refund their tickets and asked them to coordinate with the staff after leaving.

Two girls next to me were whispering: “So if we demand a refund now, will they give us one too?” I asked them whether they really wanted to leave. After confirming I wasn’t a crew member, they relaxed and told me they didn’t actually want to leave—they were just guessing whether the refund-demanding audience members were plants.

![A Wuzhen performance still from *Like a Fish in Water* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201810252155298gUrkBJV3EIQeFH7.jpg-w600)

If you don’t usually have much exposure to contemporary art, confusion, bewilderment, even anger are all understandable at this moment. The two refund-seeking audience members may simply have reached the limit of their patience—and not just because of those 10 minutes.

Before that, we had also watched 《孕》 (*Pregnancy*), the first work contained in *The Old Goblin*; together with *Like a Fish in Water*, it makes up *The Old Goblin*.

To be precise, “The Old Goblin” is the name of the troupe to which the two directors, Lin Cuixi (*Pregnancy*) and Lyu Yuzhou (*Like a Fish in Water*), belong—not the actual title of the show. This past June, these two works premiered together with 《生存者之书》 (*The Book of the Survivor*, dir. Lydia Lai) at McaM (Ming Contemporary Art Museum) in Shanghai.

At the *Pregnancy* venue, the audience sat on the floor, a table was placed in the center, and a pot of water was boiling on it. An actor tossed in a slaughtered chicken and threw in chunks of chopped ginger.

Amid the gurgling of the chicken soup, with no clear character relationships, almost no dialogue, just a few fragmentary utterances, and no real scenes between actors… what director Lin Cuixi wanted to explore was the identity of the mother, the female body—or, one might say, the female animal.

![A Wuzhen performance still from *Pregnancy* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018102521541742WmSBUdwaRYpkle.jpg-w600)

As the performance neared its end, a chicken, chased by an actor, flew low without warning through the gap between me and the audience members beside me, and in that instant I screamed.

Director Lin Cuixi did her undergraduate degree in computational mathematics and theater at UCLA, followed by a graduate degree in directing at East 15 Acting School in London. She later explained to me: “If it were a dog, it would seem very docile, tamed. But this is a bird—you can’t communicate with a chicken; it flies, it shits.”

“When an audience member consciously reflects, ‘I felt terror at a chicken,’ that’s really a sensation produced by their own body. There are certain moments when you feel your own body mutating into something animal, a state that can’t be interpreted.”

If you view *Pregnancy* as contemporary art, everything about it is legitimate. The creators have their own creative intent, but the audience’s understanding is never the only one; it points toward the self. Although many people felt they couldn’t understand it, *Pregnancy* is a carefully rehearsed performance involving only the actors—apart from that live chicken, everything was controllable.

![Lin Cuixi and Lyu Yuzhou “walking the chicken” in Wuzhen (photo: Huang Zhihao)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181025220540mXJ9GzFUSaCB7ViT.jpg-w600)

By comparison, what the audience went through in *Like a Fish in Water* was a bit much—at the start the director handed out little dried fish to everyone, picked one audience member and asked her on the spot “whether she was currently in an intimate relationship,” then came 10 minutes of club dancing, then someone shouting for a refund… It all looked too much like performance art.

Which reactions were authentic, in-the-moment responses? Which were rehearsed performances? Too many things defied common sense, the information exploded in a short span of time, and everyone seemed to be in a state of not knowing. More precisely: when contemporary art walks into the theater and faces a theater audience, not everyone is prepared.

The deafening music and dazzling lights of the dance party vanished without a trace; the audience tried to pull themselves away from the distraction of the refund incident and gathered around the huge fish tank in the center of the space, watching two of the actors perform.

![A Wuzhen performance still from *Like a Fish in Water* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026092049m6OeKCZMlBpfck0b.jpg-w600)

*Like a Fish in Water* is an experimental live piece about “suffocating intimate relationships.” Director Lyu Yuzhou holds a bachelor’s in psychology and sociology from Cambridge and a graduate degree in directing from East 15 Acting School in London. From late April to early June this year, with the support of McaM in Shanghai, she and her collaborator worked as researchers in a villa owned by the museum, conducting eight sets of experiments.

The experiments were conducted in pairs of two. Couples, close friends, even complete strangers were invited into a small room with basic food and water, where they could use a toilet—but other than that there was nothing. The spiritual crutch of contemporary people—the mobile phone—was not allowed inside. They had to accept the challenge of living together for 24 hours with almost no personal space.

There are quite a few similar formats abroad—for example, Marina Abramović’s [*The Artist Is Present*](https://www.moma.org/calendar/exhibitions/964) in 2010. She sat silently at a wooden table in a museum for 736 hours (30 days), staring expressionlessly at any audience member willing to sit across from her. But when the subjects of the experiment are not the artist herself but ordinary people, even what looks like a single short day becomes much more complicated.

![*The Artist Is Present* (image from [vimeo](https://i.vimeocdn.com/video/447032231_1280x720.jpg))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026105411Gzsxf7XjONFVtJoL.jpg-w600)

Lyu Yuzhou told the audience that some pairs maintained a safe distance the whole time; two strangers who hadn’t known each other began a flirtation after the experiment; one person slept 19 of the 24 hours out of sheer boredom; someone tried to escape and was dragged back.

At the Wuzhen Theatre Festival, this space was made into a giant fish tank, and everyone could peer at the two people through the transparent glass. The two actors who entered the tank were a real-life couple and also the second pair of experimental subjects. When Lyu Yuzhou first met them, they had been together less than a year; she later chose them to return to the theater as non-professional actors.

Video at the venue showed interviews with them from May this year, along with surveillance footage; a man’s and a woman’s voices expressed their own understandings of intimate relationships. The sound and image were not always in sync—Lyu Yuzhou and her collaborator Shen Bolun were the ones performing the voices. The relationship between creator and performer was juxtaposed; they freely commented on the pair in the experiment, projecting their own imaginings onto them.

![Live video (photo taken by the author)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026105644cjhOvwYurRiT1g4I.jpg-w600)

Inside the tank there was a toilet, 4 apples, 2 bottles of water, and 1 roll of toilet paper. They hugged, kissed, entwined, fell to the ground, separated… on loop. No lines, no plot—what the audience could perceive was only emotion and feeling. On site, Lyu Yuzhou kept passing the microphone to audience members watching the pair in the tank: “What do you see?”

Some began to find themselves, searching for reflections of reality: “I see my own pathetic self.” “I don’t think they’re ever truly together for a single moment—(intimacy) is unattainable.”

Someone became deeply invested: “I feel like we’re the ones being experimented on. Maybe we’re your experimental subjects, and you’ll use our answers in the next show.” Indeed, if you looked up, many people on the second floor hadn’t directly participated at all—they had been looking down at everything the whole time.

Someone else noticed the changes around them: “I see fewer and fewer people.” After a pause, this audience member who had been very active during the dance-party moment offered the director a piece of advice: “The music you used is too old—club dancing hasn’t been like this for ages.” The director burst out laughing. “Sorry, I’m new to club dancing.”

![A performance still from *Like a Fish in Water* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026105723ntJrmUGk3o4BvRlC.jpg-w600)

When one person was asked “why did you come see this show,” she moved the microphone away and quietly asked the director, “Can I tell the truth?” After getting an affirmative answer, she said honestly, “Because I wanted to see *Teahouse* and couldn’t get a ticket.” Including the director herself, no one felt awkward—everyone thought the answer was adorable. Polite white lies had no place here; the performance of *Like a Fish in Water* was itself an exposure of the self.

This is a performance that requires the audience to take part, but when it appeared at the Wuzhen Theatre Festival, it drew considerable controversy, and reactions were polarized—even though the specially invited productions had in fact been categorized for everyone: *The Old Goblin* belongs to “Theater Metamorphoses,” a label telling audiences that the works under it are not “theater” in the traditional sense.

It seems the audience’s expectations diverged from the festival’s “diversity.” When the boundaries of drama and theater blur, both creators and audiences face challenges: one side needs to provide more guidance, the other a more tolerant mindset.

Beyond “drama in my eyes shouldn’t be like this,” some also questioned whether the creators had prepared sufficiently—an accomplished performance, it seems, shouldn’t allow so many unexpected incidents.

But in Lyu Yuzhou’s eyes, the audience is also an important performer in the work; everything that happens on site constitutes an irreproducible part of the piece. *Like a Fish in Water* makes the audience choose: dance together or watch silently from the sidelines, stay or leave, exit quietly or loudly demand a refund.

![A Wuzhen performance still from *Like a Fish in Water* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201810261058560RJS42O1fUrFImLA.jpg-w600)

Beyond the audience’s reactions, the creators themselves were also implicated—some actively, some passively. Near the end of the performance, the line “The fish must die” was played on loop; it came from an unexpected incident at the premiere.

Lyu Yuzhou’s collaborator Shen Bolun publicly accused her of merely using him to push the experiment forward, of having no real desire to explore anything, because she already had a clear answer in her heart.

“Why are we exploring a relationship between two people, when the video over here and the video over there each show only one fish?” This segment of live footage she placed into the second round of performances in Wuzhen.

![A performance still from *Like a Fish in Water* at McaM (photo: Jacney Chan)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026162804ZzFtWOieTnaslYcS.jpg-w600)

At the time, Lyu Yuzhou undertook the experiment because she wanted to explore her own intimate relationships; she found Shen Bolun, a friend with whom she had an ambiguous relationship, as her collaborator. Having gone through the conflicts and confrontations of the experiment, what remains on stage today is a suggestive “absence of him”—and all of this she candidly confessed to everyone at the beginning and the end of the performance.

After the video finished playing, Lyu Yuzhou stood in front of the fish tank and delivered the final performance: “At this moment, I’m finally willing to admit that maybe this really is a one-woman show. Like a fish in water, suffocation—it’s all nothing but my projection.” She also threw a question to everyone: “Can we enter a relationship without preconceptions? If so, would intimacy have new possibilities?”

Although roughly 30 percent of the crowd around the fish tank had left by then, after the show ended Lyu Yuzhou received many hugs, all from audience members.

![A Wuzhen performance still from *Like a Fish in Water* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026110448OMk2Nb6jgRpKPsiy.jpg-w600)

### We spoke with both directors; you can learn more below.

Q = QDaily, Lyu = Lyu Yuzhou, Lin = Lin Cuixi

**Q: How do you define this performance? Performance art, documentary theater, or something else?**

Lyu: To us it’s simply theater. Actually there’s no definition, but we keep being asked this question. The theater is a very open place where anything can happen. We have our own tastes and preferences, but we feel it’s all theater. But we’ve increasingly found that audiences need an entry point—they need to know what they’re watching, and sometimes a label can help them adjust their expectations.

**Q: This time many audience members expressed that they didn’t understand it, right?**

Lin: Right. Actually, in the work I did before, a lot of it was physical, even close to contemporary dance—in that context you don’t need to interpret what role the dancer is expressing, right? The audience doesn’t come in with that preset to watch the story or the performance; they’ll interpret its musicality, and so on.

But traditional theater is narrative-driven, and audiences want to watch a story, to find a set of character relationships. So this kind of gap is unavoidable. So I’ve been thinking about how to handle this problem, how to explain better, to adjust everyone’s expectations.

Lyu: But I think the most interesting thing is that some audience members don’t understand, yet they’re curious and open—they’ll come ask, they’ll try to enter. I think as long as that’s there, it’s fine. Actually we’re slowly doing guidance, especially me (in *Like a Fish in Water*)—I’ve increasingly started doing guidance.

![Audience members at the Wuzhen venue of *Like a Fish in Water* (photo: Dai Jianyong)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026111932xoSF5CnruLdg1f2v.jpg-w600)

**Q: What do you mean by “increasingly started doing guidance”?**

Lyu: At first we didn’t realize how hard that would be. For ordinary people, they can’t understand it and can’t accept it—the moment the dancing started they were dumbstruck. For example, in the second performance (October 20) we still had to guide the audience through the dancing, because some audience members demanded refunds on the spot.

Previously we didn’t provide this much guidance around the dancing. Actually, as long as you come with an open mindset, there’s an entry point for everything. The scariest thing is when someone arrives, doesn’t know how to get in, and immediately says it’s shit, that this isn’t theater, and then refuses to enter. I don’t know how to open up such an audience member.

**Q: Your premiere at McaM was in June. What was the audience reaction there? They should be quite different from a theater audience.**

Lyu: McaM has its own audience group, so when they come to watch they know more or less what to expect—they know they’ll see all kinds of strange things. They aren’t theater audiences in the traditional sense. Some audience members even feel your work is “too theatrical,” right? For them, they’re still watching theater, so it doesn’t count as performance art. But theater people will say what you do isn’t theater—it’s too much like performance art.

What exactly are we? But I think this is a very interesting state, and I’m enjoying it. We’re doing something new—but actually, abroad, it’s not.

![A performance still from *Pregnancy* at McaM (photo: Jacney Chan)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026162549oYNi6tSazPw9cnZv.jpg-w600)

Lin: This time in Wuzhen I chatted with a staff member from a French arts festival. After seeing the show she could read the “poetry” in the theater. She felt that the language of poetry is very much at home in the theater, and even easily interpreted. But for Chinese audiences, or mainstream audiences, poetry in the theater is fragmentary and jumpy—it’s just… you don’t know what it’s saying.

Literature is the same: if someone regularly reads novels and then looks at poetry, they might think, what is this stuff, just a few words? In fact it has its own internal logic, and you need to read more before you can understand what that rhythm in the middle is like.

**Q: Have you been creating works of public-space art all along?**

Lyu: Actually, our theater work is very diverse. When I was in the UK I also made literary theater, and I love drama. It’s just that what we brought this time happens to be like this. She (Lin Cuixi) also has work that’s more physical, more sensory. Neither of us has set limits for ourselves, and we haven’t been making only this kind of work.

Lin: We don’t feel hostility toward drama, toward traditional “drama”—we were even trained in that system.

![《宗周异闻录》 (*Strange Tales of Zongzhou*), co-directed by Lin Cuixi and Chen Laoju](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201810261122560wNoDJQv96C8bVT1.jpg-w600)

**Q: In a 2016 interview you mentioned marginal art. How do you define this “marginal art”?**

Lyu: Not marginal art—I said marginal. I said that actually I’m a very mainstream person, but everyone has their own marginality, and I’m looking for that thing. Every time I work with or collaborate with people, they’re marginal in some scene. But you bring them back into the theater, back into a mainstream place, and give them a chance to speak.

When doing this kind of theater, the question of responsibility is difficult. If you’re making this kind of work, you bear a certain responsibility toward them; they have strong demands, but you also have your own artistic pursuits—you want to explore things through it. To me these two are like two stones pressing on my shoulders, and they’re hard to balance.

![A performance still from 《你从哪里来到瓦尔登湖？》 (*Where Did You Come From, to Walden Pond?*, 2015, 16, 17, 18–, Beijing), a long-term documentary project centered on a group of bankrupt art-school students](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018102611351523ek9yvQrDNcXogx.jpg-w600)

**Q: What’s the hardest part?**

Lyu: Their demands and the ambiguous territory you want to explore, the things you want to express through this—sometimes they’re not the same thing. So we have to find a suitable interface.

Beyond theater, we’re also exploring other means, like film festivals, archives, and so on. The discourse is monopolized by the mainstream, and many people cannot speak. Making a theater work makes me feel powerless—what can you do with this thing? So a hundred people came to see it, so what? What exactly are you doing? This has been my anguish these past two years.

This is a problem you constantly face when making theater with real people (non-professionals): your responsibility toward them—what are you changing as you make it, what are you really pushing forward on the practical, real-world level? Or is theater really just a symbol, a banner? I keep thinking that a work is not as simple as just telling their stories.

![In 2018, 《你从哪里来到瓦尔登湖？》 (*Where Did You Come From, to Walden Pond?*) was presented as a photography exhibition](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026113553gx9bH3AwM7hkSioT.jpg-w600)

**Q: Did you encounter intense conflicts in this process? Were there times you felt you couldn’t go on?**

Lyu: Many times. Every time you try to expose their stories, they’re ordinary people and may not want to expose their most painful things. There are a lot of problems like this. So whose will matters most for this work—his or yours? We’re always searching for these answers.

**Q: *Like a Fish in Water* drew on a lot of your personal experiences. Is exposing yourself to the public dangerous? You’re the narrator; the other audience members are strangers to each other, and only your identity is public.**

Lyu: I don’t think it’s dangerous. From the very beginning of the work, when we were interviewing audience members and getting them to expose themselves, I kept trying to build an environment where exposing or sharing very personal things was safe—so I took the first cut at myself. Having different voices, even conflict, is wonderful.

I’ve realized I’m always on that boundary; reality and theater are forever in a mutual relationship. Of course I also expose myself in a controlled way. For example, in the opening I shared that a breakup is a kind of detachment—originally the concept hinted at “sex,” but sex is very private and might instantly set the floor at the lowest level. So actually a lot of it was designed; it’s not that I just expose myself recklessly.

And for example when I cried at the end—there was actually a performative element to it, but the audience felt I was immersed in my own emotional relationship. Sometimes the boundary between the real and the performed is ambiguous.

**Q: After the Wuzhen Theatre Festival, what are your plans?**

Lin: We’re planning a new event, “Scratch Night,” starting in early November and running for five consecutive weeks, with one new piece every week. Each week we make a small play and meet the audience on Wednesday nights. The work is unfinished, but the interesting point is that the audience comes in with the attitude of “I’ve watched an incomplete work,” and then takes part, exchanges, gives feedback.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181026132609kx9bzT6KLBPwo1t3.jpg-w600)

Lyu: I don’t want to be a project-based independent creator anymore; I want to slowly build a community and a theater-based life. Creating at high intensity once a week—constantly experimenting, failing, zeroing out, and starting again—is actually a process of cultivating new seeds, of stimulating each other.

In spring and summer we do a performance season: someone wants to explore something, and others assist—that’s a relatively complete expression or attempt. These two parts complement each other for me. I want to build something with long-term planning, instead of sometimes passively waiting for resources and opportunities to complete a project.

**Q: Are you still collaborating with McaM now?**

Lyu: Because our producer Zhang Yuan is at McaM, we had this opportunity. McaM has many plans of its own, and it can’t always be a perfect fit. For the last three weeks of Scratch Night, for instance, we’ll be collaborating with the Dinghaiqiao community and a photo studio. We need some new explorations, and we also want to enter society more—on the one hand within the art or theater circles, on the other hand entering broader public spaces.
