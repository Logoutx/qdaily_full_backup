"Once you have something like this, the steps that carry the rest of your life upward are different."

**Lin Tianhong has always been pleased with himself. Beyond that, the phrase he mentions most is "meaningless."**

**There was meaning once. In 2008, Lin Tianhong was 29 and a lead reporter at Freezing Point Weekly, a supplement of China Youth Daily. The feature he wrote, *《回家》 (Going Home)*, won him that year's Best Reporting award at China Youth Daily, as well as the feature-writing award in Southern Weekly's annual Media Salute. In 2008, the fee for Going Home was 3,000 yuan, but its impact on Lin Tianhong far exceeded anything money could measure — ten years on, the story is still the most distinctive label attached to him.**

**His memories are still staggering.**

**On the evening of May 12, 2008, Lin Tianhong was at home writing a story. At eight o'clock he turned on the TV, and the screen showed that the death toll from the Wenchuan earthquake had reached 8,000. He immediately asked his editor, Du Yongtao, to send him to the disaster zone, only to learn that Freezing Point, as a central-level outlet under China Youth Daily, had received notice from the Central Publicity Department at the earliest moment — "Stay put."**

**Lin Tianhong forced himself to finish the story. At three in the morning he turned the TV on again, and the death toll kept updating until he fell asleep at five. He usually silenced his phone before bed, but that night, utterly exhausted, he forgot.**

**At nine in the morning he was woken by the phone. He was about to complain when the screen showed Du Yongtao's name; by the time he picked up, the call had already ended. He dialed back immediately, and the voice on the other end said: "Get to the newsroom now — you leave this afternoon. If you hadn't called back, in two seconds I would have dialed another reporter."**

**Lin Tianhong sprang off the couch, stuffed his computer into his bag, put on his shoes and rushed out the door. He even forgot to bring a change of clothes.**

**Only afterward did Lin Tianhong learn that sixty or seventy reporters at the paper had signed up to go to the disaster zone that day, and the paper finally picked two. "They went back and forth over the print reporters and settled on me," Lin Tianhong said.**

**The other was He Yanguang, a 57-year-old photojournalist who had won China's highest journalism award seven times; the years he had worked at China Youth Daily were roughly equal to Lin Tianhong's age at the time.**

**"I felt pretty great about it. A chance like this was hard to come by... I wasn't afraid at all. For a 29-year-old it was like: fuck, I've finally run into something like this."**

**On May 13, through military connections, China Youth Daily got seats on a flight leaving Beijing for Chengdu at five that afternoon — the first flight after service resumed. The plane was almost entirely relief teams: doctors, technicians from China Mobile and China Unicom, and the like. Lin Tianhong also spotted quite a few familiar faces, mostly from state media like *Caijing* and *Legal Daily*. They understood each other tacitly and did not say hello.**

**The plane circled over Chengdu for more than an hour before landing, and it was nine in the evening by the time they arrived. Lin Tianhong and He Yanguang stayed at a People's Armed Police guesthouse on Jiangxi Street in Chengdu. Although Chengdu itself was almost entirely unscathed, the atmosphere was already enough to make Lin Tianhong tense: no pedestrians on the streets, military trucks and ambulances threading through the emptied streets, the air above the city filled with piercing sirens. That night Lin Tianhong barely slept.**

**The next morning, before the nine o'clock departure, He Yanguang instructed Lin Tianhong: "Find a slip of paper, write your name, your work phone number, and your emergency contact on it, and put it in your pocket. Keep it on you at all times."**

![Rescue scene in Dujiangyan; we have hidden the bodies of primary school students buried under the rubble. pic/scmp](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201805271923225dJweQ81fL27TRVo.jpeg-w600)

**By the time they reached Dujiangyan, "it was basically already a paralyzed city," Lin Tianhong recalls. In the earthquake, 3,069 people died in Dujiangyan. Lin Tianhong's first stop was the Dujiangyan TCM Hospital: the inpatient building had completely collapsed, and more than 200 patients and medical staff were buried under the rubble.**

**Lin Tianhong noticed an old man pacing back and forth on the rubble. The People's Armed Police officer beside him told Lin Tianhong that the old man's daughter was a nurse at the hospital. He had arrived earlier than everyone else and had been helping the armed police clear bodies.**

**A crane was hoisting a massive roof beam, and beneath it in the rubble a pink nurse's uniform slowly came into view, the head and limbs buried in the dirt. The head was gradually uncovered by the armed police. The old man stepped forward, took one look, showed no expression, then turned and left.**

**Lin Tianhong hurried after him and asked whether he had found his daughter. The old man answered: "Yes. I saw the mole on her nose."**

**The hospital was more than fifty kilometers from home, and the couple didn't often get the chance to see their daughter. Not long before, the daughter had called her mother to say she had prepared a Mother's Day gift — she would keep it a secret and bring it home to her mother the following week. Her mother replied on the phone: "Things are expensive; don't waste money," and hurriedly hung up.**

**At this point in the story, Lin Tianhong remembered, "the old man suddenly broke down and said: 'We will never know what she bought for us.'"**

![Parents mourning their children beside the rubble of Xinjian Primary School in Dujiangyan. pic/scmp](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527192526DzT0B7pIUW2EqKPC.jpg-w600)

**Across from the hospital was Dujiangyan Xinjian Primary School, where more than 300 students died in the earthquake. In Lin Tianhong's memory, only a quarter of the school building remained. In the middle of the school's playground stood a pickup truck, its bed piled high with children's bodies.**

**A woman's heart-rending wail suddenly rose from the rubble; then four armed police dragged her out of the ruins, and a child's body was laid in the center of the empty ground.**

**Lin Tianhong saw a man of about 40 photographing the child on the ground frantically. He learned from bystanders that this was the child's father, the head of a construction crew in Dujiangyan who owned the city's only civilian crane. After the earthquake he had driven the crane to the school as fast as he could, and over the past 24 hours he had helped dig out quite a few bodies.**

**Epidemic-prevention workers sprayed the child with disinfectant, and then a doctor prepared to put the child into a green body bag. The father took over: "Let me." He slowly placed the child into the bag. "He was extremely, extremely calm at the scene. I didn't hear a sound," Lin Tianhong recalled.**

**Then the father, holding the green body bag, walked slowly toward the pickup on the playground — the students' temporary burial ground — while everyone watched in silence. "When he reached the truck, he suddenly raised his head and howled at the sky. Half a minute. A very, very long time. A wolf-like howl."**

![The father holding his child's body "howling at the sky"; He Yanguang captured the moment. Photo/He Yanguang](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527191105ZQof3X4P0DRFOLGm.jpg-w600)

**Within just a few days in the disaster zone, Lin Tianhong found grief everywhere he looked, corpses and broken walls at every turn. He decided to stop sending the paper this kind of repetitive death news. "What meaning did news have in those days?"**

**He began looking for a complete story.**

**At a road junction in the town of Yingxiu, Lin Tianhong met Cheng Linxiang and his wife, carrying their son's body on their backs. Their son, Cheng Lei, was a high school student whose height topped his father's by two centimeters. Lying across his father's back, his feet scraped the ground from time to time, and Cheng Linxiang had to keep stopping to re-settle his son, who had stopped breathing.**

**Lin Tianhong wrote in the story: "Cheng Linxiang looped his son's hands around his neck and rested them gently against his chest. As he walked, he talked to his son: 'Yao'er, Daddy's taking you home. Lie still now, don't squirm...' Along the way Cheng Linxiang kept slipping, and Cheng Lei's body fell to the ground. Apologizing to his son, he hoisted him onto his back again."**

**That story was Going Home.**

**"I became famous."**

**"All the confidence I have comes from this one story, and its influence has lasted right up to today."**

**After returning from the disaster zone, Lin Tianhong once wrote: "In my nearly thirty years of limited life up to then, I had accumulated some understanding of life, some reverence for death... I suddenly realized they had collapsed, just like the pulverized ruins before my eyes."**

**He was no longer interested in other topics. Most everyday news became irrelevant in his eyes. Although he later wrote another piece, *《人民会用脚投票》 (The People Will Vote With Their Feet)*, which again won a Southern Weekly annual salute award.**

**By then he felt it "should be about the end," and that he "should go out and take a look."**

**In June 2011, Lin Tianhong left Freezing Point Weekly. He went on to serve as assistant to the editor-in-chief of China Weekly and deputy editor of Portrait magazine.**

**Like many of his peers, Lin Tianhong entered another circle in 2014 — business. What he talked about most now was "financial freedom" — many colleagues had obtained that freedom a step ahead of him. He went to Wanda to run its internal magazine, then jumped to Alibaba Pictures.**

**His earlier résumé was not forgotten, but it was now viewed from a completely different angle. "Later, at Alibaba, when the Lei Yang case came up — the guy who was said to have been beaten to death by police in Huilongguan, Changping, over soliciting prostitution — and you heard what the post-90s kids in our department were chatting about, you felt that everything you'd done in media had been a fucking waste," Lin Tianhong said.**

**"Listening to those Alibaba kids discuss the Lei Yang case, I thought: all that stuff we used to talk about — 'enlightenment,' 'citizen awareness' — what the hell was it? These kids all went to college too, and they still have no concept of it. And you have to realize that people like this are the overwhelming majority in China. So what's the meaning? My precious, limited life was wasted on these meaningless things."**

**"Because there is no meaning, there is no desire."**

**Lin Tianhong acquired a new way of surviving — shut down every channel of communication, delete Weibo, keep a low profile.**

**What Lin Tianhong does now is still related to the feature-story business he was in ten years ago — except there is now one extra step: turning the stories he digs up into films and TV series. Last March he became editor-in-chief of ONE and chief content officer of Youshu Culture; his boss is Han Han.**

**Lin Tianhong doesn't deny that he is a refined egoist and a pragmatist. His measure is "whether doing a thing can produce results in the shortest possible time."**

**Being a reporter was useless; turning stories into films and TV is far more useful. "I think the film and TV market now needs things that portray ordinary people's real lives — to put it grandly, things that can shape social mores, or the world's understanding, more than *Eternal Love* did."**

**In April, we met Lin Tianhong at ONE's office on Fuquan North Road in Shanghai. He hesitated for a long time before agreeing to this interview. During it, he recalled his ten years in careful detail; but the moment the interview ended, he immediately became cautious again.**

**"My silence today is also a safety consideration. First, I have to think of the company; second, I have to think of my family," he said.**

![Cheng Linxiang carrying his son's body on his back. Photo/He Yanguang](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527191355uWc8mF57pt34wlYM.jpg-w600)

"We came out of Dujiangyan and reached the Zipingpu Dam around one or two in the afternoon. Everyone queued to take speedboats to Yingxiu, landing at the Wenchuan Aluminum Plant, then walked two or three hours along a mountain path — it barely counted as a path. It was on that path that we ran into Cheng Linxiang, and that's how the story of Going Home came about.

I thought it was a good story. The feeling I had when I saw them was different — a pair of parents carrying their son's body home. I felt that a journey of three people like that was the plainest yet purest kind of emotion, sure to move people. It's also what we've always been looking for.

I stayed two nights in Yingxiu, then went back to Chengdu to regroup, planning to go back to the town of Shuimo to find the father and son after regrouping. But I had to go together with He Yanguang, and he had driven off to Mianyang, so I looked for stories in the hospitals near Chengdu — but they were truly hard to find.

I believe there are still many good stories we didn't discover, but for one reporter to find them in a sea of people is very hard. Besides, at the time everyone was chasing hot figures, and I involuntarily looked for hot figures too — but you had no chance to interview them at all. You might manage to meet one and exchange a few words, but countless media outlets were waiting for them to speak. Lin Hao, for instance, was produced in large quantities by CCTV. But for a book like Zhang Liang's *《汶川地震 168 小时》 (168 Hours of the Wenchuan Earthquake)*, CCTV might give it a mention, and nobody would dig into what was behind it. Ten years on, when you look back, what Zhang Liang did was valuable; purely as writing, that book's value is immense.

Later He Yanguang came back, and we interviewed the Cheng father and son together. During the interview the two of us cried until we could barely breathe.

Cheng Lei's grandmother spent those days in regret. On the day Cheng Lei left home, he went to pick cherries from the family's cherry tree, and she, afraid he would slip and fall, scolded him harshly. 'My good grandson,' the old woman wailed at the sky, 'come back — Grandma will let you pick as many as you want!'"

![A photo of Cheng Lei](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527191822qF5PDcVgJjieIsR3.jpg-w600)

"I started writing at two in the afternoon the day after I got back, and finished by seven that evening — five hours. Not eating or drinking all day, smoking two packs of cigarettes, and the story was done. That story was truly written as if possessed by a god — by that child's spirit. To be honest with you, I don't know how I wrote it myself.

After finishing it I started to doubt myself. With so many reports out there, who would care about an ordinary story like this? I sent it to a Freezing Point colleague. After reading it she said, 'I couldn't keep reading this story — I couldn't stop crying.'

Then I felt slightly more sure of myself, and I sent it to our boss. I went to bed very early after sending it and slept extremely deeply. The next morning I saw the boss's reply: 'Tianhong, I want to thank you. No vast grief compares to one most specific grief.'

He was the one who had recruited me into the paper, and he knew my temperament perfectly. There's a saying that a loud drum needs a heavy hammer; I'm the kind that sounds even without being hammered. So he was a boss who took pleasure in keeping me down. He liked me — I know that — but that was how he treated me. It was the first time he had spoken so earnestly. When I read the message, tears came. I was especially sensitive in those days; I cried at the slightest thing.

After the story was published, it went up on Sina's homepage a little past nine in the morning, and my phone never stopped. All kinds of people, people I knew and didn't know, called me. In those days I used a Nokia brick phone with a battery that lasted; that day I burned through two batteries — that had never happened before.

Strangers said they wanted to donate money. They called and asked through tears: 'Are you Reporter Lin? I'm a reader from Jiangsu, and I'm a father too,' and then they started crying. Several calls like that — outright wailing.

Our editor-in-chief also texted me: 'This morning I did the math — I received messages from 19 propaganda department chiefs around the country, all saying your story was good.'

That's when you knew the story's reach.

This one couldn't be compared with any of my previous stories, because any story with this kind of impact is the product of the right time, the right place, and the right people. 'The right time' means you need a huge historical event that countless people care about; 'the right place' means you have to be at the scene; 'the right people' means your story turns out decent. Miss any one of them and it doesn't work.

This story made me instantly famous. In the newsroom, in the industry, I became someone everyone knew — someone who could write.

I became famous.

From a reporter's point of view, anyone doing feature stories back then had one wish: to someday win Southern Weekly's annual salute. In that era it was the industry's benchmark, a recognition of you. Freezing Point had won it a few times before, and I thought: if I could win it once, my career would be complete. It was something you could hope for but hardly reach.

And then, suddenly, I had it.

You know, once you have something like this, the steps that carry the rest of your life upward are different. Without this story, how would I have become an editor-in-chief? How would I lead reporters? Lead a team? All the confidence I have comes from this one story, and its influence has lasted right up to today. When people think of you, they still think of this story."

![Cheng Linxiang's family holding a photo of Cheng Lei](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527192130G8oeT5AkC0lXRwmN.jpg-w600)

"But I was also shaken. After I came back I lost interest in routine topics, and my views and understanding of many things changed fundamentally after the earthquake; the things I used to care about, I no longer cared about. I had seen life and death, and I felt that many things — money, buying a house, dating — were all bullshit. If you can't afford a house, you can't afford a house.

I was in the middle of a lawsuit over my apartment at the time, and that lawsuit was a very serious matter for me then — the apartment I bought might be taken back. I started the lawsuit in early 2008 and fought it until 2011, three years. When I first got into it, I thought my life was over. I didn't want to fight anymore; I wanted to flee, to go back to my hometown of Fuzhou. But after coming back from the earthquake, after seeing these people's courage, their resilience, their insistence on life, I felt I should keep fighting in Beijing and not be crushed by things that look so serious — in fact they amount to nothing for you. So what if I just fought the lawsuit to the end? Keep fighting.

I think it was a very good change for my life. My heart became stronger, and nothing could crush me.

I also stopped caring so much about money. I used to think surviving in Beijing was hard and spending should be careful. Afterward I didn't care; spend it however. Save for what? Being happy is enough."

**During the interview, Lin Tianhong laughed often — sometimes at himself, and when he spoke of heart-clenching pain or death, he could laugh it off with a jest too.**

"I think this influence is 80% good. When I was in the disaster zone, a good friend from Fuzhou called and asked: 'So, any insights?' I said: 'Brother, I'll give you eight characters: life is short, seize the day.' (laughs)

That's not negative — why would it be negative? I think it's a very positive attitude toward life. Whatever you have to face, you don't need to tremble with fear; when soldiers come, build a dam to hold them — nothing is a big deal.

Later I switched to editing at Freezing Point and occasionally wrote, but I no longer had that fight. I wasn't interested in participating in ordinary topics; I could probably write them in my sleep.

My boss saw the state I was in back then. I'm not resting on my laurels — I truly couldn't muster interest in ordinary stories.

Once my boss and I were in a car, and he obliquely needled me: 'I've been an editor for so many years, and the reporters I look down on most are the ones who ride one story their whole life.' I answered: 'What if it's two?'

That was exactly my mindset then, because in 2011 my other story, The People Will Vote With Their Feet, won Southern Weekly's annual salute again. So I figured it should be about the end, and that's when I really wanted to leave — to go out and take a look.

China Youth Daily is, after all, a media outlet inside the system. It wasn't that there was no room to rise — by the time I left they were about to promote me to deputy division head — but I thought, don't squat in that slot.

I'm a person driven very much by interest. By then writing stories had become manual labor for me; I couldn't find the joy. No energy, no interest. It wasn't fun anymore.

Maybe it would be different if there were a big event, but how many events like that are there? None. The Luzhou and Yushu earthquakes afterward didn't amount to anything. A colleague asked me: 'Yushu earthquake — are you going?' I said: 'Whoever wants to go, goes.'

I stayed at Freezing Point until 2011. When I left I was 32, taking home 70,000 to 80,000 yuan a year. For a 32-year-old man scraping along in Beijing, that was downright destitute. (laughs)

I got married in 2009. An income like that can cover basic needs, but if we were to have a child, you'd definitely want them to have a better education.

But even if China Youth Daily had offered me 200,000 yuan, I would still have left. Going from 70,000-80,000 to 200,000 was impossible — our editor-in-chief made just over 10,000 yuan a month."

![Lin Tianhong. Photo/admin5](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527193437dioa85kbsFKXYG3m.jpg-w600)

"I came out and took a new job, as assistant to the editor-in-chief of China Weekly, 200,000 yuan a year; I stayed six months. Then Portrait was founded, and Li Haipeng called me over.

I worked at Portrait for more than two years. We truly worked like mad to make a magazine — shooting films, covers, spending tens of thousands of words on a single profile. The people you dealt with were different, too. At China Youth Daily, Freezing Point used to focus on little people; once I got to a market-driven outlet, I focused more on big figures, or on doing more playful special topics that China Youth Daily couldn't do.

I don't think Portrait trained me in anything; Portrait was pure giving. I turned everything I had accumulated before into what I could give at Portrait — I was completely burning myself out. Looking back, training those Portrait kids was actually very fulfilling: how to turn a bunch of blank sheets into decent reporters and make their work known to many people.

But later at Portrait it went stale again — boring, back to routine, no fresher things to see. I had met everyone I was supposed to meet: top stars, businessmen, good hosts — I'd even interviewed Bai Songshi. But that was that. Everything became routine. I knew how to break a story down, what angles it would come from, what the assembled package would look like. When I could close my eyes and run through everything in my head, what fun was left?"

**After leaving Portrait in 2014, Lin Tianhong received invitations from Tencent, Sina, the Wanda Group and others — all entirely within his expectations. He declares that he has never failed an interview: "Normally I might be a 70, but in a flash I can erupt to 120."**

**He ultimately chose Wanda. That year the Wanda Group's revenue reached 242.4 billion yuan, and for nine straight years its growth rate had topped 30%.**

**Lin Tianhong joined Wanda's Corporate Culture Center, mainly responsible for running the company magazine. Lin Tianhong has always valued himself highly, and he fully accepted his superior's remark that the job was, for him, a bit "beneath his talents."**

**But given a career change at 35, the internal magazine was something he could pick up immediately and the safest choice.**

**A year earlier, his former Freezing Point colleague Zhang Wei had founded a personal official account, which later evolved into Xinshixiang. Lin Tianhong, too, desperately wanted to jump out of the original swamp.**

**The former journalists' "roads to riches" seemed to give Lin Tianhong no small confidence.**

**Zhu Defu, former editor-in-chief of China Weekly and Beijing Times, joined Alibaba in 2010 as a group vice president. Gu Jianbing, Yan Qiao and Yang Lei of 21st Century Business Herald became Alibaba's PR directors. Chen Liang of Nanfang Daily became Alipay's PR director.**

**In 2014, Alibaba invested 500 million yuan for a 20% stake in 21st Century Media, under Nanfang Daily Media Group.**

**Lin Tianhong claims he does not envy those journalists who jumped to Alibaba earlier, but in the interview he mentioned again and again that this group had long since achieved "financial freedom."**

**He believes his own desire for material things is extremely low, but his child must enjoy the very best conditions: "Everything for the child is the best."**

"I didn't want to stay in media anymore, and I couldn't become a civil servant, so wasn't business the only option? Then let me go into business and see what it's like — do PR.

I have no snobbery about PR, never have. What right do I have to look down on it? Look down on what? Every industry is an industry — are reporters so special? My mindset has always been healthy. What I look down on is specific people, never an entire industry.

And 2014 was the year traditional journalists were all transitioning; I counted as a late one. Several good friends of mine had left media for Alibaba PR very early, and after Alibaba's IPO they were all financially free. Many of them don't work anymore; some run their own funds and do investing; one went to Canada to open a Chinese restaurant and bought an ice hockey team, living that kind of life. That friend with the hockey team got bored after two years and came back to China to work.

Anyway, most people hop from company to company, because once you hop, your market price goes up."

![September 19, 2014: Alibaba lists on the NYSE. pic/juesheng](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527194157Qr0WPxMNFdbtzhAI.jpg-w600)

"There are also old colleagues still at Portrait. Everyone who stayed had their own reasons; most of those who stayed are women — after having children, women lose their drive on the career front. Reporters don't have to clock in, so they can look after their kids, and the income is decent.

Most women are like that; basically all the men came out, and they're all financially free now.

I'm not envious. So they bought a big villa — I think, hey, not bad, but in fact I don't have a strong desire for material things. To this day I still wear plain T-shirts from NetEase Yanxuan; I own no designer brands. The biggest household expense is the child — everything for the child is the best.

At the time I had several options: Tencent, Sina, and a few other companies; several places sent offers. Then I figured: go to Wanda — it's one of China's most awesome companies. If you're going, go to the best company.

After I got there I found the place ran on a different logic from media. I used to think media was truth, a life value, the commanding height of one's worldview. But once there I discovered that media is actually the minority.

China's real logic is down below; media is the logic at the very top, the thing that sets the ceiling. You become increasingly aware — including enterprises, the unofficial tier-three and tier-four cities — that those things are the real China. Media is relatively highbrow, a relative enclave. Seen today, that enclave doesn't even exist anymore. It's gone.

Back in 2014, everyone in our department belonged to a class with very high annual income; the department's starting salary was 700,000 to over 1 million yuan. When I arrived I did a little survey: 'Do you read magazines and newspapers?'

Of more than 20 people, only one subscribed to a newspaper — and it was a pet newspaper.

There were young people in the department too, and they didn't read them either. Later, at Alibaba, when the Lei Yang case came up — the guy said to have been beaten to death by police in Huilongguan, Changping, over soliciting prostitution — and you heard what the post-90s kids in our department were chatting about, you felt that everything you had done in media had been a fucking waste.

The only people you can influence are those who want to be influenced — an extremely small number.

I can't go back to media; there's no meaning in going back from any angle — your curiosity, your interest, your income, the career track you've built up, your skill set. Why go back?

I think young people fresh out of school can still get a lot of training from two years in media — training you might only get as a screw at a place like Wanda. In media, your overall quality improves: your ability to deal with people, to gather and analyze material. After media, with those qualities you can do well in any industry.

But the truth is, it's not a place you can stay long.

My main job at Wanda was making the Wanda internal magazine, with over 100,000 readers, costing more than Portrait — just a tycoon company. The budget passing through my hands ran to tens of millions of yuan a year — for one company magazine.

I joined Wanda in 2014, changing careers at 35. I wanted to take a risk, but not a pure risk — it had to be safe, because the cost of changing careers at that point was too high. It would have been a problem if it wasn't safe. So I went to the Corporate Culture Center. First, I knew the internal magazine inside out — it was second nature. Second, the Corporate Culture Center is the clearinghouse of information for the entire group; all the exits and entrances are there. I could quickly learn about every department, every subsidiary. The people there know the parent company better than people in other departments, and it suited my wish to understand how a company actually works, what its real logic is.

That place is full of gossip too, because you're close to the boss and you know what these big bosses are thinking and doing. For a while I followed Wang Jianlin every day, watching his style of doing things — all of it was new stimulus, new experience and growth for me. When I was at Portrait I also interviewed Wang Jianlin; how could the face he put on before media be the same as the real one? He has another face for media."

![Wanda Group chairman Wang Jianlin. pic/zhihu](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527195011zM0w84VDfH1SL6J2.jpg-w600)

"I had no sense of achievement at Wanda. The salary was several times higher and spending money was a lot more comfortable — but beyond that, what achievement was there? It's just that with every new industry you can learn many new things: you can understand the operating rules of a new world, how these people understand the world, and what abilities you need to survive in that world — and how to get them.

Once, at a Wanda team-building event, I discovered that my colleagues could actually laugh. Everyone was usually very quiet, very repressed, restrained, not talking much. Whatever the leaders said, they obeyed and executed — I'd never seen that kind of execution anywhere, including at Alibaba.

Every subsidiary under Wanda has a corporate-culture officer liaising with headquarters — more than 400 corporate-culture officers in total. Say you post in the group chat: 'Headquarters is doing such-and-such today.' Leave WeChat alone for ten minutes and you have over 400 unread messages — all of them 'Received, received, received, received...'

One day headquarters says: 'We're running a big group event. All corporate-culture officers please change your personal avatars and work-ID avatars to the unified avatar. Do it by 3 p.m.' At 2:50 p.m. you look — whoosh, all changed, like an army. Can you imagine that in media?

At a deeper level, it's the relationship with your boss. I used to joke around casually with Lao Du, and Li Haipeng and I would curse each other.

At Wanda my boss said: 'Tianhong, do you not know how to write news briefs?' In my heart I thought: fuck, I'm someone who won Southern Weekly's annual media salute. What I said was: 'Yes, boss, your criticism is fair, I really haven't written briefs.' And I had to face it with a smile.

Every company has its mess — scheming, office fights, all of it. I don't play those games myself; I came as a passerby, to experience it. I had planned it out: Wanda has many subsidiaries, and in the end I still wanted to do actual business — the corporate culture department was only a transition.

So later I went to Wanda Pictures. I was 40; it was time to dig into one line of work. I wanted to make films, and film itself was my interest. After eight months at headquarters, an opportunity came up and I took it. From 2015 to now I've been doing film, and I think I'll keep doing it.

After Wanda, I spent two more years at Alibaba Pictures. At first at Alibaba I had a sense of achievement: do a good job of film promotion, make everyone know about it, and decent box office is achievement — something you can see directly. I also learned a new skill, so in the film industry I have a specialty of my own.

The problem with film promotion is that you know full well something is a pile of shit, but you have to package it as ice cream — yet it's still shit, and you still want to make ice cream. Since that's the case, why not make the content yourself?

That's related to why I came to Shanghai, to ONE, to do content. One reason was wanting to leave Beijing — the smog there was too bad — and it happened that Haipeng was here and asked me to come over."

**In 2011, a piece Lin Tianhong had written for Freezing Point was used as the modern-prose reading passage on Fujian's gaokao Chinese exam. He wrote on Weibo: "I thought, it's my own article, of course I'd know it cold and handle it easily. I never expected that when I checked the answers, there was so much I couldn't answer."**

**The post was reposted 60,000 times and drew 14,000 comments.**

**Lin Tianhong does not resist being called an early Weibo "KOL, public intellectual."**

**He believes that was a period when young people could accomplish something, and also the time when he was at his most brash.**

**The person he looked up to then was precisely his current boss, Han Han. "At a certain stage of life, some of Han Han's views matched mine very well; his worldview was sound — that phase when he was acting as a citizen."**

**The labels "Citizen Han Han" and "intellectual" date to 2008, when Han Han rushed to the disaster zone in Sichuan at the first opportunity, used his blog as a material relay station, asked netizens for supplies needed in the zone, and published *《地震思考录》 (Earthquake Reflections)*, which was reposted 320,000 times.**

**In a sense, Lin Tianhong and Han Han followed remarkably similar trajectories over the past ten years.**

**They went from active engagement to silence.**

**Lin Tianhong sanded down his edges as much as he could, deleting all his Weibo posts. Han Han kept his Weibo account but gives almost no media interviews, spending most of his time making films.**

**Lin Tianhong calls all these changes "maturity."**

![Lin Tianhong's Weibo post was named one of Sina's top ten Weibo posts of 2011](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180527195609bWMqyszUt4mnr3Qk.jpg-w600)

"After I got to Wanda I shut down my Weibo account; I wanted to hide myself, because the lower your profile at a company, the better — you don't want people holding too much of your information. I stopped posting to Moments too; when I did, it was company stuff, or I'd block colleagues in groups. In that kind of environment you learn to protect yourself, and the best way is to keep a low profile. The more people, the more slips — don't let people think I'm someone with a personality.

Everything that should be deleted has been deleted. I don't use Weibo anymore — I don't use it now either.

I have nothing I want to say; what I want to say, I say to friends. Still be a KOL? Meaningless.

Actually, in Weibo's early days I had my moments of fame over several things. One of my posts was among Sina's top ten Weibo posts of 2011 — 2011, when Sina was still the hottest and the public-intellectual role was at its peak.

But now, what's the use of speaking? None. Back then I wrote so much, wrote so many stories, said so much — so what? Isn't everything going backward? The Southern Weekly episode back then made such a huge splash — that was probably the last stand of traditional Chinese media's elite against the system, and it failed too.

Those buddies of mine are all doing PR at Alibaba now. It's meaningless. It's not that nobody supported what you did — but it was meaningless, which is rather tragic. And then you find nobody cares; only people in your little circle care. Most people keep dancing, keep riding their horses, keep playing mahjong; their lives haven't been affected in the least.

Listening to those Alibaba kids discuss the Lei Yang case, I thought: all that stuff we used to talk about — 'enlightenment,' 'citizen awareness' — what the hell was it? These kids all went to college too, and they still have no concept of it. And you have to realize that people like this are the overwhelming majority in China. So what's the meaning? My precious, limited life was wasted on these meaningless things.

Because there is no meaning, there is no desire.

Han Han, too — Han Han is a person of exceptionally sound values, and a very good boss. He used to write; now he doesn't say a word. He now only cares about the things he's interested in — actually very much like me. We pay more and more attention to what's inside ourselves and don't care about what's outside. And what can the outside do anyway? Today's post-90s and post-00s — why would they care about these serious matters?

I'll just make my films. If my films turn out well, they can still influence more people. As for saying Han Han has gone greasy — I don't see it that way. I think this is a mature way of looking at the world.

Anything you keep doing has its effect; you just have to weigh the effect against its cost-effectiveness.

When you're young you feel many things can be wasted. You say something and it's wasted — fine. So what? But as you get older and your energy gets more limited, you feel: unless saying this produces ten parts of effect, I won't say it; even for one part of effect, I might not say it. A person's energy is finite.

Take the Hongmao Medicinal Liquor affair. In those years it would have been smacked dead with one blow at the earliest stage; now it has already developed to this point before the man was fished out. In those years it would have been unimaginable. A similar thing back then — the county chief of Xifeng County sending police to Beijing to arrest a reporter: by that afternoon it had spread through the reporter community, everyone posted on Weibo to voice support, and there was an immediate apology.

That was then. Now it's going backward. Nothing to be done.

Thinking about it now, that Freezing Point could publish something like that in 2006 — it's simply inconceivable today. That people like us could frolic on Weibo like that in those days, and nobody came after me — it's all inconceivable.

My silence today is also a safety consideration. First, I have to think of the company; second, I have to think of my family.

I now have a family and a child; they still live in this country, and I am their support. From that angle, I can't do things that might bring too much risk on myself. I won't speak casually in the public sphere — you don't know where the state's line is now, and with such severe censorship it's very easy for something to go wrong. Why would I do these things and bring such big trouble on myself?

I currently have several novel stories in development. If they can be done well, they can still leave something behind. At the very least, I know what China's TV drama market lacks; let's see if I can put out good work and fill the gap. That's my most practical thought.

I think what I'm doing now is very meaningful. First, I must be worthy of the salary my boss pays me. Second, I must be worthy of my team — I lead them, and they can't walk away without any growth, without their market value rising. Third, I really can make some good stories.

I've always been pleased with myself, and I'm pretty happy now too. I think the greatest gain in growing up is learning how to get along better with this world, more peacefully. There's an old saying: after you've seen much of the world's ugliness and darkness, if you can still be full of hope and curiosity toward the world — that's the truly good attitude.

I think that's exactly who I am."

**Lin Tianhong does not admit to being a cynic; he just believes his way of getting along with the world is not confrontation or change.**

**"A friend of mine is named Jia Jia — look at what he's doing now? He used to be that kind of person, and what is he doing now? I think among our generation of traditional journalists, people with ideas like this must be the overwhelming majority. It's just that there's no meaning. If you truly had the courage to change things and really could change something, I would say a sacrifice like ours might be worth it."**

**"But you later realize there's no meaning. What can you change? Nothing can be changed."**

**Nine days earlier, on May 19, 2018, Lin Tianhong reposted his only Weibo post of the past year.**

**The repost came from He Yanguang, the photojournalist who had gone with Lin Tianhong to the disaster zone in Sichuan in 2008 to report Going Home. He Yanguang wrote that on May 16 he had received a WeChat plea for help from Liu Zhizhen, Cheng Linxiang's wife in Going Home. The house their "benefactor" had given them remained unresolved after eight years of delay, and on May 11, 2018 — the day before the tenth anniversary of the Wenchuan earthquake — the government took her husband, Cheng Linxiang, away.**

**At press time, the post had 0 reposts and 2 comments. Neither had anything to do with the family.**
