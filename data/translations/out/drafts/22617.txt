How did Pizza Hut, once regarded as high-end Western dining, become what it is today?

"If the all-you-can-eat salad bar came back today, would you return to Pizza Hut?"

Turn the clock back ten years: the salad bar at Pizza Hut was always ringed by young people, who piled peach halves, corn, and every fruit and vegetable they could into the not-very-big bowl as if in competition. When a towering yet steady "salad tower" emerged, it would leave everyone else—and even the servers standing nearby—open-mouthed.

Back then, forums like Tianya Club and Renren were flooded with all manner of strategy guides and boastful posts about Pizza Hut "salad architecture." Transplanted to today, the scene would more or less be a viral Zhihu thread: "What's it like to build a nine-layer salad tower at Pizza Hut?"

In 2009, Yum China, Pizza Hut's parent company, announced it was canceling the all-you-can-eat salad. Many people voiced their displeasure, disappointment, and bewilderment on blogs, forums, and other communities. Quite a few, on hearing the news, rushed to experience the offering—more a "game" than a dish—before the salad bar was officially discontinued in September.

Pizza Hut's official reason was that "the salad bar no longer served customer needs well." But Zhu Yutong, a marketing expert at the time, said in an interview with *United Daily News*: "This decision will definitely cost Pizza Hut some of its young customers." Pizza Hut offered no further explanation in response to consumers' reactions.

Actually, the "willfulness" of a Pizza Hut riding the crest of dining fashion is easy to understand. Pizza Hut entered China in 1990 and was then, perhaps, living through its best years. In its third-quarter 2007 earnings report, Yum described the competitive challenge facing Pizza Hut China as "No Significant Competition" (no meaningful market competition). In 2008, amid the U.S. financial crisis, Pizza Hut China's same-store sales grew 17%. In 2009 its store count reached 558, up 10% year over year.

But looking back a decade later, canceling the salad bar looks more like a turning point. Coincidence or not, Pizza Hut's same-store sales fell 1% in 2009, though growth resumed afterward. Pizza Hut's corporate office presumably never realized the changes this move would set off.

"I really miss the old Pizza Hut—the one with the fruit salad bar, food served in big bowls and on big platters, and lots of deals," Shen Jiake posted on Weibo last June. What struck him was that, all these years later, he still saw so many fellow post-80s people like himself reminiscing online. He now eats at Pizza Hut once or twice a month; his reason is: "There's one right at the entrance to my neighborhood. When I go out and don't know what to eat, I just go there."

Sun Jiayuan, 17, feels Pizza Hut more or less witnessed her grow up. Recalling her first visit, at age nine, taken there by her family, her impression was: "It was the first time I ate with a knife and fork—I thought it was so novel." But now, she feels Pizza Hut is no different from KFC.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021724359FwTp6Gm1xsrViKZ.jpg-w600)

In every quarter last year for which Yum China reported results, Pizza Hut's same-store sales declined, down 5% for the full year. In December came [news that Yum would aggressively expand overseas this year for Taco Bell, its Mexican-style fast-food brand](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/18945.html). Compared with KFC and Pizza Hut, Taco Bell had long made no real moves in international markets; Yum now plans to grow its 280 overseas stores to 1,000 by 2020. Yum said it had not expanded earlier because fried chicken and pizza were far better known globally than Mexican food, "but now, outside the United States, people in other countries are getting more and more familiar with Mexican food."

China is included. If it follows its earlier statement, a Taco Bell will open in China this year. This invites speculation that Yum China may be pinning its bigger hopes on Taco Bell, while Pizza Hut, showing no signs of recovery, will likely no longer receive much attention or effort.

What has happened to Pizza Hut China over these ten years? Is it that Pizza Hut hasn't been trying, or have we changed?

Deng Fei still remembers when Pizza Hut ads covered the area around Dongzhimen in Beijing, and how her walk to find her mother after school happened to pass a Pizza Hut. To her, the "red roof" logo was stylish, fancy. The Supreme pizza was something classmates yearned for at the mere mention. "We really didn't understand Western food then, and had never had a proper pizza. Going as a group of classmates, or couples going to Pizza Hut on dates—those were quite trendy things to do." As for whether it actually tasted good, Deng Fei is honestly no longer quite sure, because what left the deep impression was the feeling of consuming something new and fashionable.

Yuan Siyuan is the same. She told us: "Only classmates you were fairly close with would invite you along for their birthday, because eating at Pizza Hut back then meant really splurging on a meal." A student surnamed Shen, now 19, remembers being taken to Pizza Hut for the first time by her mother—the four of them ate for nearly 300 yuan, and "it felt like real face."

Most of these memories date to around 2005. Queues were common outside Pizza Hut then, and it represented a fashionable, respectable, high-end Western dining culture. It expanded across China's first- and second-tier cities in the role of "pizza expert" and "Western food authority," and young people flocked to it. Around the same period, it was also where many children chose to celebrate birthdays. In 2003, Pizza Hut settled on "Joy Restaurant" as its brand identity, and in 2006 positioned its tagline as "Western casual dining expert"—both moves tied to exactly these patterns of consumption.

For most people, Pizza Hut was the kind of place you needed a bit of resolve to spend at—or rather, a restaurant for treating yourself. It also signified a certain purchasing power. An incident that happened to Shen Jiake reflects, from the side, how society saw Pizza Hut back then: he had just bought a new phone and went to Pizza Hut with friends. Just outside the door, the phone was stolen. "The pickpocket sure knew how to stake the place out," he recalls.

On the Tianya forum, one of the hottest threads carried a title that captured one segment of attitudes toward Pizza Hut at the time—[*Setting Aside the Showing Off, Does Anyone Genuinely Like Eating at Pizza Hut?*](http://bbs.tianya.cn/post-funinfo-1250539-1.shtml). In this thread, launched in 2008 and drawing more than 2,500 replies, people debated whether those who ate at Pizza Hut liked the food more, or liked the feeling of high-end Western dining more.

Ten years ago, the answer would surely have been the latter. Post-80s consumers chose Pizza Hut more because it represented a fashionable, casual lifestyle.

Even the salad bar that so many miss mattered more for giving young people a sense of participation and an entertaining experience—something few restaurant brands could pull off. Judging from the resentment and controversy when it was canceled, that was in fact one reason many people patronized Pizza Hut.

If you find this a bit hard to believe, you're surely standing in the present, abundant in both material goods and consumer awareness. According to data released by the National Bureau of Statistics, China's urban per capita disposable income was 10,493 yuan in 2005; by 2015, the figure was 31,195 yuan.

Ten years ago, today's big four fast-fashion brands were nowhere in the picture; Jeanswest and Baleno were the trendy labels of the day. Drinking Nestlé or Yaha instant coffee already made you feel sophisticated; the most cutting-edge phones mostly came from Nokia; remember how cool "Hello Moto" once sounded? And the orange storm Jay Chou unleashed—"my turf, my call" (China Mobile's M-Zone)?

Doesn't the phrase "consumption upgrade" suddenly feel vivid and concrete? Society, the market, and consumers were all slowly changing.

For Pizza Hut, the 2009 cancellation of the salad bar now looks like a turning point. Around that year, Pizza Hut closed 17 stores in Shanghai. Of course, that was not the only reason for its decline.

Pizza Hut did start adjusting itself, in fact. In global markets it kept rolling out flashy, gimmicky marketing: claiming to use eye-tracking technology to read how long a customer's gaze lingered and infer which dishes they might like; converting pizza boxes into projectors; and even, at one point, a much-hyped line of "fashion."

!["Fashion merch" launched by Pizza Hut in the US](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172435NSCUbpXL5OmkMzAF.jpg-w600)

In November 2014, [Pizza Hut carried out what it called "the biggest change in the brand's history"](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/3527.html) (not yet rolled out in the China region). It flattened the logo, updated the website, product packaging, and staff uniforms, and launched dozens of strange new products.

In China, after canceling the salad bar, Pizza Hut launched a business lunch; in 2014 it added breakfast and late-night hours. At the end of last year, Pizza Hut converted two Beijing and Shanghai outlets into personalized restaurants, with special menus, open kitchens, and the like. It had been changing its menu twice a year since 2008—this company really did put in no small effort to please Chinese consumers.

![Promotion of the fall menu on Pizza Hut's official website](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172435HpX2IVvhWRdu5bEx.jpg-w600)

But nothing else could offer the sense of participation and organic conversation the way the salad bar did. Pizza Hut never meant to drift away from young people, but young people were steadily drifting away from it.

In 2005, Yum China completely split off from Yum! Brands International and began reporting directly to the U.S. headquarters. For a considerable stretch after that, the China market's importance to Yum globally had already surpassed Yum's home market, the United States. It was from this point that Pizza Hut began its rapid store expansion: within three years, Pizza Hut's store count shot from 230 and 291 up to 404.

Another coincidence: also in 2005, Sudan red was found in the seasoning of KFC's New Orleans roasted wings and New Orleans chicken burgers—the first food-safety incident KFC had faced since entering China in 1987. From then on, the price fast food paid for food-safety scandals, KFC and McDonald's included, grew ever higher.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172436lw65PDmJuojcUAQ1.jpg-w600)

But in China, KFC has always been Yum's most important brand, accounting for roughly 75% of all Yum China stores. Next to the profit and revenue contributed by the massive KFC, Pizza Hut always remained orders of magnitude smaller.

And after KFC ran into trouble, Yum pinned its hopes wholesale on Pizza Hut, which—whether in feel or in price point—still sat squarely in young people's core spending.

In a sense, Pizza Hut took on the function of upgrading Yum's image. You'll find that Pizza Hut later entered a dilemma, torn between "plugging KFC's holes" and "being the respectable Western restaurant young people like."

And when Pizza Hut took on that hope, it diverged from why young people actually consumed it.

Pizza Hut slowly strayed from that sophisticated, tasteful Western dining venue and became ever more fast-food-like. Pizza Hut was fixated on the spectacle of queues at its doors and the high table turnover behind it. Becoming an "all-day-parts" restaurant was one means of lifting turnover; at some prime-location Pizza Huts, opening hours have now been extended to 18 hours. It wants you to finish quickly and leave quickly.

![Pizza Hut's once-familiar queues](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172436SvEIsFrmRJVUtyTn.jpg-w600)

The emphasis was on boosting revenue over a short window, not on the dining experience. Also worth mentioning is Pizza Hut's delivery business—Pizza Hut Delivery. It can be seen as Pizza Hut's pursuit of table turnover taken to the extreme: more convenient, faster, and requiring no contact with customers—all tactics that previously belonged to fast food.

Pizza Hut Delivery was introduced to China as early as 2001, appearing as a standalone brand rather than a sub-brand of Pizza Hut—they had separate official websites, separate delivery fleets, and different menus. Yum at one point leaned toward placing Pizza Hut Delivery ahead of Pizza Hut in importance.

![A Pizza Hut Delivery storefront](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172437NBwAmG2q7SceJp64.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172437fsuEV8O7PMqgr06Q.jpg-w600)

But Pizza Hut forgot the most important question: was pizza really the source of young people's irreplaceable attachment to it? Search Weibo for "Pizza Hut Delivery" and you'll find no shortage of complaints about the food. Once, the focus of young people's chatter was not Pizza Hut's flavor. It was those labels—foreign, young, fun, high-end.

Pizza Hut's site selection also tilted ever more toward fast food—that is, toward places with heavy foot traffic. After opening two or three stores in landmark shopping complexes like Xujiahui in Shanghai or Xidan in Beijing and gradually saturating them, Pizza Hut, in order to keep up its growth, began opening stores in secondary malls or big supermarkets—plazas anchored by chains like Tesco or Wumart. It even opened near scenic spots, parks, and neighborhoods with many families: the Qianmen and Temple of Heaven Pizza Huts in Beijing, for instance, both opened in recent years.

In short, no longer where young people cluster, nor anywhere high-end. The brands taking its place might be Starbucks, Wagas, and the like. Even within the pizza category, consumers now had far more varied choices—Mr. Pizza, a Korean chain focused on hand-tossed pies; the more refined PizzaExpress from Britain; Tube Station, with its American rustic style and giant platters; and Le Caesar, famous for its durian pizza.

![Mr. Pizza, from South Korea](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172438QxuAvCDP8F9bXdWY.jpg-w600)

![Tube Station, founded in Beijing, now with 15 branches](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702172439in83rZWYehmHcwFU.jpg-w600)

The rivals Pizza Hut faces are far more complicated than they appear.

Shen Jiake, the one who misses Pizza Hut's salad bar, will probably see his wish come to nothing. But even if Pizza Hut brought the salad bar back, he probably wouldn't return. Pizza Hut has become a fast-food signifier, unable to offer young people an atmosphere of freshness, uniqueness, and upscale consumption. Young consumers have lost their active curiosity about it and their preference for choosing it; Pizza Hut has become a "just somewhere to eat"—because the location is convenient, or because you happen to spot it when you're short on time.

The young people of every era are different—post-70s, post-80s, and post-90s each get their own labels. But young people in any era are drawn to what's new, and they champion the brands that can "represent me."

Pizza Hut was once one of them; young people back then chatted, celebrated birthdays, and dated there. Today's young people still need face-to-face interaction, still need to take photos, still need to post to Moments. Who could have known that a decade later, phones and Wi-Fi would become so important in dining settings? And consumers' dependence on phones and Wi-Fi sits in natural contradiction with Pizza Hut's "fast-foodification."

A seemingly unrelated technological change may have driven away its most loyal consumers with more force than the salad ever had. So ten years from now, what will become of the young people's brands that are red-hot right now?
