He was the editor of manga artists Tetsuo Hara and Tsukasa Hojo.

Manga entrepreneur Nobuhiko Horie, founder and CEO of Coamix, created the Silent Manga Audition (SMA). But his better-known identity has always been tied to *Weekly Shōnen Jump* — during his tenure as the magazine's fifth editor-in-chief (1993–1996), the weekly's circulation reached an all-time high of 6.53 million copies, earning him the nickname "the six-million man."

He was the editor of manga artists Tetsuo Hara and Tsukasa Hojo. Without him, *Fist of the North Star* would never have been born, *City Hunter* would have been frozen forever at a rushed ending, and readers would never have had the chance to see the sequel, *Angel Heart*.

Compared with the famous manga artists he personally trained and promoted, Horie — in his role as "editor" — was far less known among readers, a man who quietly toiled behind classic works. But at SMA's opening ceremonies, talks, and interviews, when he discussed the expressive power of manga, the relationship between artists and editors, and the problems facing the Japanese manga market — punctuated by earnestly animated hand gestures — people could sense a responsible, dedicated man who loved this craft and knew exactly what he was doing, and could understand why he enjoys such a fine reputation in Japan's editorial and publishing world.

Born in 1955, Horie became an editor at *Jump* in 1979. ["Entering this trade was like marrying manga,"](https://www.oricon.co.jp/special/50302/) he said. After that, his working style as an editor gradually took shape. He also happened to catch manga's golden age in the 1980s — or rather, he himself was one of the key figures who propelled that golden age.

In Horie's creed, an editor's duties are weighty and numerous: he must work in close partnership with the artist, not merely chase and collect manuscripts. He himself worked diligently toward this end — he ranged widely, following not only manga but many other entertainment products to catch market trends and supply inspiration to his artists at any moment; he had a knack for guessing what readers liked and an extremely sharp market sense, and he championed the questionnaire system established by the first editor-in-chief, Nori Nagano, because "it brought us closer to our readers," and also because *Jump* entered the market too late — by the time the cake had been divided up, the questionnaire route was the only efficient way to find good work.

On the other hand, he was acutely aware of the considerable limitations of a weekly magazine deciding a series' fate by reader votes. "Building popularity takes a process. We generally decide whether to cancel a series after 10 weeks of serialization. But there are always works that only gather momentum in weeks 7 or 8. I picked such works several times. The questionnaire system dictates all of this, so slow-burning works are always at a disadvantage."

Within the scope of what an editor could do, Horie always tried as much as possible to let new talent shine. Even if they only had "partial" talent, he believed they should be given a chance, because "making up for weaknesses with strengths" was one of an editor's duties. For example, Tetsuo Hara was not good at writing stories, so Horie brought in Buronson as writer — and that created today's *Fist of the North Star*. And one of the work's most crucial settings also came from Horie — [he revealed in an interview](https://matome.naver.jp/odai/2136062338868966201) that one day he read a book on traditional Chinese medicine in a secondhand bookstore, which said, "If you press the wrong acupuncture point, the entire internal organ will be damaged and the person will die." He immediately called Hara, then still a newcomer, and told him to add this new setting right away.

"Editors should put all kinds of proposals to manga artists and read other works widely for inspiration," Horie said. Though he never considered himself a literary type, he loved reading film scripts of all kinds, especially those of Akira Kurosawa and Yoji Yamada. This accumulation became one of the wellsprings of an editor's strength.

In handling his relationship with artists, he kept public and private matters strictly separate and always maintained a professional attitude in the manga field. By the time Hara was drawing *Hana no Keiji*, he was already regarded by everyone as a manga master, but Horie still demanded much of him and repeatedly made him revise and redraw. After Horie was promoted to editor-in-chief, another editor took over the series. Hara recalled: "Unlike Mr. Horie, the other editors often invited me out for drinks and were very warm-hearted, but that was as far as it went. There was none of that feeling of creating a work together through discussion — they'd just take the manuscript and leave." It is generally believed that the poor quality of *Hana no Keiji* in its later phase was the result of Horie's departure.

6.53 million is a number this editor-in-chief can never avoid, though he revealed he has no special memory of that 1995 issue of the weekly. And he wisely chose not to print many additional copies of the next issue. "The return rate for that issue was 2%, meaning 98% sold. Today's magazine world agrees that selling 70% is a remarkable achievement; 98% back then amounted to a sellout. But a 2% return is still 130,000 copies, and if we had reprinted, that figure could easily have exceeded 200,000. Suppose we printed 7 million and sold 90% — then 700,000 copies would come back. Those extra hundreds of thousands of copies would have required over a thousand trucks, whether for distribution or returns. In that period, we also had to pay attention to the circulation of returned magazines."

*Jump* soon faced a crisis. In 1996, as *Dragon Ball*, *Yu Yu Hakusho*, and *Slam Dunk* finished their runs one after another, circulation plunged from 6 million to 5 million. Though this was not Horie's responsibility, he still resigned as editor-in-chief. His replacement, Kazuhiko Torishima, also failed to turn the tide, and by 2001 the weekly's circulation had fallen to 3.4 million.

Beyond lacking a major hit to carry the magazine, it ultimately fell out with many talented authors over its high-pressure policies (an all-round squeeze on manga artists), and rifts grew between artists and the editorial department. But Horie always maintained his friendship with Tsukasa Hojo, Tetsuo Hara, and the others. After he left *Jump* in 2000, he immediately founded the manga company Coamix with these two friends and began publishing his own *Weekly Comic Bunch*, where Hojo's *City Hunter* sequel *Angel Heart* was serialized.

Having launched his own enterprise, Horie also gained near-absolute freedom. [Since 2012, the SMA has been something like a collection of his ideals](http://www.manga-audition.com/presscenter/#headlines) — promoting dialogue-free manga to return the form to the essence of "dynamic expressive power," unearthing talented newcomers and training them into masters, and building a platform where manga artists around the world can exchange ideas, letting their concepts collide to produce new original works. And as a judge, he seems to have returned to that editor's role: serving the artists, pushing them toward more successful paths, and bringing readers purer manga.

**The following is a Q&A with Nobuhiko Horie at the SMA event**

**Q: Why did you choose a dialogue-free selection method?**

**A:** At Jump I worked with many colleagues to unearth quite a few newcomers and let them show their talent. So this time the purpose is the same — I want young people to enter SMA. Japanese manga technique includes "action" — manga needs continuity and lots of action, and the images need a sense of "motion." So when we look at SMA entries, we don't focus that much on the whole; we actually look at whether the effect expressed in parts of the story comes close to dynamism, and we select works by that standard. In fact, many artists dislike their own stories and give up, so we encourage them to develop their partial talents. Jump used to find these people too. For this competition in China, the purpose is not to collect everyone's works, but to unearth and cultivate talent and turn them into masters.

**Q: For a period, JUMP serialized silent manga. How did the company agree to that at the time?**

**A:** At the time I also discussed this with Inoue (Takehiko Inoue). Inoue really wanted to use dialogue-free manga to express emotion. I agree with what teacher Tsai Chih-heng said, because sometimes dialogue limits expression — some things simply can't be said in dialogue.

**Q: What is the relationship between a writer and an editor? How is the work divided between the two?**

**A:** Based on our experience: first, if we find a talented author, we remove financial obstacles from their environment so they can concentrate on creating without economic pressure, ensuring such a creative environment. Because concentrated training of these talents over a certain period when they are young, to bring them up, is a crucial matter — so we place great emphasis on early-stage investment.

After these manga artists debut, publish works, and have one or two popular series, they enter another critical period: the author himself becomes confused about what to draw next, and this is where an editor's ability matters greatly — we provide strong support at this point. And when an author hits a bottleneck, an editor very much needs a market sense. Jump used to conduct many surveys; each weekly survey would yield more than 60,000 responses from fans, from which we obtained a lot of data. We would share the research results with the authors to judge which works could be popular in the market.

Beyond that, on a personal level, an editor must read more novels and watch more films, expose himself to as many works as possible, and draw inspiration from them. When we see popular works or works with potential, we must also share them with the manga artists in time. For example, before *Fist of the North Star* was serialized, Bruce Lee was huge, and *Mad Max* was also hugely popular — so what would happen if Bruce Lee appeared in Mad Max's world? *Fist of the North Star* was born from a brainstorm like that. And *Dragon Ball* — the origin of its theme was actually "what if Jackie Chan appeared in *Fist of the North Star*."

In this way, editors put all kinds of proposals to manga artists to open up their imaginations. Then the artists exercise their imagination, and many classic original works come into being. In the process of a manga artist's creation, the editor plays the role of a catalyst.

After a work is born, the editor must also think about how to sell it in the market, considering the commercial aspects — such as how to develop the work comprehensively. In today's terms, it must be sold not only in print but also as an e-book, and includes games, films, live-action adaptations, and so on — incubating the IP in every respect to make it a big, popular franchise.

Actually, an editor's work is extremely varied, and I could go on forever, but this doesn't mean editors are so amazing or capable. It's simply that they must create a good working environment for the manga artists, letting them focus on creation without having to do anything else — the artists don't have to think about how to sell their works either. That is the division of labor between the two.

**Q: What qualities does an ideal manga artist need? Which aspect of newcomers do you value more?**

**A:** The standard is the same as SMA's. With no dialogue, the most important thing is the expressive power of the images. That expressive power makes readers imagine for themselves, and then adding dialogue afterward becomes simple. In Japan we call this "directing power" (engairyoku).

**Q: What are the characteristics of today's Japanese manga market? What makes you happy, and what worries you?**

**A:** Compared with the optimistic things, I worry more. The Japanese market right now is very prone to bandwagon effects — when a good work comes out, many similar works also pop up, and no one rejects them. I'm very concerned about this situation. Japan also has a lot of digital manga, and in the digital manga field there are many platforms where we can publish works ourselves. With so many works of uneven quality, after consumers read so many bad works, they become confused.

**Q: So is there any solution to this phenomenon of homogenization?**

**A:** You know how it is in today's Japanese market — it's fixed, just that small a market. When the market is that small, everyone will naturally look for already-successful works to imitate. So let's make the market bigger and take a longer view — not just the Japanese market, but also the Chinese market and other markets. If the market expands, this problem won't arise. So I hope there will be more opportunities for Japanese manga artists to exchange with artists from other countries. It's possible that an idea doesn't work in Japan but works in China or the United States — so such an exchange platform is especially important for breaking this homogenization. Only by sharing ideas with the whole world will more good works be produced. And the Chinese market is very large — it will never reach the kind of saturation Japan has.

**Q: What is the biggest difference between manga in print media and online media?**

**A:** The brain's degree of pleasure is different.

**Q: Can you elaborate?**

**A:** We worked with Japan's top universities, the University of Tokyo and Kyoto University, to research the topic of pleasure in the brain. We found that when reading a book versus looking at a screen, reflected light actually exceeds projected light in terms of affecting the brain's activity and excitement levels. Projected light means the person is viewing one-sidedly — there is no reflected light — but reading a physical book involves reflected light. So after studying the difference between reflected light and projected light, we found that the brain is more pleased after reading a book. But for relatively shallow content, looking at a screen is fine.

**Q: What role do you hope to play in the manga industry today?**

**A:** I was originally a manga editor, and I'm very familiar with this field. I know that manga's relationship to games and animation is very close. I hope to take the good and bad things in manga, combine them with surrounding entertainment media, and let the good things shine even more. I hope to play a mediating role in all of this.
