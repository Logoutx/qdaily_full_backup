So far, this company hasn't made a single misstep, but that doesn't mean it feels secure about the future.

We don't know how Nike CEO Mark Parker views Apple's new product, the iPhone SE, but one thing may be the same: compared with the anxious chasers, the company sitting at number one in its industry always has to go through more choices—and perhaps more pain—when it comes to deciding what to do next.

On the afternoon of March 16, U.S. time, Mark Parker gave a 15-minute speech under the spotlights of the 2016 Innovation Summit, mentioning a shoe that can automatically tighten itself, an upgraded Nike+ that claims to help consumers achieve personalization, and nine other product updates.

"Connection" and "relevance" were the words he used most in that speech and in the interview he gave us afterward. Over the two-day launch event, Nike arranged for hundreds of media outlets from more than 170 countries, a stand-up set by comedian Kevin Hart, a product demonstration staged like an Olympic opening ceremony (complete with its own small symphony orchestra), and more than a dozen small exhibition booths including a Flyknit knitting machine.

Nike wants to prove to its consumers that it is still the best choice in athletic products, and what it has come up with is a "personalization" strategy that includes the HyperAdapt 1.0, a "shoe of the future."

The HyperAdapt 1.0 has built-in sensor chips; once you put it on, the shoe tightens from both sides, and it comes with adjustment buttons. Its designer, Tinker Hatfield, said, "If it can really become fully automatic, the relationship between people and shoes will change completely." That may be saying too much, too soon—at least for now, it looks like Nike needs this concept more than consumers do.

After Nike posted it on Instagram, it won nearly 390,000 likes—far fewer than the Nike Air VaporMax Flyknit announced at the same time. The latter strips away the rubber midsole and carries the slogan "Run on the air," and it won nearly 440,000 likes.

Consumers care more about what they can get right now; Nike wants to know what it can capture in the future. You can tell from the upgraded Nike+ App, set to launch in June, just how eager this company is to capture the attention of future consumers.

The new Nike+ merges the two training apps NRC and NTC with the e-commerce app Nike+ SNKRS. It recommends personalized athletic products to you based on your sports preferences (a full set, from scarves to shoes), which you can buy with one click—and if it's a new product, you can make a reservation right away. Simply put, it combines e-commerce with a sports community. As for the so-called personalization, it's more of a moving turn of phrase—because everything is still a product off an industrial assembly line, and nothing has actually been changed for you.

Given that Nike+ SNKRS had previously been tested only on a small scale, Nike could not provide conversion data from recommendation to purchase.

In essence, Nike has added a new information gateway for itself. In the past, new product arrivals could only be announced through store displays and social networks, and outfit recommendations could only be made by human staff; now all of this can be integrated with sports data, relying on a recommendation mechanism to build consumers' trust.

In this sense, Nike has put itself in an unfamiliar position. As a manufacturer and seller of athletic products, Nike has to read the subtext in the data: daily active users, the click-through rate from product display pages to sales pages, the final conversion rate—and one more key factor: how to get people to keep downloading and opening this App. They will face the cost of acquiring new users, a problem as complex as the mission of a physical store.

Competition in the sports industry is more complex than ever. Uniqlo, Zara, and a large number of small companies with unique product selling points are all entering this field. How to maintain the connection with consumers has become Nike's biggest concern. Although Nike is the largest company in the industry by scale, the target consumers of each of its product lines face extremely diverse choices. How to sustain the relationship between the brand and its users—and the anxiety that comes with it—is probably the main reason Nike has put forward its so-called "personalization" strategy.

![Nike Kobe 9 Flyknit](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124415SnNsLY0V1fuZHjDT.jpg-w600)

Yet even though the sports trend is already in full swing, Nike refuses to utter a single word related to "style" or "trend." This has always been Nike's strategy: at all times it ties itself to "athletes," stressing that this includes "both professional athletes and ordinary people who love sports." Then they talk about "how to improve athletic performance." Just as there was a full year of groundwork with Kobe Bryant before Flyknit launched its basketball shoe, Nike believes that athletes demand far more from products than ordinary consumers—and if you satisfy them, in theory you can satisfy everyone.

Even when "style" comes up, Nike's answer never strays from athletes. John Hoke, Nike's vice president of design, told QDaily: "Our technology ensures that everything we produce can pass the most demanding tests (by professional-athlete standards), and the art part ensures that our products can be embraced by the mass market and resonate with society and consumers at large. When people put them on, they feel confident—and for athletes, that confidence naturally leads to better performance on the field."

Is Nike the best running gear? Not really. At least many runners disagree. But so far, this company has maintained astonishing growth. In its fiscal 2016 third-quarter results released on March 22, U.S. time, revenue rose 8% to 8 billion US dollars, which Mark Parker called "strong growth."

From 2006 to 2015, Nike's stock price rose 600%, and annual revenue exactly doubled. It not only maintained its leading position in the basketball market but also, in the 2009–2010 season, overtook Adidas to take the number one spot in soccer. If you had had the foresight to buy its stock years ago, it would likely have been one of the highest-return investments (another similar company is Starbucks).

CEO Mark Parker calls Nike a collection of "several startups." Keeping a big company in a startup posture—you may have heard similar language at Apple in the Steve Jobs era. According to Harvard Business School professor Clayton Christensen's argument in 《颠覆式创新》 (*Disruptive Innovation*), this is one of the hardest things in the business world.

![The Nike LunarEpic Flyknit running shoe, released in February 2016](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160703124415onrh9RmAyV1PgGlt.jpg-w600)

Mark Parker took over in 2006 and reorganized the company's business units around specific sports, changing the brand from a structure based on sub-brands and products to seven "consumer-focused" product categories: running, basketball, women's training, and so on. He said: "This is how we ensure the company's expertise in every segment. The organization is no longer bloated, and the company's growth no longer wanders aimlessly."

In many interviews, he has stressed the importance, for management, of "editing and amplifying" grassroots innovative ideas. "190 countries, 13 sports categories—add those up and we're talking thousands of product teams. This is a complex, fast-paced business. You have to calm down and think about what really comes first. Thinking about what consumers care about most helps us do the 'editing.'"

He also reallocated business regions, making China and Japan new priorities for growth; streamlined reporting processes; reduced mid-level regional management; and presided over a round of layoffs rare in the company's history. After this round of reform, sales rose 70% in 2008.

Seen at the retail end, this approach is exactly what we described earlier when discussing Nike's retail strategy: the store in Beijing's Sanlitun and a store near a university in Shanghai may have completely different sales focuses. The preferences of the surrounding crowd determine what Nike sells in a given store, and if it's a flagship store, it must also balance the relationship between distributors and other Nike specialty stores.

As for the Flyknit technology that now benefits Nike so much, the first prototype was just a pile of socks made of foam plastic. It sat there on a designer's desk until Mark Parker asked, "What is this?" Once he learned that knitting technology might be applied to sports, Parker realized this was a huge opportunity.

In hindsight he treats such moments as "intuition," but Parker has more than enough accumulation in design and in "how to realize a design." He is someone who loves sketching sneakers at every moment. John Hoke, who has worked with Mark Parker for many years, says the CEO is always sending over sketches drawn on his iPhone, and that sketch then becomes the topic of discussion at meetings.

The Flyknit department now employs more than 100 people at Nike. It doesn't produce standalone products but collaborates with other departments. This structure, built from nothing, is what Mark Parker calls "amplification." When the CEO himself acts as a deep participant in a project, the project gets enough support.

Explore the birth of Flyknit and you'll find that, like Apple—or any company's disruptive innovation—it essentially invented a manufacturing process, not just a product. After deciding to develop Flyknit, Nike set out to find manufacturers willing to develop the technology, discussing not only the production process but also developing the machines to make the product first.

Over the past three years, Nike's strong financial performance has essentially been owed to high-margin products—that is, the Flyknit line launched in 2012 and the star shoe models such as Jordan and Kobe. So far, this company hasn't made a single misstep, but that doesn't mean it feels secure about the future. The "personalization" strategy looks like a bet with unknown odds. At its core is the problem every company wants to solve: how to capture the consumers of the future.

**Q: QDaily**

**MP: Mark Parker**

**Q: You say the different departments are each startups. How do you get every team to operate with discipline without producing bureaucracy?**

MP: It's the art of balance. The company keeps getting bigger, and the scale at which you operate is different from before. What matters is staying mentally agile and keeping communication open. Here, we make sure the creative environment is rich, that creative communities communicate with each other and can operate, experiment, and try new ideas. Many of them are about a specific strategy and agenda, and we must execute these at a high level—especially the things related to scale and innovation projects.

Take Flyknit as an example. At the time we were sitting at one corner of some table, and I looked around and said, 'What's this?' They explained it to me, and I said this is a Big Idea. So the important thing is to nurture and support this idea. In the end, the company's resources started tilting toward them, and it became a collaboration among more people. Ideas can happen in special corners; sometimes they're not part of the regular agenda. I think the best thing is to allow them to exist and let them happen—then, through your support, give them the chance to flourish.

**Q: How could you know at the very beginning that Flyknit was a great idea?**

MP: Intuition. Uh, sometimes it's just intuition. You saw the potential of this kind of knitted shoe in the engineers. We had never done this before. We had to retool the production machines and reprogram them. Starting from this simple, rough idea—if we could pull it off, it would be a game-changing product that would change how our shoes are designed and manufactured. It's not just one area. We started with running, but we're exploring its potential across all sports.

So sometimes you have to read an idea from the perspective of the category: can it scale? Is it relevant to consumers? Can it thoroughly improve athletic performance? Even—does it have value for sustainability? We want designers and engineers to understand these criteria creatively. When they come up with a new idea, they have a concept in mind. That puts us in a better position to "edit" ideas.

**Q: When you spot a good idea, what specific questions do you usually ask?**

MP: How will it change the person/athlete who uses this product? Why will it appeal to them? It has to be real, meaningful, and purposeful. It can make an impact in performance, comfort, aesthetics, or visuals, but it must be a truly "meaningful impact."

**Q: What does Nike see as opportunities today?**

MP: How do we create products? How do we manufacture them? How do we use materials? How do we design? How do we collaborate with engineers across industries to achieve our goals? How do we communicate with our consumers in new ways? How do we serve our customers more effectively? How do we give them more seamless, easier access to Nike? It's all about the individual consumer and delivering real value to them. That's where our drive comes from.

**Q: If you look ten years ahead, where do you think innovation will appear?**

MP: I think it's more personalized products. Changing and customizing in real time for you—I can't spill everything. But say, maybe you want a shoe that can change into different colors? You could perhaps do all of that in real time. These—functional and aesthetic changes and innovations made for you, including entirely new ways of making things that we can't do today—will happen very soon.

3D printing is amazing technology. Whether in function or visual effect, it's (3D printing) more of a tool in our creative process. Investing in it lets us create things most people can't even imagine.

**Q: How do you communicate with consumers effectively?**

MP: I think you still have to understand what's real, what's valuable, and what has purpose. Each specific sport has its own unique needs and culture. So the athletes in each of their fields must be the most insightful and the ones with the most say. This works—and the rule applies not only to product innovation but also to communication. Communicate with people "locally," because every culture has its nuances. Sometimes if a product concept is valuable in the East, bringing it to the West can be very interesting. That's the value of a global company: the inspiration you get locally, you supply that idea to everyone globally.

**Q: In your current position, where do you still get new knowledge?**

MP: Everywhere. I'm very close to my grandmother. She taught me to learn like a sponge, to observe everything around me—life, nature—to look for inspiration, the things many people don't notice. This ability lets me see a thing more deeply, whether from a visual angle, a problem-solving angle, or a communication angle.

I love this interconnected world right now, where we can share each other's influence, mix different ideas, and cross cultural barriers effortlessly. You can see this in music, fashion, and food. The possibility of putting different elements together to create something new is an incredible inspiration to me.

**Q: Is this what you call intuition?**

MP: Intuition... I think it's just observation, curiosity. That's the most important quality. The desire to learn more, to become a sponge, to broaden your horizons, to see things deeply. And then find your inspiration.
