The Study is a new QDaily column for 2018; it is concerned with how people structure what they know.

Robert McKee clenches his fist, mimicking the look of a stomach cramp.

He's heard "the question" again.

He thought he could finally dodge this stupid question this time. He's 77, long since a blazingly famous "story guru," "the teacher no screenwriter can afford to ignore," yet people have been asking it for more than 20 years.

"Um... I see from your résumé that before you became a screenwriting teacher, you were yourself a screenwriter... so I'm a bit curious... what made you become a teacher instead of continuing as a screenwriter?"

He sits on the sofa of a luxury hotel room in Beijing. The conversation had been going pleasantly — about the suspense novel at his side, his movie-watching habits, his wildly popular story theory, the film kids he taught at USC who became famous after the fact but were unremarkable at first, and a play he acted in when he was eight or nine — playing a cobbler.

Robert McKee's profession is teaching people "how to tell a good story." In 1997 he published [*Story: Substance, Structure, Style, and the Principles of Screenwriting*](https://www.amazon.cn/dp/B00NBT68SA/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1528999294&sr=8-1&keywords=%E6%95%85%E4%BA%8B), revered as the "screenwriter's bible," and it has sold ever since. His personal résumé is attached to a long string of award names: 18 Oscars, 109 Emmys, 19 Writers Guild of America awards and 16 Directors Guild of America awards — Peter Jackson, director of *The Lord of the Rings*; Jane Campion, director of *The Piano*; Eric Roth, screenwriter of *Forrest Gump*...

Those people are his students, and those are awards his students won. His way of fighting back against the question is: "Why do you want to know? Because your question has a subtext — let me say it for you: screenwriting is the more romantic, the greater, the truly creative work (compared to teaching).""Why don't you ask it this way — you didn't become a screenwriter, you became a screenwriting teacher, isn't that even better?"

He devotes himself to repeatedly polishing the question, then trying to answer it, being unsatisfied, and starting over.

Apart from that moment, the man in the dark-red shirt who puts two packets of creamer into his espresso maintains throughout the poise of a veteran speaker and a trained sense of rhythm.

In early June, Robert McKee's "Story Seminar" came to Beijing for the eighth time. Because the film and TV industry has grown so fast in recent years, this city hides more screenwriters than you'd imagine — the lowest-barrier core trade in the entire industry. They all love Robert McKee, and believe they will soon grasp the secret of storytelling.

Robert McKee doesn't believe he created story theory; "they are simply facts" — he merely discovered them and systematized them. Now scientists and businesspeople have become his students too. Major corporations, national television networks, and NASA have come to learn from him. Robert McKee has developed new courses, including "Genre Writing (GENRE)" and "Storynomics." He also published the second volume of his narrative trilogy, [*Dialogue*](https://www.amazon.cn/dp/B079257SBX/ref=sr_1_2?ie=UTF8&qid=1528999294&sr=8-2&keywords=%E6%95%85%E4%BA%8B), in 2016.

Years ago, he told a *New Yorker* reporter that he was writing a novel called *The Woman Who Tell Lies*, whose protagonist is a famous female writer who has exhausted her talent, and who meets a heartbroken man searching for an old love in a café. He had set the characters in the story from the start, and the whole novel could be summed up in one sentence, as all Hollywood studios like — though perhaps at the cost of a bit of novelty.

He has yet to finish the novel. "I don't think I will ever finish that novel." Still, he spends a great deal of time writing. "I have too many writing plans — the third book, on *Character*, and other topics I'm interested in. The urgent task now is to get everything I'm thinking in my head down on paper. I have a sense of urgency."

On workdays, Robert McKee, 77 this year, writes in his study from 9:30 in the morning to 6:30 in the evening. His study has a fireplace, and the walls are covered with artworks. Some are from his wife. Others are his personal collection — he favors the work of Latin American artists Francisco Zuniga, Francisco Toledo, and Fino Tumayo. These artworks, he says, are not necessarily the pretty kind, but they are very powerful.

Because we couldn't visit him at home, his wife sent us photos of the study. The photos also show a corner of his family's yard, and a wooded path leading to the pool.

When he's not out giving lectures or teaching, Robert McKee lives with his wife, Mia McKee, at their home in Connecticut. Mia McKee is a painter. Every day after dinner, he and his wife set aside a fixed hour to watch TV dramas — long-running serials have become his preference in recent years. Like most creators today, he believes that compared with film, the length of TV drama makes characters fuller. Recently they've been watching *Santa Clarita Diet*, a dark comedy. The story takes place in a typical California small town, and both leads are real estate agents. "In the first episode, the female lead turns into a zombie, but her husband loves her deeply."

### Turn on the radio any day and there are plenty of wonderful stories in the news

**Q: What books did you bring on this trip?**

M: I brought a murder-mystery from a mystery series, by a Black writer, Walter Mosley. I also brought a short-story collection — you might ask the title, but I honestly don't quite remember it. Anyway, it's a contemporary short-story collection, let me think, it should be called [*A Collapse of Horses*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/25330189-a-collapse-of-horses?ac=1&from_search=true) (by Brian Evenson). The name is a bit odd, because horses is plural. I can picture a single horse collapsing from exhaustion, but a herd of horses? So I find the name quite interesting. The stories in the book all take place in the remote American West, and involve spirits, supernatural experiences, and so on. But it's not actually about ghosts — though the stories are strange enough. Anyway, the stories are wonderful, enjoyable and easy to read. I also brought another suspense novel, it should be called [*Old Joe*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/13118962-old-joe?from_search=true), a bit of a postmodern short piece, a mix of Western stories and supernatural experience — very interesting.

When I travel I like to read short stories. If I bring a novel, I remember getting to chapter three or four, but the problem is, if I then lecture for four or five days straight, I can't touch the book for four or five days, and afterwards I've forgotten the plot and have to go back and review the earlier chapters to remind myself. Short stories don't have that problem.

**Q: You probably don't like being interrupted either, right?**

M: Yes, but being interrupted mid-book is not as maddening as being interrupted mid-film. If I'm interrupted while reading a novel, I can still return to the same page, the same words, and keep going. But with a film or a play, there's something called narrative drive. You move forward with the story in the film or play: what happens next? And then what happens after that?

A film being screened or a play being performed in a theater cannot be paused. When you watch a film at home, you can pause it. But I don't like doing that, because film and theater have a "rhythm." Novels have rhythm too, but not as strong as the former, and a novel's rhythm is not a musical one. In performance, in film and theatrical storytelling, there genuinely is a musical rhythm. If you want the deep pleasure of watching a film or a play, you should want to watch the whole thing and only then savor it, rather than stopping halfway.

**Q: What's a good story you've heard recently?**

M: Every day, as long as I turn on the radio, every day's news has plenty of wonderful stories in it.

**Q: Really? I've never thought of it that way.**

M: These stories are simply superb. Take the one I just heard, a story that happened in Paris. A four-year-old boy slipped and fell off a balcony, and happened to get caught by the balcony of the floor below, so the little boy was left hanging in midair, roughly between the sixth and seventh floors. Then a refugee from Mali in Africa climbed straight up the face of that building to save the boy, like Spider-Man. He climbed up floor by floor to rescue him, and someone recorded the whole thing.

Another one is... a political matter. I don't know how it is in China, but every day in America, this political news is pretty horrifying. This morning, Rosanne Barr, the star of the TV show *Roseanne*, posted a racist tweet — really over the top. And the star was fired by ABC, and her show was canceled. Over a year, her advertising alone could earn ABC $50 million, but ABC fired her anyway — equivalent to tens of millions of dollars going up in smoke. Because that racist remark could damage the network's reputation. They knew that if they kept airing a show starring this racist, they might lose viewers, and then they'd lose money too.

The two non-fiction stories I just told — you could say one is a heroic story and the other a scandal.

**Q: What do you find interesting about these two stories?**

M: Very interesting. Think about it: if you put yourself in that refugee's shoes — he's staying illegally in France, and any behavior that draws attention could get the authorities to deport him. Yet he climbed up six floors of a building to save a child, and he risked his own life to save that boy. Once this happened, everyone would know there was this illegal immigrant in France, and he would be sent back to Africa. But what actually happened is that the president of France gave him citizenship.

The second story. Here's a female star who earns a million dollars a week. Because she posted a racist tweet, she was fired by the network, and the million dollars vanished just like that. She must have known what consequences sending that post would bring, but she did it anyway.

These people, for certain reasons, did something positive or negative. If I put myself in their position and think about why they made such a choice, what state of mind they were in in that situation, and why they picked that one option out of infinite options — is there anything more interesting than that? Human nature itself is endlessly interesting. So these two stories are not merely a heroic tale or a gossip scandal. The decisions of these two people — whether saving a child or posting a racist tweet — were irrational. They made irrational decisions.

### I don't think stylistic consistency in a director amounts to authorship

**Q: You've said that people today don't treat storytelling as an art, whereas in the 1940s and 1950s of the last century, that wasn't the case.**

M: But they are valuing storytelling more and more now. Because of the emergence of long-form TV series, like *Breaking Bad*, *Game of Thrones*, and *Mad Men*. The writers of these series — the creators of these wonderful stories — we call them show runners. Now they receive the same respect as novelists and playwrights, and we regard them as great creative artists. But in the film world, everyone still believes the director deserves the most credit.

**Q: You don't seem too happy about that — about the director getting all the glory in the end.**

M: If the director also writes the screenplay — a director like Ingmar Bergman, who writes his own original screenplay and then directs the film — that kind can be called an "auteur." But some directors adapt novels or hire screenwriters to write the story, and then interpret an existing story according to their own understanding. In my mind, those directors cannot be called auteurs. The French have a theory, the auteur theory — they might accept that kind of director as an auteur, but I don't think so.

I don't think stylistic consistency in a director amounts to authorship. I also don't think that interpreting another person's original story confers the qualification of authorship. The director is an interpretative artist. Exceptional interpretation is often what distinguishes a good work from a great one, and the expression of the story's content thereby becomes graceful and powerful. So in a sense, the director is like the conductor of a symphony — the conductor also has to interpret the composer's music, a composer's composer. And the source of the creativity is still the composer.

If the director is also the screenwriter — Truffaut, for example — he is an auteur director in my mind. Godard too, because he both wrote scripts and directed. So I think it's fine to call those two auteur directors. But nowadays those second-rate French directors who adapt novels also want to call themselves auteur directors.

![A still from Godard's *Breathless* / Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180615000522k7uANUPSsbd8KGFw.JPG-w600)

**Q: You used to teach at USC. USC has a long screenwriting tradition and has trained many great directors and screenwriters, so when you were at USC, did you meet anyone who left a deep impression?**

M: Yes. Of course we know students come in excellent and mediocre varieties, and I did meet some very talented students. George Lucas was once a student at USC. Steven Spielberg — he never actually attended USC; he was a very ordinary high school student, roughly a C student, very mediocre. But he had a strong passion for film. Later he went to Cal State Long Beach, a small school. Then he dropped out and came to USC, volunteering to do shooting and editing work, creating things with some young filmmakers at the school. That's how he and George Lucas met. He pretended he was attending USC, but he really wasn't, haha.

So, I sometimes also got to see very good student films. But at age 21 or 22, there's no telling. In general, back then they were all learning craft. At school they watched many professional, excellent films and learned by imitating. Excellent students could imitate very convincingly, because they could see the subtle differences between good films and other films. But even so, you could hardly see any student capable, at that age, of making something new that hadn't existed before — say, a camera position or lighting setup no one had ever seen. Everything students did had already been done in the previous hundred years. If those good students did well at school, they might later get the chance to enter the industry, and perhaps by the time they're in their thirties or forties, having tasted a bit of life, they'll have something original. But at the student stage, it's unlikely.

**Q: What was the ethos of screenwriting like back then? Did many people have the film dream, hoping to write great stories, or were more screenwriters just making a living?**

M: Of course both kinds existed. There's one kind of person: they love film very much, love this art, want their name to appear in a film, and want to get into the film and TV world, into Hollywood. They have this kind of social ambition, but they may or may not have so-called talent.

Then there's another kind, but fewer. A small number of people also love the art of film, and they genuinely have something they want to express; if they couldn't make films, they'd write novels, write plays, write poetry — they would create one way or another. Because they have this creative talent, this condition compels them to exercise their talent. I like this kind of person, because they have an inner drive to express; art lives in their bodies. This kind of person is very rare. Among the crowd wanting to break into film and TV, they are just a small fraction.

And talented people mostly don't persist to the end. Because they are too sensitive — they get knocked down easily and feel discouraged. I always tell them: if you can't make films, you can always go write novels. Because writing a novel won't be blocked by anyone. If you have a story in your heart, a world full of characters, can see conflicts on different levels, and people's inner worlds — you can write a novel. Some young writers never know how to start writing. But they actually shouldn't worry about how to use words. It's screenwriting that should worry about words — screenwriters have to express the richest meaning with the fewest strokes, which is extremely difficult. Of course, good novelists know how to imply.

In short, I always encourage those people who aren't so good at the social side of the film world to write novels, to put their stories down on paper. Maybe one day Hollywood will take a liking to their story and send them an invitation.

### Bad stories fascinate me too — I wonder whether there's some deeper meaning

**Q: About your writing experience — how do you usually begin a book?**

M: The usual practice... though it has changed now. In the past, I would teach for a long stretch, say several years. Students would ask me many questions, and I would answer accordingly. Until I had taught long enough — long enough to have lectured thousands upon thousands of people, having heard almost every possible question and knowing the answers to them. At that point I might start writing a book. [*Story*](https://book.douban.com/subject/25976544/) was like that. But [*Dialogue*](https://book.douban.com/subject/27204782/) — I never taught a class on "dialogue"; there was indeed a section on dialogue within the big story lectures, but I never explored the topic specifically. So I had to do a lot of research myself.

Then there's [*Storynomics*](https://www.amazon.com/Storynomics-Story-Driven-Marketing-Post-Advertising-World/dp/1538727935). I had previously taught a course on storynomics, and then I realized I still needed a business expert, so I found Thomas Gerace, a Harvard Business School graduate who is also the CEO of a marketing company. With his help, we wrote *Storynomics* together.

Now I'm writing a book about "character." This is the hardest thing I've ever done. The book is called *Character*. But you could also call it *Human Nature*. How complex human nature is! In this book, I try to explain human nature to writers, while giving them a methodology for thinking about human behavior at different levels of life, and so on. So it requires a huge amount of research. If I ultimately fail to finish this book, I will spend the rest of my life exploring the subject of human nature anyway.

**Q: You believe a screenwriter must get inside a character's heart — act the character out once. Is that related to your early acting experience?**

M: Much of what I do now comes from my previous experience as a theater actor and director. I've acted in about as many plays as I've directed — I directed more than sixty plays, and professionally appeared in roughly as many. What directors and actors do in the theater is roughly to take the script apart, interpret it piece by piece, and then reassemble it — you need to break it down and then put it back together. To take it apart, you have to understand the author's work. Because a play must be performed according to the script; unlike film and TV, there isn't so much room for improvisation. So my experience working in the theater has indeed benefited me greatly.

**Q: When did you first go on stage?**

M: Age 8. The play was called *Martin the Shoemaker*, a story with religious overtones. The protagonist is a cobbler who one day dreams of Jesus's coming. After waking, he begins to prepare for Jesus's arrival, cleaning his house spotless. Though his house is small, it looks quite nice once cleaned. And so many people come to his house one after another — the poor, the hungry, and so on... All day long, he tends to each of them. At the end of the day, Jesus does not come. He thinks his dream was false. Then a voice tells him: "Jesus was among the poor who visited you during the day. Because you helped them, you also helped Jesus." A rather heartwarming little story. I no longer remember which teacher picked me for the lead, but she could never have imagined that she truly changed my life.

**Q: Once you've mastered the principles of story, does your experience of reading or watching films change? Does it get boring?**

M: For me, actually no. My wife probably understands this very well, because the two of us now and then watch some very bad stories. But for me, watching these bad stories is also interesting. My thought is: how did they make this so badly? What were they thinking? What effect were they trying to achieve? Why did it fail? So bad stories fascinate me too. I wonder whether there's some deeper meaning. Or: how could this story have been told well?

If a story is very unusual, I'll watch it a second time, even a third, to figure out what they're trying to express and how the story is told. Then I can learn from it.

Because storytelling is my trade, all stories are interesting to me. From when I was very small until now, it has always been so. There's an expression in English: it's so bad it's good. Many stories are like that — they're so bad they're good. They're good because they're bad enough.

Of course the experiences are different. A good story stirs your emotions; you feel empathy. This is a vicarious experience. But watching a bad story, you are in a completely analytical, critical posture.

When you see mediocre work, you realize that creating really excellent work takes special talent. Those who produce mediocre work have actually tried their best — it's just that their emotional intelligence is limited. That's how they see the world, so they can only create flat, shallow characters.

**Q: I noticed the final segment of your lecture is a scene-by-scene analysis of *Casablanca*. Why is this film so important to you?**

M: Because I need an example that can comprehensively illustrate the theory I teach. I've tried using other films — Roman Polanski's *Chinatown*, Woody Allen's *Hannah and Her Sisters*, and so on. Those films are all quite good, but they always have limits; if used to illustrate my theory, there's always something they don't cover. *Casablanca* is different — it has six stories. The central story is a love story, plus five subplot lines. It is simultaneously a romance, a political drama, a comedy, an action film, and a musical drama, which lets me explain every one of my principles.

![A still from *Casablanca* / Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180615001023s67AOpXPFWEZH0NY.JPG-w600)

But I want to say, this is not some "favorite film." I don't have a notion of a "favorite film." Having a "favorite" film is rather silly; it's limiting yourself. Moreover, a "favorite" film also affects your experience of watching the next good film.

### It doesn't have to be a cinema — I don't want to watch films with those people

**Q: Whose works or theories influenced your screenwriting theory?**

M: First, Aristotle — without question; everyone should like him. In the 1930s of the last century, there was a writer who wrote a lot about writing, named John Howard Lawson. He wrote a great book called [*The Theory and Technique of Playwriting*](https://www.amazon.com/Theory-Technique-Playwriting-Howard-Lawson/dp/0809005255). There's also a novelist named Henry James, who also wrote many essays about writing. I remember it should be called [*The Art of Fiction*](https://www.amazon.com/Art-Fiction-Henry-James/dp/B004QO9VHO/ref=sr_1_2?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1528996238&sr=1-2&keywords=the+art+of+fiction+henry+james) — that book is important. At the time, there wasn't really anyone writing about screenwriting, at least nothing very influential. In total, it's these three people: Aristotle, John Howard Lawson, and Henry James.

**Q: Did you read them in college?**

M: Right, roughly sixty years ago.

**Q: Your doctoral dissertation was also about narrative structure, right?**

M: Mm, it should be said to be about "story." But that dissertation was actually never finished. I spent about three years gathering related material and read more than three hundred treatises. I wrote several chapters, roughly thirty or forty pages, but later I had to sell my screenplays, so I had to stop writing the dissertation. The lecture outline from which the book *Story* grew did indeed come from the doctoral dissertation. I'm very glad now that I didn't finish that dissertation. Back then I still had the research files from that period — big boxes, many index cards, all sorted into categories. When I pulled them out again to read, I could barely get through them, because the language was too academic... I thought, good thing I didn't finish it, because even if I had written it, no one would have read it. With luck, maybe 12 people in the world would have read that dissertation. But then again, you don't write a doctoral dissertation for everyone to read — it's to get the degree.

**Q: How often do you watch films?**

M: Once a week. So in a year I watch roughly 50 films. Of those 50 watched, maybe 5 are pretty good. Occasionally we also watch films on TV. But TV dramas are too good nowadays. Because my wife and I both work every day — you know, I usually write the whole day... and my wife mainly helps me handle some business matters. She is an artist herself; she has her studio at home, and she usually spends her days in the studio. Basically we work until evening. After dinner, we have a fixed time for watching TV dramas, and afterwards we discuss the plot together. A recent American drama we're watching is *Santa Clarita Diet*, a dark comedy set in California.

**Q: Are you someone who has to watch on a big cinema screen?**

M: I do really like watching on the big screen. I think everyone does. But now we face a choice: choose the cinema's big screen, which means going to the cinema, buying a ticket, sitting there to watch; or choose a smaller screen, watching whenever you want and with whomever you want — friends, family.

**Q: Which do you prefer?**

M: My choice is — a big screen in an empty room. It doesn't have to be a cinema — I don't want to watch films with those people. First of all, they have no basic viewing etiquette; they talk the whole way through the film. So my wife and I have a big screen in our bedroom, at the foot of the bed. The viewing experience is excellent. And there's no one chattering behind us.

**Q: Where do you write?**

M: When I first started writing, I wrote in restaurants. There were five or six restaurants in town I frequented, and I would rotate among them to write, sitting down and ordering a coffee. The servers all knew me; every time I stayed for a couple of hours, they'd always refill my cup and keep my coffee full. I went to those restaurants to write because restaurants have a special background sound, the murmur of human voices. There were no phones or internet then, nothing to distract you easily. The background sound in restaurants seemed to help my early writing.

Later I had my own study and stopped writing in restaurants. I had told myself that I couldn't write in silence, that I still needed some sound. But it turned out I was wrong — I really can write in silence. So I think... I've matured, and discovered that I was deceiving myself, playing games with myself. These habits I thought I had were merely personal rituals.

### In their eyes, popularity matters more than truth — that's why their stories are so shallow

**Q: Have you watched any Chinese films this year?**

M: Not this year. But every time before I come to China, I ask the staff here to find me the three films from the past year they think are best. This time, though, I don't know whether they couldn't find them or forgot — they didn't get them for me. But watching Chinese films in recent years feels different. There was a period — thirty years ago — when China's Fifth Generation directors dazzled the world film scene. They created many excellent works, but later, for some unknown reason, they drifted further and further from their style.

Afterwards there were some avant-garde art experiments. Of course not limited to China — experiments on a global scale. Such attempts are actually "alternative for alternative's sake," and consist of two parts. First, fashionably fragmented stories. Using a very fashionable technique, the story is chopped up and reassembled like a collage — as if that were some great feat. In fact, anyone can chop a story into little pieces at the editing table and reshuffle the deck. Even a child can do it — it really doesn't take much talent.

Second, taboo topics. Poverty, sex, the LGBT community, and so on. Combine the two points, and you create a fragmented story on a taboo topic — and that's the so-called avant-garde, the so-called cutting edge. The avant-garde artists also feel very important; they carry a hero complex, as if they're fighting the whole world. In a sense, they are quite great... but what I want to say is, by fragmenting a story with a taboo theme, you are hurting those very people. If you truly care about the poor, sex workers, or other marginalized groups, you should make for them a tense and moving story — say, I want to make a coming-of-age story about being gay.

**Q: A documentary?**

M: Not a documentary, but fiction. Tell a story solidly, with a central character in it. Of course documentaries are fine — they tell us a lot of information — but in making a documentary, you still haven't lived the same life as your subjects. They're in front of the camera, you're off to the side; they and you are two different kinds of people. It's just that by filming them, you have a record of them. Drama is not a record. In drama, in film and TV, you genuinely live the same life as the characters, feeling the same feelings through empathy. Documentaries don't evoke empathy — sometimes they might, but not necessarily.

If it were me, I would choose a taboo topic, say a marginalized group, and tell a direct, truthful, and powerful story about them. What kind of life they live. So when people watch, they feel they are the people in the story, as if they themselves are going through what the characters go through, rather than looking at them from a bystander's angle.

The problem with avant-garde artists is that, compared with other people, they are more interested in themselves. They want to show off their talent and ideas. Of course, occasionally I also see some experimental, terrific films whose creation is based on pure coincidence and the randomness of life. Like *Run Lola Run*, a German film — very interesting.

![A still from *Run Lola Run* / Douban](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180615001200BbirW1CxelF5S9pE.JPG-w600)

I can teach some knowledge about story and characterization. But the author himself must look toward the deeper levels of life; they should strive to avoid fixed thinking patterns and stereotypes. Too many writers nowadays... I'm going to start complaining — too many writers. They want too much to be liked by others, to become popular. In their eyes, popularity matters more than truth — that's why their stories are so shallow. They know how to take shortcuts, to do whatever will please the audience, and this precisely guarantees that they can't make good films.

On the other hand, they may also have an audience that buys in. For example, in China — China has 1.3 billion people; as long as you win even 1% of the audience, you're economically successful. But I can't tell Chinese filmmakers what they should do; all I can do is teach what I know. Once they understand this art form, they will put it into practice in the environment they find themselves in, and we can see how it evolves in the future.

Still, I know China can make terrific films. Because they did it 30 years ago. I still remember several excellent films — one called *Shanghai Triad*, and *Ju Dou*, *To Live*, *Farewell My Concubine*, and so on. More recently there's *Lust, Caution*. The acting is simply superb, and the adaptation is excellent too. I even have that original novel — roughly a hundred-plus pages.
