60 Billion Cigarettes Have Gone Missing — Do Young People Now Think Smoking Isn't Cool?
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
For the first time in 20 years, cigarette sales in China have fallen. Will this trend continue, and what caused it?
@@QD_BODY@@
For the first time in 20 years, cigarette sales in China have fallen. Will this phenomenon continue? And what caused it?

Cigarettes were forced out of public spaces long ago, but they have never faded from people's lives.

You may not know this: China accounts for 45% of global cigarette consumption — and that's against the backdrop of a worldwide decline in smoking. Over the past decade, global cigarette sales fell 2%, while China's grew 21%.

That's a bit puzzling. Population size is certainly a factor, but without matching enthusiasm, such growth would hardly be possible. China has done no less than other countries in publicizing the harms of smoking. And, as everywhere else, this exhortation has simply gone unheeded by smokers.

In 1929, when the painter René Magritte created his famous pipe painting, he inscribed a line of small French text beneath it: "This is not a pipe." It happens to capture a certain way people look at cigarettes: what fascinates people is the act of smoking itself. The swirling smoke, the flicking fingers — whether visually or tactilely, it gives off an ambiguous allure, and it was once thought to represent all kinds of fine feelings: freedom, pleasure, passion, sexiness. Or stress relief — that's the bare minimum, at least.

![René Magritte's pipe painting | from [the-art-minute](http://www.the-art-minute.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824235529oAz9cxMnqJiVLrbE.jpg-w600)

None of this has changed today — but the statistics have.

According to [data](http://www.ftchinese.com/story/001068109) published by Euromonitor International and cited by the Financial Times, cigarette consumption in China fell 2.4% in 2015, a drop of about 60 billion cigarettes — the first decline in cigarette sales in 20 years.

Are more people quitting? A [report](http://www.tcrc.org.cn/UploadFiles/2016-03/318/201603231215175500.pdf) from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention shows that — to say nothing of the quit rate — the number of people even considering quitting is no different from what it was five years ago.

Is it because prices are higher? Data likewise shows that after smokers had to pay more in tobacco taxes, the most expensive cigarettes actually sold better.

If existing smokers haven't cut back, where did those 60 billion cigarettes go? A more plausible explanation is this: there is no fresh blood joining the ranks.

In Europe and America, where the tobacco industry was more marketized and mature, the tobacco giants had already made young people of every generation their target group for driving cigarette consumption decades ago — and those same young people, under the influence of various factors, have been smoking less and less. The same problem is now arriving in China.

Stricter smoking bans, the steering of popular culture, and the expansion of the middle class have all curbed cigarette consumption. This article tries to answer why the number of smokers in China is now declining, why smoking is no longer embraced by young people as a symbol of rebellion, and why health has only now become a major deterrent to consumption.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824235005SuaKnJfElxGm4A3H.jpg-w600)

### The young people seen as the future of the tobacco business are dwindling

Just as companies and brands today love to talk about the post-90s generation and young consumers, the tobacco companies of Europe and America had already set their sights on young consumers back in the 1960s.

According to *Alluring Smoke: A History of the Cigarette* (《烟火撩人：香烟的历史》) by Didier Nourrisson, Camel filter cigarettes wrote in a market research document of the time: "To ensure the company's long-term development, we should set about strengthening our market share among the 14–24 age group. Consumers at this level have a more open-minded spirit and represent the future of the tobacco business."

The twenty years of competition for young consumers that followed were also the golden age of the tobacco industry in Europe and America. Annual sales of tobacco products in France, for instance, soared from 3.66 billion francs in 1960 to 26 billion francs in 1980; [cigarette consumption in the United States](http://www.statista.com/statistics/261573/total-cigarette-consumption-in-the-us-since-1900/) rose from 484.4 billion to 631.5 billion cigarettes.

This was closely bound up with the rapid population growth after World War II. The most representative case is the American baby boom generation — Americans born between 1946 and 1964. As you can see, the golden age of the European and American cigarette markets coincided with this generation's coming of age. After that, they never enjoyed such a demographic dividend again.

And as the young population shrank, [smoking rates were also falling](http://www.gallup.com/poll/187592/young-adults-cigarette-down-sharply.aspx): among Americans aged 18–29, the smoking rate dropped by 11 percentage points over the past decade.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160824235024vGuUiNw9FnryO2qa.jpg-w600)

For China, cigarette consumption entered its rapid-growth phase somewhat later, roughly from the 1980s onward. "[Judging from the research data](http://news.xinhuanet.com/finance/2016-03/29/c_128843422.htm), people born in the 1960s have the highest smoking rate, at nearly 70 percent," said Ma Yan, an assistant researcher at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences. For those born in the 1970s and 1980s, smoking rates had already fallen to 58% and 49.57% respectively — still far higher than in the United States.

China still has no shortage of young smokers, but this group is also growing more slowly. The *2015 China Adult Tobacco Survey Report* shows that between 2010 and 2015, the number of smokers in China grew from 301 million to 316 million — an increase of just 15 million in five years.

One objective reason for this shift is that the young population is declining year by year. Since 1987, China's birth rate has been in [steady decline](http://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SP.DYN.CBRT.IN?cid=GPD_WDI&locations=IN-CN). The cohorts of young people who turned 15 in 2010 and 2015 differ by roughly 3.9 million. You can see the rapid decline of China's 15–24 population in these years in the chart below.

### The films, TV shows and ads that seduced young people into smoking have been banned every which way

Having made young people their target consumer group, tobacco companies began bombarding them with advertising in every form. American tobacco advertising led the trend in creativity and style at the time.

The classic of tobacco advertising was the series of macho-image ads that Leo Burnett, a New York creative agency, began making for Marlboro in 1954. From 1965, when Marlboro became bound up with the image of the Western cowboy, it came to be seen as a symbol of wildness and freedom — and thereby became the best-selling cigarette in the world.

![An early Leo Burnett ad for Marlboro | from [adprinciples](https://adprinciples.wordpress.com/2013/04/18/marlboro-man/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160825000706ZRsqJ7j6ru92C4e0.jpg-w600)

Large numbers of ads pandering to young people appeared on television, in magazines and in newspapers at the same time. The American tobacco giants once paid huge sums to Hollywood stars including Gary Cooper, Clark Gable, Joan Crawford and Henry Fonda to have them praise the taste of cigarettes.

Shrewdly sidestepping the question of "smoking is harmful to health," the tobacco companies instead got young people to treat cigarettes as one of the tools for entering the adult world. "If you can find yourself at 20, you'll be as mature as a 30-year-old grown-up." That was the idea peddled in cigarette ads in the September and December 1976 issues of the French magazine *Marie Claire*.

What seduced young people even more were the characters in films. After the first 007 film was released in 1962, embedding cigarette advertising in movies became a fixed pattern: besides cars, champagne and watches, cigarettes were part of what gave Bond his secret-agent charm. Cigarette advertising not only contributed enormous revenue to the film industry; it also shaped characters' personalities. What made Audrey Hepburn so alluring in 1962's *Breakfast at Tiffany's* was the cigarette holder more than 20 centimeters long in her hand, and the way she smoked.

![Hepburn's smoking image has become a screen classic | from [bestmoviesbyfarr](http://www.bestmoviesbyfarr.com/articles/movies-with-cigarettes/2015/01)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201608250010376SZKBOtax3XLideq.jpeg-w600)

But as more and more experimental images and medical reports on the health harms of cigarettes were disclosed — first, *The New York Times* published the famous report on "12 dogs that developed lung cancer after smoking," prompting a congressional directive that cigarette packs and ads must carry warning labels; then in 1969, on the eve of hearings on cigarette legislation, anti-smoking organizations screened a carefully edited film "exposing the tobacco companies' dirty faces" — the United States banned all cigarette advertising on television in 1971. In 1981, U.S. cigarette sales fell for the first time.

Later, in 1989, the European Community's Council of Ministers adopted a directive banning tobacco advertising on television, which the European continent began actively enforcing.

In China, where the tobacco industry is basically monopolized by state-owned enterprises, there was no fierce advertising war as in Europe and America, but smoking scenes were hardly rare in films and TV shows. The cool characters in movies like *A Better Tomorrow*, *Young and Dangerous* and *Infernal Affairs* all puffed away.

And as those scenes and ads have disappeared, the odds of young people being seduced have dropped.

### Smoking once stood for freedom and rebellion, but young people no longer think it's cool

This was in fact the set of values peddled by the tobacco companies: as a young person you must rebel, and smoking can make you more confident. It also happened to suit the values of young people for decades — no wonder René Magritte named his famous pipe painting *The Treachery of Images* (《形象的叛逆》).

Whether in American or in early Chinese films and TV shows about youth, skipping class and smoking both became expressions of adolescent rebellion. Smoking was often associated with bad boys, but that was fine — it was precisely for that reason that they found it cool.

![Jack smoking in *Titanic* embodies freedom and rebellion | from [papermag](http://www.papermag.com/11-times-leo-dicaprio-acted-through-smoking-1449460797.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160825100527nQ6rV2g78KljZTEH.jpg-w600)

But young people today define and understand "cool" differently from the past, and this has more to do with the media environment and popular culture. We spoke with 29 people born in the 1990s from different cities — they work in all kinds of industries, and some are still in school. Twenty of them don't smoke; of the remaining nine, one quit last year and three smoke only occasionally. It's hard to draw any conclusion from this without some bias, but it may still be somewhat representative. Most of them consider smoking unhealthy and dislike the smell of smoke, but as long as it doesn't affect others in public places, they don't see anything wrong with it. None of them, however, felt it had anything to do with being cool or not.

You can sense this, too, from the various topics that young people active on social media care about: their understanding of cool is more pluralistic. Wang Jiamei, 19, who studies in California, defines cool and freedom as people like travel or photography masters — "someone with a skill who can stick with it, whether the gifted type or the hardworking type" — adding, "once I start working, I hope to slowly fulfill my dream of traveling around the world with my own money." Liu, 23 (who asked to remain anonymous), joked that getting married right after graduating from university already seemed pretty cool to him.

An intuitive sense of this: look at the cool, heroic characters in today's movies. Ben Affleck tears through the streets in high gear in *Batman v Superman*, Tom Cruise climbs the 828-meter Burj Khalifa in *Mission: Impossible – Ghost Protocol*, and Colin Firth's character in *Kingsman* even has a scene of patting skincare products onto his face — but you no longer see a sharp-dressed James Bond light up a cigarette. It has been 11 years since Daniel Craig became "the first non-smoking 007."

### The middle class is growing, and health concerns are becoming more pressing

Wayne McLaren, the cowboy who played in Marlboro's cigarette ads, passed away on July 22, 1992 — the cause of death: lung cancer. This death marked the end of an era of revelry for the cigarette industry. After the 1990s, tobacco consumption in Europe and America declined year by year at first, and eventually went into free fall.

This is in fact a problem facing all of humanity. According to the World Health Organization, more than half of Chinese adult men smoke, and 1 million people die each year from cigarette-related diseases. But attention to health — or rather, the increasingly urgent attitude toward this matter — has come along with the growth of the young middle-class group. Data shows that the higher one's education level and income, the lower the likelihood of smoking and the fewer cigarettes smoked.

And China's emerging middle class is expanding at a startling pace. Credit Suisse's [*Global Wealth Report 2015*](http://www.deltaspace.com.sg/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/global-wealth-report-2015.pdf) defines the middle class by the standard of holding USD 50,000 to 500,000 in wealth locally; it found that China had 71.8 million middle-class people in 2000, and by 2015 that number had risen to 115 million — growth of 60.3% over 15 years.

In fact, you hardly need such data to prove this point: you can feel it in the current fitness craze and in the popularity of health foods — people's health awareness is stronger than ever. Pursuing health has become a way of life and a life philosophy.

Well — the richer people get, the more they cherish their lives.

### Smoking bans keep getting stricter

In fact, China began moving onto the path of tobacco control back in [1979](http://news.china.com.cn/txt/2014-01/03/content_31076080.htm), when the State Council approved a notice issued by four ministries, the "Notice on Publicizing the Harms of Smoking and on Controlling Smoking." But China's situation is rather special: as a major tobacco country, its fiscal revenue depends heavily on the tobacco industry. Take 2015 alone: the Chinese tobacco industry generated total taxes and profits of [1.144 trillion yuan](http://www.gs.tobacco.com.cn/tabccoNews/Pages/%E4%B8%AD%E5%A4%AE%E5%A4%9A%E5%AE%B6%E4%B8%BB%E6%B5%81%E5%AA%92%E4%BD%93%E9%9B%86%E4%B8%AD%E6%8A%A5%E9%81%93%E7%83%9F%E8%8D%89%E8%A1%8C%E4%B8%9A2015%E5%B9%B4%E5%B7%A5%E4%BD%9C%E6%88%90%E5%B0%B1.aspx), of which 1.095 trillion yuan was handed over to the state — this not only accounted for about 8.77% of [total national tax revenue](http://news.xinhuanet.com/politics/2016-01/30/c_128685558.htm) that year, but was even higher than the [full-year fiscal revenue](http://news.qq.com/a/20160201/040569.htm) of Guangdong, the province that ranks first in fiscal revenue in China.

By comparison, U.S. tobacco tax revenue in 2015 was [$14.45 billion](http://www.statista.com/statistics/248964/revenues-from-tobacco-tax-and-forecast-in-the-us/), only about 0.44% of the federal government's [$3.249 trillion](http://news.xinhuanet.com/world/2015-10/16/c_1116838758.htm) in revenue that year.

"Tobacco taxes still rank first among all industries in China, but this situation is gradually changing," a person in the Chinese tobacco industry who asked not to be named told QDaily. "The share of tobacco taxes in total national tax revenue is slowly declining, and will continue to decline in the future. For one thing, the tobacco industry has very little room left to rise further; for another, as China's economic structure adjusts, tax contributions across industries will gradually even out."

This has also given the government greater resolve on tobacco control. Especially in recent years, smoking-control regulations for public places and rules on smoking scenes in films and TV shows have been formulated one after another, and the [Advertising Law](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/cwhhy/12jcwh/2015-04/25/content_1934594.htm) that took effect in September 2015 banned virtually all forms of tobacco advertising. Cities such as [Shenzhen](http://gd.people.com.cn/n/2014/0302/c123932-20680377.html), [Beijing](http://www.rfa.org/mandarin/yataibaodao/huanjing/jz-11282014135613.html) and [Shanghai](http://www.chinanews.com/sh/2016/08-07/7963943.shtml) have all rolled out their strictest-ever smoking bans.

![On May 30, 2015, a giant no-smoking sign hung at Beijing's Bird's Nest | from [wenxuecity](http://www.wenxuecity.com/news/2015/05/31/4307439.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160825005515HkdTAWNSUm9z2qea.jpg-w600)

Beyond that, just as the economic environment affects all consumption, the United States went through the [1979](https://books.google.com.tw/books?hl=zh-CN&lr=&id=Pjofa9Z1ProC&oi=fnd&pg=PA1&dq=middle+class+in+germany&ots=y8mnGVyR6c&sig=S-PFg8YDnr0xAe3zGzczD3mQdXI&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=middle%20class%20in%20germany&f=false) recession before cigarette consumption fell in 1981; in Germany, cigarette consumption also trended downward after [1980](http://www.statista.com/statistics/507139/cigarette-consumption-per-capita-germany/), as did [GDP](http://www.tradingeconomics.com/germany/gdp). China's situation in recent years has been much the same: GDP growth has slowed markedly since [2011](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%B8%AD%E5%8D%8E%E4%BA%BA%E6%B0%91%E5%85%B1%E5%92%8C%E5%9B%BD%E5%9B%BD%E5%86%85%E7%94%9F%E4%BA%A7%E6%80%BB%E5%80%BC#.E5.B9.B4.E5.BA.A6.E6.95.B0.E6.8D.AE).

In addition, in May 2015 the Chinese government raised the tobacco tax from 5% to 11%, which pushed cigarette prices up an estimated average of [10%](http://news.xinhuanet.com/fortune/2015-05/09/c_127781363.htm) and also squeezed some consumption among low-income groups. "Before this tobacco tax adjustment, we had projected a drop of 2 million cases in cigarette sales, equivalent to 100 billion cigarettes," the aforementioned tobacco industry insider told QDaily. "Generally speaking, for every percentage point that prices rise, sales fall by 0.4 to 0.7 percentage points."

Either way, the news that cigarette sales in China have fallen is, I suppose, something to be glad about.
