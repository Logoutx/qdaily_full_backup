## Questions collected from social media.

Yang Qing, director of *Chongqing Hot Pot*, made his debut at roughly the same time as Ning Hao, but he still counts as a film-industry newcomer.

This is not his first work. Inspired by Ning Hao — proof that low-budget domestic films could find a market — he spent three years preparing and shot *One Night in Supermarket*, released on July 24, 2009. Its competition in the same release window included *Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince* and *Overheard*, and its final box office barely passed 10 million yuan. This story of a supermarket robbery did not attract much attention beyond film-industry circles.

Seven years later, Yang Qing went for something much bigger: robbing a vault.

This time Chen Kuo-fu came on as producer, and Chen Kun and Bai Baihe were cast. For newcomer Yang Qing, nearly 300 million yuan in box office over ten days is a decent result — though Chen Kun and Bai Baihe's combined box office last year already exceeded 5 billion yuan, so the result is merely passable.

The film itself is also not the kind that sells big. Blockbusters come in roughly two types: comedies and visual-effects spectacles, while *Chongqing Hot Pot* is closer to a noir crime film. This genre has produced many fine works abroad, but given the realities of China, *Chongqing Hot Pot* was in trouble from the start: the protagonist must be drawn into a criminal event and provoke conflict, yet the protagonist cannot be a criminal — it must be believable, reasonable, and above all legal.

This predicament has also brought the film considerable controversy. Film critic Taotao Tao Dianying wrote: "Those who give it good reviews merely amplify its strengths until the flaws can be overlooked; those who give it bad reviews amplify the flaws until no amount of strengths can be accepted."

The strengths lie in its style; the flaws lie in its story.

Yang Qing is a cinephile, and not just for Hong Kong films — you can clearly see the shadows of Guy Ritchie–Ning Hao, Korean crime films, even *The Dark Knight*. Among the new generation of directors, "cinephilia" has become a particularly conspicuous trait: drawing on predecessors, imitating predecessors, and weaving it all into one's own style is an important way for directors to grow.

This time, the style is Chongqing itself. The story of *Chongqing Hot Pot* takes place entirely in Chongqing from beginning to end, and the vast majority of it inside a single cave hot pot joint and the old bank next door. This is not just the backdrop of the story — in fact it becomes the story's protagonist. Yang Qing is a Chongqing native, and he consciously wanted to make Chongqing another deeply meaningful urban space beyond Hong Kong.

The story itself, however, is somewhat sloppy, with many things in the film that don't hold together — for instance, the hole in the bank somehow goes undiscovered for a whole week, and the youthful romance plotline feels even more disjointed from the overall style. Film critic Wu Libing put it bluntly: "A noir comedy that completely falls apart — the whole story is utterly disordered, and no amount of patching can assemble even the most basic plausibility."

Some had guessed that the "youth" element might be a compromise for the market, possibly not even Yang Qing's own idea. But in the interview, Yang Qing said he put it in himself. In the film he commemorates not only the space he has already left behind, but also the time that has already slipped away.

Here is more of our conversation with him:

![](/uploads/image/201604/5c0fd4358c7b.jpg)

**QDaily: Did you run into any difficulties with the censors?**

Yang Qing: No. The censors didn't request any changes; a few fight shots were cut. (Too bloody?) Yes, that was it, nothing else. The censors were actually quite supportive of us. But I think it's because of that thing they talk about in the story — the positive energy. After all, it's still a struggle between the poor and criminals, still a main melody of justice triumphing over evil.

**QDaily: Did you ever consider having them actually become bank robbers?**

Yang Qing: They used to want to steal, that kind of thing. They had that thought a long time ago, many years ago, but I never actually wrote it — I didn't need to write it; what's the point of writing that idea? You think it over to yourself, know it can't be done, and set it aside. Actually, by the time I wrote the script — I wrote it in 2012 — I had already realized there was another way for them to reach this story.

As it stands now, they have to fix a hole. That's a world apart from them taking the money.

**QDaily: Could that hole really be patched in such a short time — about an hour? And the bank didn't notice the hole for a whole week?**

These are questions I've considered myself, things I wondered about too. But placed in this city, in this environment, I feel it somehow works.

**QDaily: Placed in the environment of Chongqing?**

Yang Qing: On one level it's placed in Chongqing spatially, but from another angle it's placed in present-day China. You really do often see far more absurd realities in the news.

**QDaily: This is a problem of plot construction.**

Yang Qing: The biggest problem is whether a robbery still needs to happen at all nowadays.

I mean, how much money can one bag hold? Four people go in — how much can they carry away? Everything is networked now; in many economic-crime cases people just move a few numbers online and the money is gone. They don't need to rush in with knives and guns, using the primitive violence of cold weapons — that looks outdated. Judged by the height of human technology, this kind of direct robbery will grow more and more obsolete. And moving money online, they can move much more of it.

**QDaily: So this kind of story is a story full of primitiveness?**

Yang Qing: Right. Actually, stories like this were filmed in large numbers in America and Hong Kong in the 1980s and 90s. But when we film such a story in the 2010s, it carries a sense of temporal dislocation. I don't think it's a logic problem — I consider it a small dislocation of the era. But I wanted to film it, I wanted to do it. Ever since watching those films in the 90s, I wondered how to transplant that kind of story to China. After more than ten or twenty years, I finally transplanted it successfully, and gaps like this were bound to appear — gaps of the era. Really, I don't consider it a simple matter of logic.

**QDaily: Going forward, would you plan to confront this kind of dislocation head-on — to directly portray the clash between primitive force and modern technology?**

Yang Qing: I wouldn't do that deliberately, because it exists in life all the time, I believe. And from my own experience — I walked from a town to a county, to a city, to a provincial capital, all the way to the capital. I experienced China's most advanced places at every step; my life has been walking up step by step like that, and countless dislocations occurred along the way. Things like the spread of culture, changes in lifestyle, technological progress — they diffuse downward through these very large administrative units, while I swam upstream, from a small place toward the upper reaches. You grow used to this kind of dislocation. You come to see it as part of life.

![](/uploads/image/201604/82d4c817b821.jpg)

**QDaily: When you mention the 1990s, do you mean the influence of American films and Hong Kong films?**

Yang Qing: Right, because I was in my teens then — I was born in 1980, and the 90s happened to catch the explosion of cinema. When I was 14, 15, 16, those films of 1994 influenced me for life — the films of 1994: *Forrest Gump*, *The Shawshank Redemption*, *Pulp Fiction*. And then Hong Kong films, plus what the mainland produced back then, *In the Heat of the Sun* and *To Live*, and Japanese films, European films. It was like an astronomical year — humanity's filmmaking talents all erupted together. So if you lived through that era, if your childhood experienced that, you're influenced by it for the rest of your life.

**QDaily: Does the film also contain many homages to other films?**

Yang Qing: No. I didn't deliberately set out to pay homage. There's only one question before me: how to shoot a Chinese city, how to make it more stylized, more distinctive in temperament. When I chose Chongqing, of course I had already, in principle, picked a relatively distinctive city. But how could I refine it one step further on screen? One can only borrow from pan-Asian cinema — you can't very well shoot it to look like Paris, that's impossible. Nor could it look like those more distant cities. But shooting it to look like Hong Kong, like Korea — that can be borrowed from, because there's a closer blood relationship.

**QDaily: Why don't some of Chongqing's more landmark buildings appear in the film?**

Yang Qing: I strongly dislike that thing when people travel, where you must stand in front of some two big characters and take a photo. Does only that prove you were here? That's a very undemanding, lazy way of doing things. We filmmakers shouldn't be that lazy.

![](/uploads/image/201604/7d6efb53e470.jpg)

**QDaily: Why did the four robbers have to be so handsome?**

Yang Qing: In a sense, they are fulfilling my earliest fantasies about this kind of subject matter. They are people who act directly; they are characters from something more story-like, more dramatic. Maybe if I were in a different environment, under different conditions, I might have made them the protagonists of this film.

**QDaily: The four protagonists develop into those four?**

They four are another projection of the protagonists. I never explain in the film what those four are to each other — maybe they're classmates. But there must be some relationship behind them: classmates, brothers, or online friends? They each have... though there's a lot of room for imagination, those four are also my projection onto the other characters, in a way. So those four actually contain a great deal of creative imagination.

They can even be my view and understanding of the way "little fresh meat" idol actors dominate today. I'm telling everyone: little fresh meat can also be like this — or should be like this. Fresh meat should go together with fresh blood.

**QDaily: Do the masks they wear correspond to the protagonists?**

Yang Qing: The correspondence first comes from the number — it's four, and the number "4" naturally makes people think they're related. But the deeper correspondence comes from *Journey to the West*. Because *Journey to the West*, as such a huge theme in our environment, has been interpreted so much, over such a vast space. So when I found *Journey to the West* within the "4," once the masks go on, the audience automatically — I don't even need to do anything — the audience will automatically make the correspondence themselves, each matching a character to a seat. That is the greatness of *Journey to the West*.

**QDaily: Had you considered this aspect beforehand?**

Yang Qing: Actually I hadn't thought about it too much before. I only knew that when I used the *Journey to the West* masks, they would bring enormous space in themselves, interpretations far beyond my design.

Actually, it's that when you use your own traditional culture with real confidence, it rewards you. The charm of traditional culture automatically gives back to you. When I used *Journey to the West* at the time, I was thinking: why do Americans use their masks — thinking of *Point Break* I'd seen before, where they used U.S. presidents' masks, their cartoon images. That's their confidence in their own culture, so why couldn't we use things from our culture? In the end, using *Journey to the West* brought me many things creatively that I hadn't anticipated at first.

**QDaily: Did you design the masks yourselves?**

Yang Qing: We did have designs. In the end our art department referenced Japanese Noh theater, absorbing that eerie style from Noh and putting it into the mask design. In fact we also had to draw on the aesthetics of the whole East and put that in. In the end, you still have to find those things from your own culture to present.

**QDaily: Why does Yu Xiaohui take Liu Bo's mask off?**

Yang Qing: It's very simple: because the audience wants it taken off. The audience wants to see the character, to see the actor. Although we know it's him under that mask, why wouldn't the audience want to see him? Earlier the plot wouldn't let him take it off — he could only wear it. But by then his identity was already exposed, and he couldn't free his own hands, so the character helps him take it off. Actually, with Yu Xiaohui's character, I wanted to see Liu Bo too. I talked with Liu Bo face to face — I wouldn't want you wearing a mask while we talk either. It's also the moment of Yu Xiaohui's life-and-death promise: I want to bear witness. That's also a character motivation.

**QDaily: Could it be that she wants to confirm that Liu Bo really has become her hero?**

Yang Qing: Right, that's her psychology — it's the "I must see him" kind of feeling.

**QDaily: Is Yu Xiaohui a Chongqing local?**

Yang Qing: No, she's from out of town — the kind of northerner who transfers into a school from elsewhere. There are many such people in Chongqing. They speak two languages their whole lives, switching between modes automatically with no pattern.

But Yu Xiaohui's case is a bit more complicated than that. The simple case is fellow townsfolk meeting fellow townsfolk; Yu Xiaohui's case is more complex, because she came to an unfamiliar environment in her teens, which took a process, and then, many years later, when she meets those people again, she goes through another process of language readjustment.

**QDaily: Why did you set up this linguistic difference?**

Yang Qing: Because Chongqing has people like that. Chongqing itself is an immigrant city; during the Third Front construction era, many residents were relocated there, and large numbers of northerners moved over. Within their factory compounds, within their collective atmosphere, they all spoke Mandarin. But when they interacted with Chongqing locals, they learned the local language too. I have friends like that around me. So when I wrote Yu Xiaohui, I roughly knew how such a person feels.

**QDaily: Her frustrations at her workplace — do they also come from this outsider mentality?**

Yang Qing: No, not that. The workplace frustrations come from the whole. I wanted to write a group of losers, to write the life circumstances of people who are not doing well in middle age. People like that cherish feelings more; they can be moved by emotion — unlike successful people, who have no feelings.

**QDaily: Why do they still treasure their middle-school memories so much — is that also because they are losers?**

Yang Qing: The middle-school thing was something I put in anyway. I feel that in middle school, in their youth, in their teens, they thought they would come to own a lot in the future. But later they discovered that what they ended up owning amounts to only this much. This is my view of the classmates who stayed in town. I don't think there's any question of higher or lower — nothing like that — but I feel that by staying there, they're bound to that thing forever, unable to break free of the relationship. Because they live in that place, they witness change every single day: this street is closed off again, the road is rerouted, that building is demolished again, this or that happens. They watch it happen. Amid those changes they come to know what used to exist and no longer does; they understand a certain thing. They're entirely different from people like us who left home to drift in other places.

**QDaily: When Yu Xiaohui first appears, she buys a bottle of medicine and puts it in the fridge. Is there a story behind that?**

Yang Qing: A scene was cut — a scene of her visiting her father in the hospital. She has a sick father, meaning she carries a certain pressure of life, a certain burden of life — a clerk at an ordinary financial institution. (Note: in the trailer they rob a bank; in the final film this was changed with effects to a finance company.) It was just to give her life some kind of burden.

**QDaily: Rather like Liu Bo's grandfather?**

Yang Qing: Right. The reason each of them cannot live freely is that they each have to have something. So why doesn't Yu Xiaohui marry? Why does she still give off a single vibe? Is there some reason? In fact these are all stories beneath the iceberg. But we didn't spell them out plainly, which gives the feeling that there seems to be something.

**QDaily: Liu Bo's grandfather — is this character based on a real person?**

Yang Qing: He also has a real-life model. On the one hand, I have deep feelings for my own maternal grandfather, which is why I wrote a grandfather (外公) rather than a grandfather (爷爷). And then there are my friends around me, who told me about their relationships with the grandparent one generation apart. He's also my take on a reality I've noticed: people in our environment are always closest across one generation — we never quite handle direct blood relations well. Between children and parents, and between parents and their parents, our expression is somewhat unsmooth. So all our expression lands on the skip-generation relationship. He was actually also there to convey the loneliness of family — I put an old man there, and someone like Liu Bo might develop an interaction with that person, with that generation of elders, while with his parents (that interaction is missing).

**QDaily: That kind of interaction instead highlights the loneliness within the family?**

Yang Qing: It instead highlights the family's estrangement, loneliness. He was born into such a family where, actually, all three generations are independent — a grandfather, a mother, and him — it's all illustrating such a person, a loneliness we feel we've seen before: in this family, one person is missing from every generation.

**QDaily: Can we imagine that Liu Bo watched football with his grandfather as a kid, since his grandfather wears an Italy team jersey?**

Yang Qing: Not necessarily watching together. Actually, what I designed is that the jersey the grandfather wears is Liu Bo's — some old people wear their grandsons' clothes. Either Liu Bo gave him that shirt to wear, or he dug it out himself and was willing to wear it — it's a kind of connection. What his grandfather did for a living — actually I don't know; it's never stated that he definitely did some particular thing.

I once thought his grandfather might have been an old sailor who ran the Yangtze around Chongqing. So in their home, I kept putting the sound of a steam whistle in, laying the sound of that boat offscreen throughout. But I feel these things are creative intent — they don't have to be pinned down; let the audience think about it.

**QDaily: Liu Bo graduated from No. 18 Middle School, and No. 18 Middle School is in Jiangbei — was that an oversight?**

Yang Qing: No. 18 Middle School is actually my own high school, but I was at No. 18 Middle School in Chengdu, so I just said No. 18. Because I know what that number means — probably every city has one. I knew that if I casually said a number — No. 32, No. 54, No. 7, No. 9, No. 11 — anyone local would summon up their own memories. I chose 18 because that's the school I attended myself. I had no idea where Chongqing's No. 18 was.

**QDaily: The gun was fake — so why were the robbers afraid?**

Yang Qing: Because the robbers didn't know whose gun it was. They only knew my accomplice had been swapped out — then who would dare swap him? SWAT, police, plainclothes — all kinds of people were possible. Then whoever holds this gun becomes "my man," because even my man isn't my man; if he holds this gun, it might not be my gun. So when the robber finally meets Sun Wukong, he specifically asks: is the gun in his hand yours? Sun Wukong says yes. And then he starts playing the game: one, two, three — he shoots me, you stab him. I wanted exactly that kind of detail.

In that situation, the robber has countless judgments to make; he's betting on this gun, but before that he could obtain no information at all. He can't bet on this gun pressed against my head being mine — or whose it is, some person of unknown identity. And at this point the person who could enter the bank should be the police; he certainly wouldn't imagine it's an ordinary person. The source of this person is far too complicated.

![](/uploads/image/201604/bde233b9645c.jpg)

**QDaily: At the end, when they escape, in that final big battle — why did you arrange for Qi Ge to fight the robbers while the protagonists just stand by and watch? It feels like they became bystanders rather than participants.**

Yang Qing: That's what the audience wants. When the story reaches the point where the protagonists are cornered at a dead end, someone must be an angel sent from heaven — someone or some force sent by heaven to save them. And after he arrives, letting the climax erupt between the two bad guys, bad guy versus bad guy — I think that's more realistic than the protagonists doing it, as just mentioned. The protagonists couldn't beat either side; if the protagonists fought either side, they'd die. But bad guy versus bad guy — it's like Peter Jackson's remake of *King Kong*: when my protagonist meets a T. rex, what can I do? So at this point a King Kong appears, an ape, and the fight between the ape and the three T. rexes is too spectacular. Actually the protagonists are still in danger too; although they don't take part inside the hot pot restaurant, they're in danger, because either side's goal after winning is still them. This is a dramatic technique being played — but behind it there's a metaphor of fate.

**QDaily: In terms of dramatic construction, isn't their sense of danger insufficient?**

Yang Qing: Mm, that's true.

**QDaily: You used a lot of post-rock music — how did you pick it?**

Yang Qing: That's because both my editor and I are post-rock lovers; I get that storytelling feeling from post-rock. When I thought about this story before — I had this story in 2009 — I kept listening to post-rock and could never stop, so by the time this film came along, without even discussing it — I knew he liked post-rock, he knew I liked post-rock — during the edit we automatically picked tracks from post-rock bands and laid them in. Post-rock itself is too cinematic. Post-rock suits film scoring very well.

**QDaily: Will you make more films related to Chongqing in the future? Or what are your later film plans?**

Yang Qing: I don't know; I probably won't. I don't like repeating myself, don't like being fixed in a mold, being positioned. I think filmmaking should be about taking risks. Don't make the kind of thing you've already tried and can do with your eyes closed — filmmaking shouldn't be like that.

Our director Ang Lee has always done it that way: every time he makes your jaw drop, and every time the result amazes you. He takes a risk every time. I think his artistic achievement truly comes from risk-taking — not like other directors who film sequel after sequel, who keep filming one genre they're good at. I think that kind of thing will ruin your artistic career.
