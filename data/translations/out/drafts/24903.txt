Before making music magazines and live shows, Li Hongjie had his own band. But he believes that "China doesn't lack people who can sing well — what it lacks are talent scouts: professional people working behind the scenes."

In high school Li Hongjie had a band called "Biantao," later renamed "Left Ear."

It was a punk band, playing the kind of music that voices young people's feelings most directly. He also had an English name, "Cheizak": "Che" from the name of Cuban revolutionary Che Guevara, "Zak" from Zack de la Rocha, lead singer of Rage Against the Machine — a band Li Hongjie loved.

![Zack de la Rocha](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021147519vAFeSIwKVfzMGsp.jpg-w600)

Back then, Li Hongjie's idols were "Nirvana" and the "Smashing Pumpkins," and he dreamed of performing onstage. That dream lasted until he graduated from college. Of what came after, Li Hongjie says it was "like an arrangement of fate." He has spent over 20 years in the rock scene, yet no one thinks of him as a rock musician anymore.

## 1

In March, Li Hongjie flew to Los Angeles. In Austin, Texas — 2,000 kilometers from the City of Angels — hordes of musicians, media outlets, and startups were preparing for a music festival called "South by Southwest." Nine years earlier, Twitter had broken out at that very festival.

Li Hongjie was going to talk cooperation with their production team. With the ambition of bringing music, technology, and art together in one place, he planned to stage an "MTA Tianmo Music Festival" that August, inspired mainly by South by Southwest.

He went in a bit of a rush. The day before his U.S. trip, on March 8, a festival called "Electric Lady" had just wrapped up in Beijing — China's first music festival for women, where twenty-one female acts performed for an audience of several hundred. It was put together by Wild Horse Live, the livestreaming platform Li Hongjie founded.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702114752dqP5mUv4SfYs0KHF.jpg-w600)

![Scene from the "Electric Lady" festival](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2016070211475305rJ8tZQyqGBzWSA.jpg-w600)

"Live music is a business with a very high barrier to entry." Fortunately, Li Hongjie had accumulated experience over the previous decade and more.

Li Hongjie first produced a live show in 2002, when *Popular Songs* (《通俗歌曲》), the magazine he worked for, launched a "Rock Your Life" concert series spanning rock, electronic, metal, and more. In his own words, "Back then I had no clear concept of what a show should be — the shows had personality, but not enough finesse."

In 2007, Li Hongjie threw himself fully into live production. That year he built a hip-hop stage for the Midi Festival, devoted entirely to hip-hop, and in the same year also built two stages for the first Modern Sky Festival — the Rock Girl stage and the Hip-Hop Reggae stage.

Two years later, Li Hongjie and the *Music Space* (《音乐时空》) team produced the first Zhangbei Grassland Music Festival. *Music Space*

is the successor to the Chinese edition of *Rolling Stone*; starting with the October 2006 issue, the *Music Space* publishing house began co-publishing the magazine with the Chinese edition of Rolling Stone. They also partnered with the Zhangbei local government, which provided the venue.

![Aerial view of the 2015 Zhangbei Grassland Music Festival](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702114753xD3PVW8O1kYIpJlj.jpg-w600)

Zhangbei's character is different from Strawberry's and Midi's.

— The Midi Festival has the Midi School behind it, regarded as a utopia for music fans; Strawberry has Modern Sky behind it, one of the country's most important music labels. Every year they hold festival editions in multiple cities at multiple times of year, drawing far larger crowds than Zhangbei, with convenient transport.

By contrast, Zhangbei sits on the Inner Mongolia plateau in western Hebei, where the geography makes transport difficult — festivalgoers routinely get stuck in traffic jams, and lodging basically means camping.

But for some fans, the Zhangbei experience beats Strawberry and Midi. "Zhangbei's location really is a constraint," says fan Harpy, "but that's exactly why Zhangbei can keep playing until the early morning — unlike Midi and Strawberry, which are nearly empty by nine or ten at night. The late-night grassland, the starry sky, and rock and roll — that's what I think a music festival should be." Zhangbei is also the country's largest festival by single-edition attendance and overall scale: the 2013 edition drew an actual attendance of 350,000, beating the typical 250,000 at Strawberry and Midi.

Li Hongjie loves the Woodstock festival — he grew up watching VCDs of Woodstock — and once he got into live music himself and worked with musicians face to face, he found a kind of joy in "making something out of nothing."

## 2

Qian Weirong, a former writer for the Chinese edition of *Rolling Stone*, first came across *Popular Songs* in his middle-school library. The magazine, he says, shaped "some of my taste and judgment in music."

Li Hongjie was then executive editor of *Popular Songs*, and he stuffed the magazine with music news, Western culture, and proper songs (*Popular Songs* usually came with a free CD).

Li Hongjie's own initiation into rock came from television at first — he fell in love with the sound of the electric guitar because of "Beyond" and "Orient Express." Later he gradually found his way to the music of the "Magic Rock Three" (魔岩三杰), "Guns N' Roses," and "Bon Jovi." Back then, all of China was still hazy about what was happening in the world. The dakou — notched "cut CDs" and "cut tapes," these pieces of "plastic trash" — had only recently entered China, and their sellers fed the young people of that era quietly, like secret missionaries. Li Hongjie was one of those being fed.

![A dakou CD — note the notch cut out at the bottom of the disc](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702114754yKzpZxkfICwNo06T.jpg-w600)

In 1996 he got into college — Hebei University of Science and Technology, in Shijiazhuang. He stayed in touch with the friends he had played music with in high school, the band never broke up, and they performed often, sometimes sharing the stage with bands like "Miserable Faith" and "Twisted Machine" — names that are quite famous today. In 1999 they even did a show in Hefei, his first time performing out of town.

Then everything changed in 1999. That year, *Popular Songs* covered a show by Li Hongjie's band "Left Ear," and from then on he was bound to China's most important rock magazine. He graduated in 2000 and, as a matter of course, was "assigned" to *Popular Songs* as an editor.

*Popular Songs* was one of the country's most important music magazines at the time, and especially important in the rock scene. It circulated widely — people could buy it even in remote small counties — and it made its way into the libraries and reading rooms of many primary and secondary schools. Although the magazine's business struggled — at its hardest point Li Hongjie was the only one left, "having to do everything," work that included interviews, contacting bands, digging up materials, and writing, none of it easy — editorially it still offered more comprehensive coverage of Chinese domestic bands than any other newspaper or magazine.

In 2002, his third year at *Popular Songs*, Li Hongjie began work on *The Rock Bible* (《摇滚圣经》). The book had two parts: the first gave detailed introductions to the genres of rock, including the history and style of each genre, while the second collected several hundred rock bands and artists from China and abroad, recommending some of their albums, scoring them, and writing reviews.

![Title page of the first edition of The Rock Bible](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021147547x9oHYe1W0tiDycG.jpg-w600)

The book was hugely influential. Even today, fans will offer 300 yuan for a used copy of the first edition. Back then, radio DJs would occasionally consult *The Rock Bible* when picking songs to play, and if you ever bought dakou discs, you should remember that the dakou dealers all used its scores to price their CDs.

"At the time I just wanted to write an idiot-proof book, so that people who didn't listen to rock, or who listened but didn't know what to listen to, could take one look and know what to go find," Li Hongjie says. "After reading it you'd have a fairly clear picture of rock's genres and styles, and if you'd listened to every record, you could count yourself a mid-level rock fan." A year later, in 2003, with much the same idea, he wrote *The Jazz Bible* (《爵士圣经》).

## 3

In the 2002 edition of *The Rock Bible*, the first foreword was written by the noted musician Yan Jun, titled "Moving the World Over" (《把世界搬过来》), which contains this line: "Information is an important constituent element of this world. Both we and rock music exist in this world, and if there is some connection between us and rock music — consumption, for instance, or being moved — then information is bound to influence the direction of consumption and the experience of emotion: either we become slaves of information, or we become free through it."

Li Hongjie wants to promote that kind of freedom.

In the past he ran magazines and wrote books; now he hopes to use a livestreaming platform so that people who aren't there can still see live shows. Last year he co-founded "Wild Horse Live" with Wang Feng — an app that livestreams rock concerts online. One piece of data research from Wild Horse Live shows:

If a live venue holds 100 audience members, there are invariably ten times as many people who want to watch but didn't come.

Abroad, Li Hongjie found, such sites had existed for a long time and were only multiplying. Evntlive, the streaming-video startup Yahoo acquired in 2013, was a video service providing web users with live concert broadcasts; Stageit, an American online music platform launched in 2011, helps singers arrange online-exclusive livestreams, ticket sales, and sharing events on Facebook; Turntable.fm started as a music website and later also pivoted into live concert streaming, following the Stageit model; and ConcertWindow is a platform for watching rock concerts online, with both a PC product and an app.

Wild Horse Live currently looks like a mobile version of Le Music, only with a UI that looks more rock-and-roll. Its popularity is hard to gauge for now, but one figure is telling: Wild Horse Live has just over 1,500 Weibo followers, while Le Music has a full 190,000. Last year Wild Horse Live livestreamed 172 shows in total, versus 367 for Le Music.

Still, Li Hongjie believes in Wild Horse Live's value, and he brought in what he considers "China's best livestreaming team" — Bi Tao's video team. Bi Tao had long been producing video for major brands, his technical chops were solid, and he happened to be a rock fan; since the 1990s he had haunted the Happy Paradise bar in Beijing's Wudaokou, reportedly showing up at nearly every live show there.

![The Wild Horse Live video production team producing the livestream for the 2015 Zhangbei Grassland Music Festival](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702114755MBSFvEwRlg30UhVI.jpg-w600)

The live video Wild Horse Live produces does have something special: it offers multiple viewing angles. Unlike most livestreams, which simply shoot from several cameras and have a director cut between the angles, viewers on Wild Horse Live can choose their own angle — watch the backstage, watch the lead singer, watch the drummer, or watch the audience — making you feel a little closer to the show.

## 4

But perhaps the most special thing is that here you can see more rock and roll. A genre that has never quite become mainstream in China and that prizes the live experience accounts for nearly all of Wild Horse's livestreamed shows. Given Wild Horse Live's current market performance and its funding of less than 10 million yuan, the product looks like two veteran rockers trying to vindicate rock and roll.

But Li Hongjie doesn't see it that way. He believes rock will slowly become mainstream culture in China, just as once happened in the United States.

"People who love rock are entering every industry, and the mainstream is slowly changing from within," Li Hongjie says. "In the past nobody read rock magazines, but now a single article from an official account covering some branch of rock easily gets tens or even hundreds of thousands of reads. China's music market is slowly maturing."

If that is indeed the case, then things are truly very different from the past. Li Hongjie discovered rock more than 20 years ago; "back then," bands were rooted in Shucun, Dongbeiwang, and Huoying, and even putting food on the table was a problem. "Now there are more people listening to rock, more shows and festivals, and everyone's life has gotten better." One data point shows that 90% of independent musicians' income comes from live performances.

And Li Hongjie himself? Though he never became a musician, he believes that "China doesn't lack people who can sing well — what it lacks are talent scouts: professional people working behind the scenes." He said it over the phone, from the South by Southwest festival.
