Dior's two businesses, separated for half a century, will finally be reunited and folded into the LVMH group.

As Dior turns 70, its fashion business and its perfume-and-beauty business — after nearly half a century apart — are finally being brought back together in a blockbuster acquisition by the Arnault family, the controlling shareholder of the world's largest luxury group.

On April 25, the Arnault family, controlling shareholder of the LVMH group, announced that it would acquire all of the remaining 26% of publicly traded shares of Christian Dior SA at 260 euros per share. That is a 15% premium over the company's closing price last Monday, bringing the total purchase price to 12.1 billion euros. It will be one of the largest deals in the Arnault family's history. The family already owned 74% of the company.

Once the acquisition is complete, the Arnault family plans to sell Dior's fashion business to the LVMH group for 6.5 billion euros, merging it with the Dior perfume and beauty business the group already owns.

To call this a strategic move is to call it a strategic move suffused with financial maneuvering. For a long time, people have mistaken Christian Dior for one of the brands under the LVMH group. In fact, they have always been two separate, independently listed companies — one holding the Dior fashion business, the other the Dior perfume and beauty business — with the Arnault family as the controlling shareholder of both.

What's more, Christian Dior SA also owns 40% of LVMH (second only to the Arnault family's 41%), so its performance is decisively shaped by the latter.

The two are finally becoming one. From a management standpoint, it helps Dior clarify its structure. And from the perspective of LVMH, the world's largest luxury group, it is a rare vote of confidence at a moment when the luxury industry is shifting from slump to stability. Bear in mind that the reason Dior was split apart in the first place was that the brand ran into operational trouble in the 1960s, and to "stop the bleeding" the Arnault family sold its perfume and beauty business to another holding company in their hands.

![Bernard Arnault](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704252336561gxjI8WSk7XaTRve.jpeg-w600)

The financial engineering also showed in the details: to avoid taxes, the Arnault family's 12.1-billion-euro purchase price was paid in a mix of cash and publicly listed shares of Hermès, with the equity-swap portion set at 0.192 Hermès shares for each Dior share. The move sent Hermès shares down as much as 5% that day.

Luca Solca, head of luxury goods at Exane BNP Paribas, told the Financial Times: "This deal adds a strong-performing brand to the LVMH group at a reasonable valuation, and it also reduces the risk of LVMH buying trophy assets at the cost of diluting returns."

![Dior 2017 S/S runway show](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170425234059V1tdG4u270wkFBIY.jpg-w600)

Dior's recent results have been strong. Last year the newly installed creative director Maria Grazia Chiuri's feminist-slogan logo T-shirts paired with organza made Dior younger. Judging from the just-released [first-quarter 2017 financial report](http://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/christian-dior--first-quarter-2017-revenue-20170411-00878), Dior's fashion business posted revenue of 506 million euros in the quarter ended March 31, up 18% year on year; in [the second half of 2016](https://globenewswire.com/news-release/2017/02/09/915736/0/en/Christian-Dior-2016-Results.html), profit from continuing operations also reached 178 million euros, up 31.9% year on year.

That said, the new designs have drawn plenty of "that's not very Christian Dior" comments. Such voices have persisted ever since Christian Dior himself left the stage, and every incoming designer has faced a similar interrogation. And on the premise of luxury-brand capital operations, the designer — as it happens — has also been the key factor rescuing this brand time and again.

After all, this remains one of the most valuable assets in the luxury industry.

***If you'd like a closer look at how Dior developed, below is an article originally published by QDaily on December 8, 2014, titled 《As Possibly the Best-Performing Luxury Brand of 2014, What Exactly Is Dior Selling?》:***

By today's standards, Dior is a luxury brand. That much is beyond doubt. But how it became a luxury brand is a story worth savoring.

In its newly released results for the first quarter of fiscal 2015, the company showed an ability to operate immune to the downturn plaguing its luxury peers: the entire Christian Dior SA group posted revenue of 417 million euros for the quarter, up 13.3% year on year — while even those luxury companies that managed to grow did so by no more than single-digit rates.

We once compiled [the luxury history of LV](http://www.qdaily.com/display/articles/2081), which, like Hermès, has a long pedigree as a bona fide supplier to royalty; we also covered [Bottega Veneta](http://qdaily.com/display/articles/2917), the house known as BV, founded in 1966, which ascended into the luxury ranks purely on the strength of its one-of-a-kind leather craftsmanship. But Dior is different from both of them. Founded in 1946, it had no particularly special tailoring technique; instead, it caught the right era. More precisely, founder and designer Christian Dior seized on what women of that era craved and created a style. That kind of life-defining brilliance became the lifelong pursuit of countless designers who followed, because a "style" means the product becomes connected to its era and becomes part of culture, history, and society. Once Dior became a point of reference, it broke free of the ordinary consumer-goods relationship between market demand and supply: it could be sold at prices far above its functional value and, on top of that, offer customers a supreme sense of superiority.

The reason this is worth savoring is that the spiritual reference point Christian Dior created for the house still shapes the brand's creative leadership and every one of its loyal customers today — even though the era is entirely different. You often hear people say "this design is so not Dior," and that is exactly what they mean. No brand embodies this kind of loyalty to its founder more fully than Dior — just look at Chanel today, which is in fact already very different from what it was back then.

Let's look at how the business got started.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702143022qOCXnyjepuaIVWSw.jpg-w600)

### 1. One classic look can create a brand, but a brand cannot live on just one look

If you care about fashion, you have more or less heard the story of Christian Dior and the New Look. Boiled down, it is every designer's ultimate dream — to design a new look that stuns the whole world, and then to truly possess everything: the brand, the stores, the customers, the capital, the reputation.

Such an era-defining fashion movement was bound to be born at a turning point in history. In 1946, Paris had yet to emerge from the shadow of war. After the Nazi occupation and the blockade, the people of this romantic fashion capital had no choice but to compromise in the face of real-life problems. Living standards were forced down to the bare minimum, and what one wore no longer mattered. With fabric scarce and rationing in effect, Parisian fashion houses closed one after another, and many designers fled to the United States, costing Paris its status as the fashion capital. It is hard to imagine that back then a refined Parisian woman's wedding dress might be nothing more than an ordinary dress with a military uniform jacket. Nylon stockings, too, had become a rare luxury.

During this period, a few people held fast to Paris's fashion traditions. Among them was the young Christian Dior. His parents had hoped from childhood that he would become a diplomat, but he showed a keener sensitivity to art. In his early years he made a living selling fashion sketches. In 1937, he joined Pierre Balmain as an employee of the Parisian haute couture house Robert Piguet, making gowns for the wives of high-ranking Nazi officials. In the same period, Nina Ricci and Jeanne Lanvin also scraped by doing the same.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021430232tFjkDeXodKrqnwI.jpg-w600)

On December 8, 1946, with the backing of cotton magnate Marcel Boussac, Christian Dior had his own couture house in Paris. Two months later, he presented his first spring-summer collection. Every garment in the collection featured a high bust line, a cinched waist, and a full, swirling skirt, utterly breaking with the masculine wartime silhouette. The extravagant designs, dripping with pleats and lace, held the gaze of people who had grown used to skimpy fabric rations. In one stroke it awakened an boundless longing for the refined, beautiful life of the pre-war years — though Mademoiselle Chanel would later remark that this design was "a man's fantasy about women."

In August 1947, Dior unveiled the "New Look" fall-winter collection, continuing to stoke desires long suppressed by war with extravagance covered in elaborate lace. Customers flocked in. By 1949, the New Look alone had brought the company 12.7 million francs in revenue. Dior by itself accounted for 75% of the French government's total garment export value. Christian Dior became something of a national hero in France; people believed the New Look helped Paris reclaim its position as the postwar fashion capital — even that it was a shot in the arm for the economic recovery of Western Europe as a whole.

Yet after the New Look, until his sudden death from heart disease in 1957, none of the several collections Dior subsequently released matched the New Look's social renown. The New Look made Dior, but it also became a barrier to further innovation. You can see that for decades to come, every creative director at Dior would face this challenge. Every time a successor presented a new collection, it would face merciless comparison in the court of public opinion.

### 2. Fashion magazines nominated Dior's creative directors, and then its boss would invariably say these people were just like Christian Dior

It is fair to say Dior's initial fame was made by the fashion magazines. In 1947, Dior named his first spring-summer collection Corolle (meaning "corolla" in French). But almost no one remembers "corolla," that awkward botanical term; upon seeing the clothes, people simply blurt out: "New Look!" In fact, that was the assessment of then Harper's Bazaar editor-in-chief Carmel Snow of Dior's designs. "Dior saved Paris, just as the Battle of the Marne saved Paris," Snow said.

Thereafter, as a national brand symbolizing the highest level of the Parisian couture industry — and compounded by Dior's sudden death in 1957 — the company's every move came under the close watch of fashion critics. The influence of fashion media on the fashion industry does not stop at the end of the supply chain. Look at Dior's history after 1957, and sometimes you will be astonished at just how much they could meddle in.

Boussac once considered shutting the company down after Dior's death in 1957, suspending all operations, but the aura the brand represented and its economic contribution to the French fashion industry kept that from happening. A new artistic director was quickly chosen — 21-year-old Yves Saint Laurent. He had joined Dior in 1955 as the only assistant Dior personally selected, and was long regarded as the successor Dior had handpicked himself. In 1958, Saint Laurent presented his new collection. It largely followed Dior's silhouettes and design philosophy while using softer, lighter fabrics to add wearability. The move made Saint Laurent, in Paris, a national hero in the fashion press — the man who saved Dior.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021430247ceINztDwO1G9ylL.jpg-w600)

The tidal wave of praise soon emboldened the young man to spread his wings and plan something big. In 1960, he presented the Beat Look, inspired by the existentialists who haunted Paris's small cafés and jazz clubs. As soon as this decadent, unbridled collection was released, it met with a barrage of harsh criticism, above all from Women's Wear Daily (WWD). That quickly threw Marcel Boussac, the man actually in control behind Dior, into a panic. Using military conscription as the pretext, he promptly kicked Saint Laurent out of Dior that same year. Marc Bohan joined soon after. Compared with Saint Laurent, his style leaned conservative. "Marc Bohan saved this company," WWD commented.

Later, the fashion media simply took on the role of recommending artistic directors to Dior. In 1997, cotton magnate Boussac had gone bankrupt, and Dior — having changed hands several times — ultimately landed with Bernard Arnault, chairman of the LVMH group. He had helped take Dior public in France in 1991, and was determined to revive it. Anna Wintour, then editor-in-chief of American Vogue, recommended to him a brilliant British designer: John Galliano. In 1997, John Galliano became Dior's new artistic director. Arnault even linked Galliano to Christian Dior when speaking to the media. "They both carry that unique quality — a blend of romanticism, feminism, and modernity. That quality is also what Dior stands for. You can find the shadow of Christian in all his work, whether suits or dresses," Arnault said.

What later truly made John Galliano famous, however, were his outrageous, even erotically charged shows and ad films. You could even argue that his unforgettable erotic advertisements influenced Dior more than any single garment design. On that score, Wintour had not recommended the wrong man. Galliano took Dior's flirtatious, romantic design language to a new extreme. So much so that later, including Gucci, quite a few brands in the late 1990s sought attention with large-scale, sexually suggestive ad campaigns.

Yet in 2011, likewise under enormous public pressure, Arnault cast John Galliano out — not for those over-the-top looks, but for his anti-Semitic remarks. Arnault called it an unprecedented "PR crisis" in Dior's history, and even though the Dior 2011/2012 fall-winter ready-to-wear show was just about to begin, he could not wait to announce Galliano's dismissal. It was the only fashion show at which the brand's CEO, rather than the artistic director, walked onto the stage to speak at the end.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702143024M8U9ZIWyYnk3ezHN.jpg-w600)

### 3. Did Raf Simons reinvent Dior? Or was everyone already looking for a new position in a new era

Since Dior's death in 1957, the house's artistic directors have rotated back and forth between designers known for flamboyant personalities and conservatives who cautiously carry on tradition. This may be a shared tension between all designers and brands, but it is especially acute at Dior, a house with a founder who was a national hero and a classic look of era-defining significance. Every new designer arriving at Dior must first look up at this mountain that seems impossible to climb, and only then search for their own coordinates.

In 2012, eager to turn the brand completely around from the John Galliano scandal, Arnault chose the Belgian designer Raf Simons as Dior's new creative director. The previously little-known young man was dubbed a "dark horse," standing out from a crowd of candidates. Arnault once again stood before the media cameras, describing Simons as a figure comparable to the brand's legendary founder. But as everyone knows, Simons is famed for clean, sharp lines — entirely at odds with Dior's past, ornate and romantic style.

The doubts vanished after Raf Simons's fall 2012 haute couture show that July. The collection — dubbed the "New Couture" — revealed both the young man's ambition and the company's signal of reinvention. The show venue was still filled with the flowers that had originally inspired the New Look, but those elaborate decorations did not appear on the garments; in their place were crisp, bright colors and lines, injecting a new modernity into Dior. The new Dior had none of the sound and fury of the Saint Laurent and John Galliano eras, yet in the Raf Simons era it created an entirely new, crisp and forceful expression.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702143025pPbQhvCA5ca37NWT.jpg-w600)

Raf Simons almost never gives interviews, and he never flamboyantly splashes his personality across the surface of a garment, instead concealing it within every meticulously considered cut. On this point, he truly carried the New Look into a brand-new era.

### 4. After the New Look made its mark, just how valuable is the Dior brand?

Just look at how successful its licensed products are.

When it comes to Dior's licensed products, don't put all the blame on Bernard Arnault — when it comes to the founder of the LVMH group, people picture a capitalist with no mercy for designers. As early as the 1950s, while Christian Dior was still alive, the brand had already grown from the New Look into a fashion empire. In 1947, the same year the New Look was released, the Miss Dior perfume — inspired by his sister Christine Dior — was born. It was the fruit of a collaboration between Dior and the famed French fragrance company Coty, and it opened the floodgates for Dior's large-scale development of licensed products. In 1950, Jacques Rouët, then Dior's CEO, decided to print the "Christian Dior" logo on a range of fashion products. At first it was necklaces; then came hats, scarves, bags, lingerie, jewelry, and furs. In France, "haute couture" is a legally protected designation: only those recognized by La Chambre Syndicale de la Couture, the French couture association, are entitled to the name. Dior's move was branded by the association as a kind of "self-degradation." But three years later, nearly every haute couture house in Paris was following Dior's example.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021430262BC5ZUNVcdamop6f.jpg-w600)

In 1953, Dior's footwear line, designed by Roger Vivier, was launched. In 1955 came the Rouge Dior lipstick, in 1962 the first nail polishes, in 1970 menswear — even ready-to-wear and furs were licensed for production in the United States and Japan. In 1975, the first Dior watch, Black Moon, was designed. As the product lines multiplied, Dior also began its global expansion.

Also in that year, Dior for the first time stated in a press release that its business "depended on licensing partnerships." From Dior's perspective, this kind of arrangement let it rapidly expand the scope of its business and reap profits with less capital and controllable risk. But as more and more licensed products hit the market, concerns about their quality and about the brand's image began to surface.

In 1982, Frank Droff, vice president of Macy's in New York, told WWD: "The era when you could sell a product easily on a brand logo alone is over. Everything now depends on the quality of the end product."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702143027p7tnNEPaSv8XwrQA.jpg-w600)

On this point Arnault saw things clearly. In the 1990s he halted nearly half of Dior's licensing business: contracts that expired were not renewed, and stores were brought back under direct operation. In 1998, Dior's CEO Sidney Toledano said publicly: "For a luxury brand worthy of the name, the licensing model is extremely destructive."

On the other hand, Arnault separated out Christian Dior Couture — Dior's haute couture line — and spared no effort to rebuild its high-end image, striving to make people recall Dior's golden age of the 1950s. Especially after Raf Simons became artistic director, Dior even moved its haute couture shows into intimate, closed-off small spaces, with invitations going only to the roughly 20 most influential people in fashion. But you could also watch the live stream on Dior's official website — which amounted to one giant marketing exercise.

Today, although those licensed products still account for an important share of Dior's revenue, Dior's designers hold absolute leadership and control over all of these businesses. After Arnault's sweeping reforms, Dior's growth rate in recent years has even exceeded that of the LVMH group.

### 5. Taking the New Look concept global, Dior's journey continues

Compared with the aggressive licensing model that once dragged Dior into the mire, Dior's channel-expansion strategy seems the more worthy example to follow. Finding new markets is one of the most powerful growth levers for a luxury company — the other being licensed product sales, as described above. If Dior's fashions are luxury goods, sunglasses at most count as premium goods, but no matter: they give you the illusion of having owned a piece of Dior.

Throughout the 1950s, Dior kept expanding westward, with North America and a Western Europe gradually recovering from the war as the focus. The first to snap up the New Look were in fact the Americans. As early as 1946, the United States had ended its fabric shortage, so it welcomed the arrival of the New Look earlier than Europe. In the minds of Americans, Monsieur Dior had become a French celebrity on par with General de Gaulle. In Europe, meanwhile, the New Look only spread after a period of economic recovery. In addition, with Hollywood's star-making machine in motion, Dior rode the classic looks of Grace Kelly, Elizabeth Taylor, and Marlene Dietrich to worldwide fame. Dior once designed the full wardrobe for Marlene Dietrich in Hitchcock's film *Stage Fright*. Elizabeth Taylor once ordered, in one go, 12 Dior gowns designed by Marc Bohan.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702143029TeCMVjEIRa6zbcH3.jpg-w600)

And after Arnault took over in the 1990s, he led the brand to gain first-mover advantage in Asia. In 1993, Dior opened its first store in Beijing. In 2003 and 2004, it opened two dazzling, large flagship stores in succession in Tokyo, becoming local landmarks; only later did more luxury flagship stores gradually open beside them. In 2012, Dior's decision to move its haute couture show to Shanghai was also a "feat" in the luxury world. Today, 37% of Dior's revenue comes from Asia.

It is worth noting that when a haloed brand like Dior — "it almost stands for France! For fashion!" — enters an emerging market, celebrities, politicians, and nouveau riche businessmen naturally flock to it, trying to demonstrate their taste by owning these goods. Such customers may alter the brand's customer profile and also diminish its mystique — people will care more about its new releases and prices than about the artistry and innovation of the products. Dior must spend more effort maintaining the New Look's reputation, and expressing its grasp of the present era — which often manifests in its haute couture shows.

As emerging markets take an ever-larger share of dominance, the tension between commercial profit and social status exists in every luxury brand.
