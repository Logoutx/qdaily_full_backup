What Are Chinese Companies Really After, Scrambling — Even “Grouping Up” — to Buy Ads in Times Square?

@@QD_BODY@@
For them, the symbolic value of being in Times Square far outweighs any real return on investment.

During the Year of the Monkey Spring Festival, Tsingtao Beer once again appeared on the billboards of Times Square — the third year in a row it had done so; a week before the holiday, giant WeChat red envelopes appeared on the big screens, which passersby could “shake” with Bluetooth turned on; and Wanglaoji had started even earlier, kicking off its Spring Festival marketing at Times Square in late December the year before.

A domestic company called Huashang Taolue also organized 100 Chinese brands this Spring Festival, and 66 during National Day last year, to run ads collectively on the big screens of Times Square. The list included industry heavyweights such as Greenland Group, Hainan Airlines, TCL, Gree, and Geely — and, for roughly half of it, names you may never even have heard of.

![The 100 brands that collectively took over Times Square for Spring Festival](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021524383cx5SBWYoNs7EkGI.jpg-w600)

Add Meitu, Didi, Coolpad, Huamei Food, Peak, Hisense range hoods, Henan's Synear Food and the like, and the Chinese companies that showed up on Times Square billboards over the past year number roughly one to two hundred.

In this plaza known as “the Crossroads of the World,” the earliest Chinese advertising was actually official: in January 2011, China's national image promotional video aired here. It led a wave of local governments to follow — Shanghai, Suzhou, Jiangxi, Wuhan, Quanzhou; by incomplete counts, more than 30 cities have come here to run promotional videos. Their purpose was direct: attract tourists or foreign investment.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152438j7TvQZXcsMEoiOBD.jpg-w600)

Chinese companies, too, seemed to have suddenly found another shortcut “to the world.” In August 2011, Wuliangye's promotional video appeared in Times Square, with Samsung and Coca-Cola ads directly beneath it; Gree soon followed suit.

![A Wuliangye ad](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152439YvaL6FQHg3epIlPK.jpg-w600)

But the domestic company with the most cumulative ad placements and the densest frequency was Shi Yuzhu's Giant Group — the company behind 17 years of brainwashing Naobaijin commercials. Starting in April 2012, it ran a string of game ads on the big screens of Times Square, including *ZT Online 2S*, *Allods Online*, and *Xianxia World*.

![An ad for the game ZT Online 2S](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152439FogGE75I3afw1Kiq.jpg-w600)

Since then, more and more Chinese companies have scrambled to Times Square to “check in.” For them, the symbolic value of being in Times Square far outweighs any real return on investment. Most are not really trying to attract overseas consumers; they hope to bring the influence and the talking points back home.

Simply put, you can think of it as an “exported, then re-imported” model: the ad playing in Times Square is not necessarily meant for the people in Times Square — you snap a photo, bring it back, and show it to people at home: “Look, we're on the classy Times Square!”

The logic is exactly the same as that of the entertainment industry's “red-carpet stars” who flock to international film festivals. Nobody abroad may notice your appearance, but back home you can put out a story like “Actress XX shines on the international stage, outclassing all the rest.”

To be fair, some companies genuinely have markets in the US — though even these target mostly Chinese-speaking communities. Tsingtao Beer, for example, shows up mostly in the supermarkets and restaurants of Chinatown. And if, in the past, some newly listed companies may have wanted to list on the New York Stock Exchange in the future and attract overseas investors, the recent wave of U.S.-listed Chinese companies delisting from the US has likely ruled out that motive.

Because Times Square ad slots can be sold flexibly by the day or even by the minute, many companies get the chance to show their faces. And a group of brokers who shuttle Chinese companies to Times Square have helped fuel the trend.

If you look closely, you may notice that Chinese companies “favor” just a handful of ad slots: the Nasdaq big screen on Tower 4 in Times Square; the screen directly above McDonald's; an LED screen at the base of Tower 1 (the building with the New Year's Eve ball drop); a spot to the upper right of AT&T on Tower 3; and the frequently seen Tower 2, which, like Tower 1, is trapezoid-shaped.

![The ad screen leased by Xinhua Gallery, a company under Xinhua](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152440GYv0ghFNLf2jlWHo.jpg-w600)

![Guoyu Wujiang's “China Red Screen”](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152441fkMcRhtBG86wmesU.jpg-w600)

And these ad slots are each represented by different domestic companies. The screen at Tower 1 has been operated by Dalian outdoor media group Guoyu Wujiang since March 1, 2012, and was even named the “China Red Screen”; the billboard on Tower 2 was leased in August 2011 by Xinhua Gallery, a wholly owned subsidiary of Xinhua; and the spot directly above McDonald's was leased by Lanse Tianmu, a subsidiary of BlueFocus, from an overseas agency. QDaily covered this earlier in [“Who Sold Those Tacky Ads to New York's Swanky Times Square?”](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/1762.html). So for Chinese companies, this may also be a kind of inertial dependence.

![Lanse Tianmu Screen No. 1](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152442bxRqZJlamcTEK5zj.jpg-w600)

Prices for Times Square billboards vary widely depending on location, size, duration, screen quality, and other factors. The Nasdaq big screen, for instance, costs roughly twice as much as the “China Red Screen.”

Huashang Taolue, which brought the 100 Chinese companies to Times Square collectively, is one of the new brokers. Huashang Taolue began as a company dedicated to writing biographies for Chinese entrepreneurs; over the decade from 2005 to 2014, it published an annual *Global Chinese Business Hall of Fame* (《全球华商名人堂》) anthology, accumulating plenty of business resources along the way. In 2014, it pivoted into advertising and self-media.

“There are two kinds of Times Square advertising. In the early days it was mainly big companies buying out the space in bulk to show off their brand's commanding status. The other function is simply to get more people to know you — whether it's impressive or not doesn't matter; you might spend a tenth of what you used to and still get noticed,” said Huashang Taolue founder Bi Yajun.

Bi Yajun told QDaily that the “100 brands” project was their first attempt at a new model, which they see as a kind of “crowdfunded advertising” — essentially group buying.

An advertising agency comes up with a theme and finds a batch of interested companies to collectively buy ad inventory and run it. The benefit is that the ad becomes an “event” — it raises influence while cutting costs, like going from retail to wholesale.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152443ufib4gKVlDYmZLEX.jpg-w600)

The cost reduction is obvious. According to the 2014 rate card of domestic agency Lanse Tianmu, running a two-week ad in Times Square cost 1.18 million yuan; even at 50% off the list price, it would still run over 500,000 yuan (though in that case the frequency is relatively higher as well).

Huashang Taolue, for its 100-brand event, charged each company only tens of thousands of yuan. It set a uniform standard: each brand's ad, in Chinese and English versions, would run twice each, 15 seconds per spot, for 60 seconds of total exposure. “This is a very practical deal — the cost is much lower, but the effect isn't discounted by much.”

And the “tens of thousands of yuan” was a package price: on top of the Times Square ad itself, it covered a full-service plan — on-site photos, follow-up publicity across various channels in China, and so on. Of the several million yuan in total fees from the 100 companies, only about one million yuan actually went to buying Times Square ad time.

“If I could, I'd rather spend just 100,000 yuan on the ad and put the rest into promotion at home,” Bi said. Huashang Taolue owns no screens of its own; it, too, pays screen owners for ad time. Since the minimum purchase is 60 seconds, they bought one hundred 60-second spots. And because competition among the overseas agencies had already squeezed most of the fat out of their prices, buying 100 at once did not earn them much of a “wholesale” discount.

The Times Square advertising business is a mature system today. Times Square's official website publishes detailed foot-traffic data — pedestrian counts for every block around the square, traffic flow changes, and so on. In January 2016, daily foot traffic was 293,600. You can see the detailed data in the chart below.

![Times Square foot-traffic distribution, January 2016](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152443PgCMEHG056lTSvsm.jpg-w600)

As for the New Year's Eve celebration each year, it draws well over 100 million people. So Times Square earned the reputation of a barometer of the US economy very early on. [According to Ad Age](http://adage.com/article/news/cost-advertising-times-square/45719/), it had already become America's most expensive outdoor advertising location ten years ago: the minimum monthly price for ad slots on Towers 1 and 2 was US$350,000. At the time, annual foot traffic was about 40 million — roughly 14% of the US population — and Kodak predicted that more than 100 million photos were taken there every year. Even now, it remains a popular photo spot on Instagram. That is why Coca-Cola has been advertising there continuously since 1935.

The Chinese companies that come to Times Square may not care much about any of this; what they care about is probably still the ringing name “Times Square” itself. To them, it works like a golden signboard. And although Times Square ad prices have more than doubled compared with ten years ago, for Chinese companies it is not as expensive as people assume.

![A Wanglaoji ad](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021524441xtDn7IOS9YEKr2G.jpg-w600)

![A Tsingtao Beer ad](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152444p0Z3yqKbStAreHE2.jpg-w600)

“Going to Times Square to run an ad isn't the important part; what matters is in what form and under what banner you run it, and whether it translates into influence back home — whether more people get to know about it,” Bi said. “We've been frank with companies about this: what matters is the symbolic meaning, not a headcount of how many people walked past the billboard that day.”

In Huashang Taolue's package, running the ad is just one link in the overall communications plan — the follow-up publicity is the main event. “For example, we'll run ads on Moments, reaching 10 to 20 million impressions — the impact is far greater. We'll also write soft articles and place reports across various channels, and companies will promote it through their own PR firms and self-media accounts.”

We have little doubt that a headline like “100 Chinese Brands Appear Together in Times Square” can generate buzz in domestic media and on social networks. As for actual results? Like almost all brand advertising, they are very hard to measure. And with the exposure spread across 100 brands, the actual benefit each brand receives is not as simple as a division problem. Big companies and small companies also gain different things from this. But having read this far, how many of those 100 companies can you still name?

According to Huashang Taolue's statistics, roughly half of the 100 companies have no business in the US at all — some operate only in the Beijing market. If you understand the “exported, then re-imported” logic, you'll see that whether or not you do business in the US doesn't really matter.

How many big companies to recruit, how many small ones, how many from each industry — Huashang Taolue had a plan from the start. In the process, it found big companies easier to work with, because they immediately grasped the model, while educating small companies was harder — getting them to understand that it was fine not to do business in New York.

“We have never told a company, ‘If you want to do business in New York, you should buy Times Square ads through us.’ Even big companies join our projects not out of practical business considerations, but for branding,” Bi said. He gave an example: Greenland's “Pacific Park” real-estate project in New York happened to be launching right around National Day, when the 66 brands ran their ads. But if Greenland were acting out of commercial considerations, it would not have chosen a symbolic place like Times Square; it would study where its consumers actually are, where its customers are, and place ads accordingly.

From a publicity standpoint, bringing along some big companies was necessary; but for practical business reasons, Huashang Taolue hoped to develop more small companies as long-term clients. And with big companies, a small agency like this has little say.

![A Greenland Group ad](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021524455LQYEzuHdwMkBvcJ.jpg-w600)

![A Gree ad](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152445DYTMhcwxnrlIHR93.jpg-w600)

But even if you know it's all hype, that doesn't stop it from being a business.

“Times Square was chosen for ads for two reasons. One, it grabs eyeballs. Two, as long as ‘advertising in Times Square’ still signifies class and prestige to most audiences, some people will keep exploiting that psychology, and the model will remain valuable to brands,” Bi said.

That said, the model is not sustainable. Once more and more companies go to Times Square, the halo on its head loses its meaning.

In the 1980s and 1990s, Japanese and Korean companies also took over Times Square's big screens in groups, accompanying Sony, Panasonic, Samsung and other brands as they pushed into the US market. Different eras of Times Square attracted different advertisers, and the shift from Japan to China also reflects changes in the international market. This is typically a stage-specific product of a country's commercial development; the fever subsides as the business environment matures, and symbolic value gives way to more practical cost considerations.

![Nissin Foods' former ad in Times Square; the company did not renew the lease when it expired a few years ago.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702152446y5YL2P4bF1fdkVCm.jpg-w600)

For now, though, “Times Square” still holds its status, and the meme has not worn out. But it may not be that these companies themselves endorse Times Square — “rather, these companies believe that, in Chinese people's eyes, Times Square is still a persuasive place.”

Is that really so?
