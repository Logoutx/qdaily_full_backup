That said, from 2007 to 2017, what they've been thinking has never quite matched what the general public thinks.

"Go help my neighbor — she's so talented."

In 2007, Milan native Andreina Longhi first took notice of Chinese designers through a friend's remark. Thirty years had passed since she left the marketing department at Max Mara to found Attila&Co., a fashion brand marketing agency, and it had been nearly five years since her first trip to China.

2007 was a year worth remembering for Chinese fashion designers. The term "Chinese independent designer" appeared for the first time at scale in domestic media. That year, Qiu Hao, fresh home from Central Saint Martins, registered his eponymous brand Qiu Hao in London, and the media were happy to call him the first Chinese designer to return home after graduating from "Saint Martins, the world's top fashion school." Around the same time, Zhou Xiangyu (Xander Zhou), back from the fashion academy in Den Haag, the Netherlands, and Shangguan Zhe (Sankuanz), who set up his studio in Xiamen, also founded eponymous brands.

The "neighbor" in Andreina Longhi's friend's words was Uma Wang. Andreina saw her present "flowers, candles, and some wonderful clothes" in "a small room." Uma Wang had already registered a studio in London at the time, but did not yet have her own brand. After that meeting in the small room, Andreina Longhi took her to show in Milan and into the pages of Italian Vogue. Today, the Chinese designer brand's average price per customer runs as high as 5,000 to 20,000 yuan, and its retail footprint has expanded to more than 70 outlets worldwide.

Uma Wang was one of the first independent designers to become widely known to Chinese consumers. Before her, a cohort of independent designers born in the 1960s and 1970s had shown their work overseas: Wu Haiyan, Liu Yang, Xie Feng, Liang Zi, Ma Ke, and Wang Tao. Among them, Ma Ke was invited in 2008 to be the first to show on the Paris haute couture calendar.

Yet Chinese consumers were hardly familiar with this list of names — even Andreina Longhi, who had spent more than 30 years in the fashion industry, had initially only read about them in French magazines.

Uma Wang's own path to debut was a long one. She did her undergraduate degree at Donghua University (then known as China Textile University) in the 1990s, and in 2002 completed an eight-month short course in accessories at Central Saint Martins in London. By the time she first walked the Milan Spring/Summer Fashion Week in 2011, she was 39.

![Uma Wang, 2017 Autumn/Winter](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170417002412uNeQHV1GcYs3dUPT.jpg-w600)

For today's younger generation of fashion designers, life is a bit easier. As with many consumer industries in China, fashion design has grown fast: young designers tend to come from better-off family backgrounds; degrees from schools like Saint Martins and Parsons are more common; they find more funding opportunities, more professional packaging, buyer channels, and showcase platforms at home; and even department stores — the mass market — are starting to extend olive branches to them as they chase greater diversity.

Around 2013, brands like Shangguan Zhe's Sankuanz and Liu Xiaolu's (Dido) Deepmoss became better known through the growing influence of Shanghai Fashion Week. And now Andreina Longhi's client roster has added several new names: Angel Chen, Samuel Gui Yang, and Percy Lau.

Compared with the two earlier waves of designers, most of them were born around 1990; after studying abroad they set up brands and studios directly overseas; their brands have existed anywhere from one to five years, with products priced between 1,000 and 8,000 yuan. They understand modern life in China under a market economy, yet have also spent three to eight years living in the West at the world's top schools. They understand fashion design, and also know a bit about styling, art, music, and culture. They are hardworking and driven, and — just like their consumers — they are fluent in social media and like to express themselves.

Yes, fashion design remains an extremely fragmented and niche presence in China's overall consumer market. As one young man we met, Dorian, put it: when it comes to fashion consumption, most people either "can't understand it," "can't afford it," or "have no occasion to wear it." And yet, as an industry, fashion now has far more possibilities than it did ten years ago — and far more optimism.

But "identity" remains a problem. As fashion columnist Leng Yun puts it: "Post-90s designers are arguably internationalized, but they also have no identity — achieving visual impact gets harder and harder. Even commercial success doesn't necessarily translate into design influence."

"Opportunities may look more plentiful, but they will also run into problems completely different from the previous generation of designers," Andreina Longhi says.

### (1) These People Are So Fresh!

On April 9, Angel Chen and French singer Petite Meller held a concert together in Venue No. 4 of Labelhood, located at the Bailian Fashion Group Center.

Labelhood is an offshoot venue for independent designers that Shanghai Fashion Week launched in 2015. It is independently operated by a company called Leihu, and one of its operators is Tasha, a co-founder of the Chinese fashion buyer store Dong Liang.

For three to four days every April and October, young designers whose applications are accepted get to present themselves here to media, buyers, and ordinary consumers in the form of a presentation or an exhibition. This year, a new format was added: a live concert.

Angel Chen and Tasha met in London. In October 2013, at the joint invitation of Shanghai Fashion Week and the British Fashion Council (BFC), Dong Liang took part in Design By Shanghai, an overseas promotion project at London Fashion Week, as the only Chinese designer concept store. Tasha brought five designers — Liu Min, Du Yang, Shangguan Zhe, He Yan, and Su Renli — to walk the runway. The following year, she also squeezed in a visit to Central Saint Martins' graduation show, where she saw the work of Chinese graduates Angel Chen and Guo Yirantian.

The runway shows went over well. During Shanghai Fashion Week in April 2014, on a whim, Tasha brought the London presentation format back to Shanghai and began "Dong Liang Day," held once a season. The first season featured designers Shangguan Zhe, Guo Yirantian, Zhang Da, He Yan, and Nan Lang; the second season grew to seven — Zhang Da, Su Renli, Angel Chen, Guo Yirantian, Liu Xiaolu, Wang Haizhen, and Feng Yuqi — and brought in Lorenzo, founder of the American buyer store H.Lorenzo, and fashion critic Colin McDowell. The third season explicitly split into runway shows and presentations: the four runway brands were Nicole Zhang (Zhang Xu), Hiuman (Zhou Xiaowen), Museum of Friendship (Wang Tianmo), and Boundless (Zhang Da), while the presentations were Deepmoss (Liu Xiaolu), Babyghost (Huang Qiaoran and Joshua Hupper), Percy Lau, Yirantian (Guo Yirantian), and Angel Chen.

Clearly, platforms like "Dong Liang Day" and Labelhood give young designers like Angel Chen and Guo Yirantian the chance, right after graduation, to walk the runway alongside designers who have already made a name for themselves in the domestic market.

![Labelhood, 2017 Autumn/Winter; the venue was at the Bailian Fashion Group Center in Yanqingli, Shanghai](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170417002914jZu6H5m1nfA8UGek.JPG-w600)

But the greatest significance of these platforms is how a small lever moves a great weight. For young designers, the Shanghai Autumn/Winter Fashion Week held every April is a bit awkward on the calendar. Many of them have already taken part in the big four international fashion weeks in February and March and completed their order-taking for the world. Staging another show costs time and money that these fledgling brands can hardly afford, while the additional orders from domestic buyers may not amount to much. Presentation-based formats, therefore, make far more economic sense.

In fact, presentations test designers' storytelling ability even more. Beyond the clothes themselves, styling, setting, music, movement, and mood are all part of the story; designers have more freedom, and it helps the audience better understand a brand's character.

Take the state of girlhood, for example. SHUSHU/TONG's girls always carry a daydream quality — for Spring/Summer 2017 the venue was dressed as a garden, with drowsy-eyed girls playing with petals; for Autumn/Winter 2017 the backdrop was a classroom, imagining what good students, bad students, and teachers would wear. Museum of Friendship's girls, meanwhile, are full of whimsy — for Spring/Summer 2017 they formed a choir, and by autumn/winter the girls were planning to set off on adventures with their passports in hand.

Compared with the generation of designers represented by Ma Ke and Uma Wang, these brands — most of them barely three years old — display far more varied personalities. Angel Chen is flamboyant: she shaved her head, leaving a small braid trailing from the back, and loves a bold red lip. Her Angel Chen is likewise an explosion of color with varied fabric weaves — last season drew on Shanhaiguan, this season's inspiration shifted to Morocco in Africa, and the trench coat most reposted on social media carries the words "In heaven above and on earth below, I alone am the honored one" across its back.

Guo Yirantian, by contrast, is reserved, with black hair parted in the middle and simple dress; in media interviews she always pauses to think for a moment before answering slowly. Her clothes are more mature, dominated by composed browns, grays, blacks, and whites, with clean cuts and an emphasis on fabric texture; most of her buyers are women aged 30 to 45. Asked "how do you see the relationship between age and style?", she will look up and retort: "Does maturity really have anything to do with age?"

On one hand, these designers' presentations try to interpret the lives of contemporary Chinese people; on the other, they connect with themes trending on international runways. DANSHAN, for instance, considered that "the pressure contemporary Chinese men face comes mainly from cut-throat competition and the absence of emotional education," turning the venue into cubicles, the runway script into job application forms, and covering a wall opposite the cubicles with job postings satirizing the limits placed on men's careers. Liu Xiaolu's Deepmoss, Xu Jiazheng's Sans Titre, and Li Jiapei's Andrea Jiapei Li all made space and the cosmos their runway or exhibition themes, echoing the international fashion world's revival of 1960s science-fiction motifs.

Even menswear, which seems less varied than womenswear, is slowly exploring its own style. Born in Beijing, educated in London, and working in Paris, Li Yang trained under Raf Simons, sources fabrics and manufactures in Italy, and has been called a "luxury streetwear brand" by i-D; this time at Labelhood he used a fictional music label, "Samizdat" (meaning underground publications), to introduce performances by bands he loves. Pronounce is known for fabric patchwork — distinctive yet wearable; its Autumn/Winter 2017 collection was inspired by the Rarámuri people, who love long-distance running, and the models entered the venue in five groups like members of the tribe, presenting male groups with different personalities. Junwei Lin excels at crafting the "ideal boyfriend" in women's eyes; the venue was staged as a wedding, with pretty, delicate male models in slim shirts trimmed with ruffles, walking out holding flowers and candles.

They also care less than the previous generation about their native identity. Angus Chiang of Taiwan, after graduating from Central Saint Martins, went on to complete three menswear collections inspired by, respectively, the trolley culture, banquet culture, and campus culture of rural Taiwan. He doesn't worry at all whether audiences in other markets will find these elements unfamiliar — because "that's exactly what piques their curiosity."

For those who know the European and American fashion system all too well — or are even tired of it — these new ideas and formats are a breath of fresh air. Jack Cassidy, a menswear buyer at Selfridges, spoke with barely concealed excitement about being invited to Labelhood for the first time this year: "You know, after sitting through so many exhausting runway shows, coming to Shanghai and seeing these young designers' presentations — there's a fusion of international vision and local inspiration, and seeing the staff run around in handsome uniforms, treating the platform as a brand in itself, is a wonderful surprise." His only regret is that his budget was already spent at the big four fashion weeks, and placing another order would require a separate budget request.

Some Chinese buyers and ordinary audience members see it differently. To some of them, presentations "without a formal runway show" feel disappointing, and they may not understand the difference between runway looks and the pieces actually sold. "It's like graduation work — still rough. Some things, like the long sleeves and oversized trench coats, don't look like anything new," one audience member, who described himself as a performance artist, told QDaily.

Anja Aronowsky-Cronberg, editor-in-chief of the fashion academic journal Vestoj, disapproves of comparing Chinese designers' past with that of Europe and America. "Compared with their peers in other countries, young Chinese designers are indeed generally better off economically. But China has only gone through a short period of development — Paris Fashion Week has a history of more than 100 years; you can't catch up. Formats like Labelhood, more avant-garde and novel, are instead an opportunity where China can stand out. You don't need to follow the Western fashion system; carving out a new one of your own is the more exciting thing."

To help such audiences better understand the designers, Andreina Longhi guided Angel Chen in hanging the video of her Milan Fashion Week runway show at the Labelhood concert venue, and Zamira, one of the founders of Tube Showroom, would remind Chen Xuzhi to remember to hang the video of his London runway show in the showroom at the order fairs.

"Let them know what you've done on the world stage."

### (2) Is It Easier to Be a Designer Now?

The new wave of designers has also happened to catch a good moment in manufacturing's transformation.

In the past, factories accustomed to state export subsidies were unwilling to serve independent designers, whose orders were typically small, whose craftsmanship was complex, and whose labor hours were hard to calculate. Now, with manufacturing shifting elsewhere, Chinese garment factories have begun seeking transformation and looking for new partners — such as the ever-growing community of independent designers.

Angel Chen and Wang Fengchen put their production lines in Shanghai, and both use suppliers from Chenfeng Group. The 50-year-old garment manufacturing group is an OEM for brands such as Marc by Marc Jacobs, Tsumori Chisato, and Adidas; its "brand garment partners" listed on its website now include Lü Yan's Comme Moi and its own womenswear brand Blinkgallery. Starting around 2015, it also began providing studios plus production lines of about 400 square meters for its independent-designer partners.

Kim, head of marketing at Chenfeng Group, declined an interview. But according to Wang Fengchen of the brand Feng Chen Wang, speaking to QDaily, her collaboration with Chenfeng Group began at the end of April 2016. On a whim, the factory manager lent them an entire empty floor — the fourth floor of the An Ting factory in Shanghai — as a studio, with the production line on the second floor. To solve the problems of cutting and craftsmanship, the manager also assigned her senior workers from the sample-making team — compared with assembly-line workers, sample makers can operate independently and complete a garment on their own. In addition, the labor fee calculation was changed from a per-piece rate to an hourly rate.

"The per-piece model makes workers ignore quality and care only about quantity. Our current model is one person completing a single piece, paid by the hour, so he has the mind to study the craft. He doesn't have to think, oh my God, I only make one or two garments a week."

![Feng Chen Wang, 2017 Autumn/Winter](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170417010725CmxXiRYKdpvVbf9a.jpg-w600)

More and more brand sponsorships and crossover collaborations are also flooding toward independent designers. Big companies all want to attract young people; brand marketing now talks about youth culture, circle marketing, and segmented markets — making independent designers a fitting target.

At the just-concluded fashion week, KFC brought in four designer brands — Rico Lee, Ren Qian, Yirantian, and Deepmoss — asking them to fold KFC's freshly ground coffee into their runway designs; Nike and Converse, respectively, appeared on the feet of models in the exhibitions or runway shows of Pronounce, Wanbing Huang, SHUSHU/TONG, DANSHAN, Hiuman, Xu Zhi, and Junwei Lin. Earlier, Chow Tai Fook and K11 approached Yirantian to launch a jewelry collection, and Gap also plans to release a collaboration collection with Pronounce.

Last year, Angel Chen also got sponsorship from Mercedes-Benz and walked the official schedule of Milan Fashion Week. "The stylist, the photographer, the makeup artist — the whole team was assembled by Mercedes-Benz. They told me to add a few hoodies so the collection would feel more complete on the runway. I went to Milan four months in advance and worked with the local team for a while to complete the remaining 30% of the collection. The final show had more than 120 SKUs." The presentation also earned her an invitation from Milan Fashion Week's organizing committee for the next season.

Another notable shift is in sales channels. Guan Lin, founder of Short Sentence, graduated from the fashion marketing program at Parsons in New York, returned to China, and worked for several years at the commercial womenswear brand Viviniko before deciding to start her own business.

At order fairs, Guan Lin set the minimum order quantity at 1 — unlike the menswear commercial brand Consistence, whose minimum order runs as high as 30,000. The practice is designed to accommodate the growing and ever-more-varied buyer stores. "You can see everyone is transforming. Department stores want to open their own concept stores, buyer stores want to move into shopping malls, and luxury brands want to sell home goods and perfume in their own direct-operated stores, making them like concept stores too."

This, in turn, gives niche brands more chances to reach consumers. Beyond SKP, which opened its own buyer store "SKP Select" in 2015, Beijing's Hanguang Department Store and Chengdu's Renhe Chuntian Department Store have both approached Guan Lin. "Buyers are becoming more professional."

### (3) But... Where Are the Customers?

"I don't understand it. China already has so many excellent designers, yet people still trust foreign brands more. Why is that?"

"Look at all these beautiful clothes at the order fair — why is it so hard to spot anyone wearing them on the street?"

These questions came, respectively, from Andreina Longhi and Hong Kong designer Minki.

Everyone has noticed the gap between this seemingly limitless market and actual consumer response. On one hand, the throngs of Chinese customers crowding flagship stores of luxury brands overseas leave fashion professionals feeling that this market has never lacked the powerful spending strength and vitality that other countries envy. On the other hand, walk down the street and you can barely find anything by the new generation of Chinese designers.

Fashion week seems cut off from people's lives: Labelhood is like an island of industry revelry, where most of the crowd is media, buyers, and friends and family of participants — models, photographers, stylists, designers, bands — plus fashion design students. Outside this circle, very few people know the designers.

Industry insiders such as Leng Yun argue that "this current crop of designers is exploratory in nature and does not yet represent commercialization." Another take is that China's huge, scattered market is still slowly adapting to "what fashion actually is."

"When I first came to China, many fashion industry people had a serious problem: they thought fashion had to be exaggerated, shocking, above and beyond everyday life. Consumers, seeing street snaps and those magazines, also saw fashion as some demonized thing. But that isn't fashion — it was originally very simple; it is actually a part of life," Andreina Longhi says.

We also met 26-year-old Dorian at Shanghai Fashion Week. He completed two years of undergraduate study at Donghua University plus two years of menswear design study in Italy, then worked for two years at Uniqlo's design department and at the apparel brand Neiwai. Most recently, he was just admitted to a master's program at the London College of Fashion.

Dorian is not optimistic about the domestic market: "You see how diverse overseas consumer markets are — that's because the consumption scenarios of fashion and the culture that breeds it are very diverse: punk, rock, art, equal rights — countless small circles give rise to countless fashion consumption behaviors, and people use clothing to signal that they belong to a certain circle. In China, by contrast, such scenarios only became familiar to people in recent years as social media developed, but in daily life they remain very niche, and independent designers can't push prices very low given the constraints of production and channels. Even people who are willing to understand and have the money to buy may not know what occasion to wear it to, because there are so few such occasions."

From the designers' perspective, they too are puzzling over how to find their own consumers. In Europe and America, young designers join established brands and learn how to grasp consumer demand; but in China, there are very few companies suitable as career starting points, which greatly raises the entrepreneurial risk for young designers.

The young designers interviewed by QDaily all expressed varying degrees of anxiety: funding, connections, and exposure. Each person's need for the three differs, and the way they talk about them ranges from implicit to blunt — but how much exposure, and how to get it, is a source of anxiety for nearly all of them. In an environment with so many brands and such fragmented information channels, whether you can be heard and understood directly determines whether your products sell.

Showrooms and PR firms now generally help designers gain exposure in two ways: celebrity marketing, and placements in fashion magazine editorials. While the variety show *Ace vs Ace* (《王牌对王牌》) was on the air, Tube Showroom used its resources to get Bai Baihe to arrive at Chengdu airport wearing a pair of frayed jeans by Xu Zhi; after entertainment and fashion media reposted the airport photo far and wide, the jeans sold out fast. Xu Jiazheng's Sans Titre was founded only in 2015 and has a single store in Beijing; but after celebrities like Yang Yang and Kris Wu wore the brand, media attention and consumers came flooding in like water from a tap, leaving the designer fretting over how the supply chain could keep up and how to maintain quality and price.

![Tube Showroom](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170417011324f91k2howmiDFnyVX.jpg-w600)

![Not Showroom](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170417011307gMloe7jwIPqrEKxS.jpg-w600)

One fairly effective solution is to nail down your own positioning, rather than merely chase a so-called style. Designer Guan Lin argues: "Overseas consumers are already very mature in fashion consumption and taste, so they're willing to buy high-value basic pieces from brands like Acne and Everlance online. In China, demand for basics has already been met by fast-fashion brands like Uniqlo, Zara, and Gap; what's missing are brands positioned in the mid-range that also care about design."

The Short Sentence she founded is Tube Showroom's best-selling brand, priced from 1,000 to 4,000 yuan.

Of course, social networks are always the most convenient route. Starting last October, Labelhood began assembling "Cool Kids" to cover the shows. Most of them are new-media or self-media personalities who have amassed large followings on Weibo or WeChat — such as YohoGirl and fashion blogger Yvonne Ching, whose Weibo follower counts reach 1.66 million and 1.21 million respectively. This April, Labelhood also brought in 18 bloggers to livestream the event via its official account on Yizhibo, with a combined following of at least 4.3 million.

No one can say exactly what kind of consumers those 4.3 million followers are, what brands of clothes they usually buy, or whether they'd be willing to spend 1,000 to 8,000 yuan on a piece from a designer brand less than five years old. But it is plain to see that the number alone can put many minds at ease.
