## This Kind of People

"Follow Your Heart," a netizen born in the 1970s, is a farmer in Hubei. During the slack farming season in autumn and winter, he makes a habit of posting follow-posts on NetEase, and he is especially good at writing poetry. Under an article about a contractor in Xiangyang who fell ill but was still owed back wages, he wrote an acrostic poem whose first characters read, "Utterly helpless — one long sigh."

"Just venting anger in the name of poetry," this follow-poster, known on NetEase as "the Poet," said of his own published verses. "Nothing poetic about it."

"Dynamic Superman Superfly," who wants to become a scientist, has never met "Follow Your Heart," but on the internet they belong to the same species. He claims he was inspired by a remark from Fan Changjiang, a reporter for *Ta Kung Pao* in the early 20th century, and began posting follow-posts on NetEase in 2014. The elder statesman of Chinese journalism said: "News is the important facts that the masses want to know, should know, and do not yet know." Although he is only 18, "Dynamic Superman Superfly" says he does not hesitate to speak out about society.

There are far more than these two follow-posters on NetEase, but a large share of them can be summed up as follows: keen to comment on current affairs and spread opinions in the public sphere, they are sharp and forceful in their wording, rallying people to their side.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021740558xJATiaXcIQStZBR.jpg-w600)

Huang Yu first read NetEase News in high school, and her values took a hit at the time. Now a senior in the law department at Sichuan International Studies University, she can calmly rattle off the characteristics of NetEase follow-posts: "Full of regional attacks," "America is either heaven or dog shit," "Criticize everything the government does, and add: this is China!"...

Naturally, this group of people speaking out on NetEase does not entirely meet the standard for public intellectuals proposed in the 1980s by the American writer Russell Jacoby: professional competence, a global vision, a sense of social responsibility, action to transform society, and the courage to serve as a guide for society.

Yet this group does somewhat converge with the embryo of the public intellectual in France's 1894 Dreyfus Affair. At the time, the writer Zola published an open letter in the newspaper addressed to the President of the French Republic (also known as *J'Accuse!*), rallying a large contingent of scholars, teachers, media workers, and students to support Dreyfus, a trainee officer sentenced to life in exile on insufficient evidence — and public opinion swung in an instant.

![Russell Jacoby's *The Last Intellectuals*, which proposes five standards for intellectuals](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021740551jqVlmIk0z93iCvR.jpg-w600)

Except that in the internet age, thanks to the rise of user-driven content generation, these voices are heard more easily and can gain influence even without the ability to publish an open letter in a newspaper.

"Drillbit," who would not reveal his real name, admits he found a sense of belonging on NetEase. He works at a culture magazine in Chengdu and cares about current affairs. What frustrates him is that anything even slightly sensitive he comes into contact with at work gets replaced. But not on NetEase. "It makes you feel less alone. Beyond yourself and the people around you, you know there are still people in China who dare to speak." "Drillbit" also compared Guancha.cn, another current-affairs website, and felt that "NetEase's IQ is still a bit higher."

"Peachy Wang" started out as a devoted reader of *Vista* magazine, buying nearly every issue, and thereby picked up some fuzzy knowledge of current affairs. "After getting on NetEase, I really came to appreciate the place, and my thinking was clearly pulled in." He spends one to two hours a day on NetEase — "I refresh news more often than social media!" Later, "Peachy Wang" describes himself as having become a "US cent" — someone who praises anything American. When netizens and roommates took positions against him, he quickly mastered effective ways to fight back.

The purely "representational" and "disclosive" mode of journalism that Jürgen Habermas criticized is being transformed. Follow-posters participate in news dissemination two ways, through dialogue, becoming "public intellectuals." As spokespeople for a certain class, they want to "change the world" — like the "organic intellectuals" Gramsci proposed in *Prison Notebooks*.

## These "Public Intellectuals" Follow a Mode of Speech Called the "Follow-Post"

On December 30, 2008, Tang Yan — founder of Momo — was still editor-in-chief of NetEase News.

He and his team released a five-minute [Flash video](http://news.163.com/special/0001sp/2008ending.html) as a year-end review. The editors had spent nearly a month sifting its material from 41,658,635 follow-posts on 2,397,339 news items that year, and the video prominently featured the slogan "No follow-posts, no news."

That was the sixth year after the launch of the follow-post feature, and it was being elevated to an extremely important position. It went on to prove its value again and again. According to data released by iResearch in July 2015, NetEase News had a monthly reach of 38.3% and an average browsing time of 23 minutes, exceeding Sina, Tencent, Toutiao, and Sohu News.

![A 2013 ad for NetEase follow-posts](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702174056mBKWUEbRXTqASrOp.jpg-w600)

The watershed came in 2003, when Li Xueling — then content director, now founder of the internet voice platform provider JOYY — launched "NetEase Follow-Posts."

In 2006, Li Yong, not long after becoming NetEase's chief editor, once again emphasized the interactivity of NetEase News. "Simply adding a comment page after the news is far from enough. We need to give users greater power and let them truly participate in the creation of content," he said.

The landscape at the time did not belong to NetEase News. Sina News pioneered the 24-hour news model, using speed and comprehensiveness to deliver the most primitive "sense of information security." However, it had launched a "Let Me Say a Few Words" forum mechanism as early as 2000, which had not yet matured, and Weibo, where discourse was more vigorous, would not peak until 2010. Tencent News had large readership and no glaring flaws, except that its follow-posters, drawn from QQ users, were of uneven caliber. And fellow portal Sohu News played a different game — it excelled at coverage of major sporting events.

None of them valued "follow-posts" the way NetEase did, or worked as hard at them.

NetEase divided its news operations into four levels: besides the chief editor steering the overall direction at the top, there were senior editors, high editors, and junior editors.

A junior editor's KPIs were the easiest to quantify through "follow-posts." Sun Meng, a junior editor who had just joined NetEase News four months earlier, had to keep the follow-post count above 15,000 for every column; if she hit the target, her bonus would be multiplied by 1.5. She was indeed worth going to extra lengths for.

Zeng Jian, who served as NetEase News editor-in-chief in 2006, later summarized that one feature making "NetEase Follow-Posts" stand out was floor-building. Initially, though, the "floor-building" format existed only to solve the problem of commenters being unable to see the context of a discussion. As the product iterated, that "building" stretched from its original 20-plus floors to more than 70 floors, becoming exceptionally lively.

As the rule-maker, NetEase also arranged a hierarchy for its follow-posters. The names of this "hierarchy" look quite amusing: on the one hand they reward ordinary people's voices, but on the other they bluntly display the bureaucrat-centric culture's interpretation of "authority." A junior follow-poster is called a "clerk," followed in turn by section chief, division director, bureau chief, and party secretary — and once a poster's points reach 65,000, they become a "veteran of the Follow-Post Bureau."

Of course, posting points are not determined purely by number of posts; they consist of a series of "daily tasks," "daily achievements," and "extra achievements."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702174056jSA3Tpf7QsxdzRVP.jpg-w600)

NetEase's official team also compiled these influential hot posts into "red booklets," published on Amazon for anyone to download free. A new red booklet came out every two months; on the one hand it helped consolidate NetEase follow-post culture, and on the other it burnished the company's reputation — so much so that *Southern People Weekly*, a magazine known for in-depth reporting, commented: "How high netizens' IQ is — just look at NetEase's floor-building crews."

Most likely, NetEase's floor-building crews in turn influenced NetEase News' editorial approach.

In 2011, Tang Yan proposed a new slogan for NetEase News: "Be Opinionated." According to Tang Yan's explanation on Zhihu, "opinionated" meant "on the one hand solving the problem of identification, and on the other providing as much directional guidance for internal business."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702174057HDaN2SiXnVh3xojJ.jpg-w600)

Thereafter, NetEase News headlines responded to the new slogan with gusto. An article titled "Where Is the Confidence in the Chinese Dream?" was reposted from *PLA Daily* to NetEase News with the headline changed to "PLA Daily: The Doctrine We Believe In Is the Truth of the Universe." The same thing happened with "PLA Daily: Understand, Don't Distort," retitled "PLA Daily: Grasping the Intentions of Superiors Is the Top Priority for Being a Good Cadre." And a news item about swimmer Jiao Liuyang returning home with a gold medal had its emphasis shifted to Moutai liquor: "Jiao Liuyang Gets a Hero's Welcome Back Home: Moutai and Flowers Greet Her."

NetEase has its own yardstick: it believes it can see through users' needs and emotions. Simply put, it's about figuring out where the "excitement points" are.

## The Distance Between NetEase's "Public Intellectual" World and the Real World

People quickly discovered: the satisfying words you can't say in real life can be said on NetEase.

In its 2015 year-end review, NetEase, under the title "We," thanked "the unsilent majority." According to a NetEase employee who wished to remain anonymous, the meaning of his "opinionated" work was not to be novel for novelty's sake, but to respect netizens' voices and dare to speak on their behalf.

More than one voice emanates from NetEase, which is attractive, and NetEase consciously resisted the pressure to delete posts, taking the heat for its users.

But this laissez-faire also created crudeness and chaos, making regional attacks and abusive posts ubiquitous.

"Tourists Overcharged 10,000 Yuan at a Harbin Restaurant: One Iron-Pot Fish Costs Over 5,000" drew 120,000 participants and 17,000 follow-posts, and within those posts, evaluations of the city of Harbin took center stage. "Harbin is just like that — reach out your hand and you can't see your fingers," "Harbin is just... hehe da! The smell of the toilets at Harbin Station represents this city"... And in one of the posts, two netizens whose IP addresses showed them in Shenzhen and Qiqihar respectively began flaming each other.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702174057Cx7vteO2gdHY0Irp.jpg-w600)

Region-based prejudice and attacks have probably always existed. In a map circulating online titled "China's Hometowns of Crime," Yichun in Jiangxi was named the hometown of lock-picking theft, Zhumadian in Henan was full of seduction scams and fake marriages, and so on. NetEase highlighted the importance of region across all topics: in early 2007 it launched a feature that automatically matches geographic information, and it deliberately emphasized city names in headlines. Regional strife was therefore more likely to be inflamed there.

Things certainly did not stop at regional attacks. Under a news item about a murdered female college student, netizens eagerly tagged the story with labels like "slept with a celebrity," "spring chick," and "rotten goods."

When the State Administration of Press, Publication, Radio, Film and Television ordered *The Empress of China* to be sent back for re-editing, even Huang Yu — who had once been unaccustomed to NetEase's atmosphere — felt the thrill of "flaming." "When everyone more or less agrees on something, watching others flame feels great," she said.

But "Drillbit" still disdains keeping company with follow-posters. His reason for only reading and never posting is that he feels he has read books for a few years, while "these people only want quick takes — they don't do in-depth commentary." He considers such talk unworthy of being called "high-level."

True enough. On NetEase, the faster you post, the more likely you are to accumulate the most "likes" and become a hot follow-post, staying at the top of the comments section. This makes the stance of NetEase follow-posts very hard to reverse. Huang Yu says she sometimes reads comments on Weibo too, and finds that if an argument breaks out there, the majority opinion tomorrow may differ from today's — "but NetEase almost never changes," she says.

Seize ground fast, don't expect to argue everything through, but be substantive — this is roughly the advanced playbook summarized by "Dynamic Superman Superfly," whose follow-posts reportedly get "liked" and "sunk" about fifty-fifty. "I think it has to do with how fast you can find something after news is pushed out, with the quality of your take, and with follow-post moderation."

Commenting on a news item about "Lijiang liquor touts," his first reaction was "Holy shit, quite a few people with their brains switched off and their lust switched on." He then put himself in others' shoes and listed out a few keywords, finally stringing them together. Details, theory, feasibility — once all of that checks out, he can turn it into a readable story, a bit playful, very immersive.

It's hard to hear these stories from the mouths of follow-posters in real life.

"Dynamic Superman Superfly" has posted more than 4,700 follow-posts over three years, which makes him a "number-one figure" among follow-posters. But he prefers to keep this identity hidden. He refused to discuss that awe-inspiring "follow-poster" persona with us over the phone, saying: "Apart from access to certain information and the time it takes to get it, follow-posting's effect on my life is negligible."

But at least here, they talk with all their might.

In the follow-posts under the news item "Indian Scholar: Indian English Is the Purest in the World; British and American Accents Are Too Heavy," netizens mimicked Indian-accented pronunciation, playfully coining words like "Aglee," "me doo," and "velly velly absolutely." In the follow-posts under "Man Whose Name Contains the Obscure Character '瓛' Has Never Succeeded in Buying a Train Ticket Online," they contributed obscure characters such as "颽," "轥," "鯶潅," and "臩矃."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702174058MkxKZIrR0yQlcVEo.jpg-w600)

In January 2015, in the Asian Cup quarterfinals, the national soccer team faced Australia. Follow-posts under related news items produced several buildings of 60-plus floors. One of them read: "Last night I observed the stars and cast a divination: our dynasty's cuju team is in grave danger — unless... the second-floor poster admits he's a fucking idiot, which will surely turn misfortune into blessing and carry us into the semifinals."

Before long, the post received a reply. The dialog box marked with a 2 in the top-right corner — also called "second floor" — wrote: "For the boys of the national team, I'll do it. Listen closely: I! AM! A! FUCKING! IDIOT!"

## So, What Changed the Public Intellectuals

In *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*, Jürgen Habermas proposed that the commercial operation of media would lead to a structural transformation of the public sphere. The "critique of the reading public" would gradually give way to consumers "exchanging tastes and preferences," thereby turning the "culture-debating public" into a "culture-consuming public."

That is exactly how it is.

Pi Tuo, who once worked as a reporter at NetEase, says he actually knew what kinds of posts could go viral. "Sensational topics like GMOs, traditional Chinese medicine, the government, the Cultural Revolution, regional stuff — every one of them hits." One week, his column failed to hit its required follow-post numbers, so on the last day he ran an LGBT article, which neatly saved his KPIs.

This is, first of all, an advertising-supported news portal, where high click counts and reply numbers are never a bad thing. But day after day, people more or less forgot why they came here in the first place.

"Qin Shou," who does physics research, often reposts the best content to the Douban group "I Just Love Reading NetEase Comments 2.0." Follow-posts once brought outside information — and rare excitement — to his relatively monotonous life. But gradually, "Qin Shou" came to "only read the headline, then the follow-posts; I barely read the body text anymore." He admits he still reads NetEase out of inertia, or just for fun.

"Follow Your Heart" says he has felt himself growing numb on NetEase: "I used to reply to every insult. When I saw someone cursing me, I'd get angry and curse back. Now I'm used to it. I still reply, but I don't get that angry anymore."

"Peachy Wang," for his part, feels that only "flaming" makes him feel involved — otherwise things get distant. Under an abstruse tech news item, he remembers one follow-post reading: "How am I supposed to flame this one? Urgently waiting here." The only thing he keeps his distance from is regional attacks; as a Henan native, many times it felt like personal attacks to him.

And those old NetEase memes — "Regional Dog," "Japan-America Cat," "50 Cents," "Little Li" referring to servile government functionaries — have also become a form of NetEase self-mockery in cultural terms. The former became "badge" tags that could be attached to others when replying; the latter is the graphic on the follow-post 404 page. The "Second-Floor Law" also became part of NetEase's self-marketing, since the "since time immemorial, the second floor produces XX" meme had been accepted by most people.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702174058IospFUYRBGacbV7d.jpg-w600)

"The quality of NetEase follow-posts has really nosedived these past two years," says "Little Bunny's Uncle," who first came into contact with NetEase in 2003. As the portals' stature declined and more competitors emerged, NetEase indirectly stepped up post deletion and thread closure, so as not to affect the platform's stability. The follow-posts under a piece titled "The Biggest 'Map Cannon': Northerners Don't Fear the Cold" were shut down for this reason; NetEase's explanation was — to prevent "water-meter checks," and to crack down on regional attacks, personal attacks, and spam ads.

It wasn't only NetEase that changed.

Russell Jacoby's standard for intellectuals in *The Last Intellectuals* — "professional competence, a global vision, a sense of social responsibility, action to transform society, and the courage to serve as a guide for society" — is also being changed.

What they still hold onto is a superficial — and possibly important — one: go to the most bustling place to air your views. But they also deeply understand what Habermas said: "cultural critique" is being replaced by "cultural consumption."

"Yes, I'm a public intellectual. I am consuming politics, I am consuming current affairs, I am consuming hot topics," Han Han wrote in his blog post "I'm Going to Be a Damn Public Intellectual."

Zhihu, a site that initially hoped professionals would answer questions, is also expanding the boundary of "knowledge." Pop-psychology and relationship Q&A have become popular there, often appearing in the form of finely crafted stories and quips, rapidly cultivating opinion leaders in these fields and making the platform popular with a more mass audience.

NetEase is like their microcosm: it encourages people to speak up early, ideally catching others off guard and landing with a bang. As Habermas put it, through this kind of "cultural interactivity," public intellectuals make their rapid appearance atop the "high-rise buildings" raised together by the era and the platform.

## A Dictionary of NetEase Follow-Post Customs and Culture

**1. Follow-post:**

Specifically refers to NetEase's comments, as distinct from the generic online "comment." Its stages of development: December 3, 2003 — NetEase follow-posts are born. February 24, 2005 — NetEase's first building is erected. January 20, 2007 — follow-posts begin attaching IP address identification. December 30, 2008 — the "No Follow-Posts, No News" special goes online.

**2. The Second-Floor Law:**

"Since time immemorial, the second floor produces idiots." Some netizens eager to reply on the second floor either just climb up the thread mindlessly or hurriedly leave a message without understanding what's going on, producing stupid or ridiculous statements that often get them ridiculed. This is the elementary form of the "Second-Floor Law."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021740595oTpDgLG7X2IbnYR.jpg-w600)

**3. Map cannon:**

The map cannon originally referred to a map-wide attack weapon in the *Super Robot Wars* series, and was later used as a general term for weapons or magic of mass destruction. Online, "map cannon" has acquired the extended meaning of verbally attacking an entire group; on the internet it usually refers to regional attackers, or the act of negating a whole group based on the behavior of a few. It typically means comprehensively condemning all members of a group because people from a certain place said certain things.

**4. Mouth cannon:**

Refers to people who often make statements they themselves cannot back up. In plain terms: a braggart. In ACG (anime, comics, games) culture, it refers to protagonists who fully exploit their glib tongues, talking enemies around with heartfelt persuasion and ear-splitting shouting to inflict mental damage — a commonly used "skill."

**5. High-level black:**

Refers to fairly veiled content that subtly mocks or ridicules certain people or events. Under NetEase news items, netizens often use high-level black techniques, in single follow-posts or floor-building form, to express their discontent.

**6. Martian netizens:**

While compiling the IP address database, NetEase came across IP segments with no geographic location, or that showed only "ICCAN unallocated"; NetEase collectively calls these "NetEase Martian netizens." Because they cannot be identified and netizens actually use few such IPs (not counting wrongly matched addresses), Martian netizens appear extremely rarely, and cause a stir and admiration whenever they do.

**7. This Old Monk / This Poor Nun / Little Li / the Bureau Chief:**

"This Old Monk" and "This Poor Nun" represent disadvantaged groups, who often grumble about social injustice in a playful, humorous, sharp style. Little Li represents a low-level government clerk, whose attitude is always fawning and submissive. The Bureau Chief represents officials, and his manner is self-important to the point of arrogance.

## **What Hot Follow-Posts Has NetEase Produced Over the Past Year?**

**"Great, I support it, awesome, there's hope now":**

Follow-posts like this often appear under current-affairs news. On NetEase, they mostly mean the exact opposite of their literal meaning.

**"Open the door, there's a delivery":**

In the old days, to get a criminal to open the door during an arrest, the police would usually make up an excuse. It started with claiming to be checking the water meter, and gradually spawned various versions, like "open up — delivery, checking the electric meter." In NetEase follow-posts, once someone posts an overly incendiary remark, someone will reply: "Open the door, SF Express delivery." It means you know too much — a kind of tease.

**"Hello, one-one-zero? The scene is about to get out of control":**

A common reply from follow-posters to defuse the mood when a discussion gets heated.

**"I owe Qvod a membership":**

Under a NetEase news item last January about the pornography trial of Qvod, follow-posting netizens all sided with Wang Xin. In the follow-posts, netizens played the emotion card by telling their personal stories with Qvod, reminiscing about it. Slogans produced at the time included "They arrested the man with the most guts in China" and "Qvod may fall, but diaosi don't cry — Caoliu remains."

## **What Different Identities Do Follow-Posters Have?**

Follow-post masters can be divided into "Poets," the "Talented and Incisive Party," the "Alter Ego Party," "Brother Persistence," the "Li Jufu Party," and more.

**"Poets":**

Refers to people who write poems in follow-posts. The famous poet "Li Bubai y," for instance, wrote under a recent news item "High School Student Lin Jiawen, Who Published a Historical Monograph, Dies of Depression": "Here stands empty the Huangyi Tower" (a play on the line "Here only the Yellow Crane Tower remains"). Those good at rewriting lyrics include "Mingqian Maofeng." There are also poet collectives with organizations (even offline), who often team up to build floors together.

**"The Talented and Incisive Party":**

They post short commentaries and riffs from angles different from the ordinary. For example, the netizen with the ID "Follow Your Heart," under a social news item about "a younger sister caring for her bedridden older brother for years," took this angle: it's because the two-child policy was relaxed. His explanation: only after the two-child policy was relaxed does "a younger sister caring for her older brother" comply with family-planning policy and become worth discussing.

**"The Alter Ego Party":**

They often disguise themselves as specific people from the news and jump into follow-posts. Take "Quick-Pisser Han Chong": in his follow-posts you'll find that "this man has worked as a waiter, been a big boss, studied in the West one moment and gone hunting in Africa the next" — every post reads like personal experience, and strangers would believe him at any moment.

**"Brother Persistence":**

Posts the same follow-post every day. Or posts every day under the same news item.

**"The Li Jufu Party":**

For a news event, they analyze each point one by one, then draw a conclusion — stubbornly insisting on their own view. (Li Jufu is a pun on "reasonable and well-evidenced.")

(Drillbit, Sun Meng, and Pi Tuo are pseudonyms; all others are real names and NetEase follow-post or Douban IDs.)
