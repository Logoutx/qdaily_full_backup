"A Brief History of Everything": Was the English Revolution Not a Bourgeois Revolution?
@@QD_EXCERPT@@

@@QD_BODY@@
Goldstone argues that in analyzing the long-term social causes of state breakdown in the English Revolution, demographic change—not the various consequences of capitalist development—must be placed at the center.

The conventional explanation of the English Revolution of the 1640s holds that it was a conflict between groups who benefited from the rise of capitalism, on one side, and a conservative king and the old nobility, on the other. In other words, the English Revolution was a bourgeois revolution.

Who exactly made up the bourgeoisie? According to the traditional account, the bourgeoisie consisted of agrarian capitalists among the landowning class, together with commercial capitalists drawn from overseas trading companies. The bourgeoisie deeply resented the restrictions that the feudal monarchy and aristocratic politics of the time imposed on their production and business activities, and the conflict between the rising bourgeoisie and the conservative feudal classes was precisely what caused the revolution.

The American scholar Jack Goldstone, however, argues that the English Revolution was rather an early modern crisis—a composite of a series of crises triggered chiefly by population growth—whose character was much like the upheavals that brought about the fall of the Ming and Qing in China.

At first glance this claim seems somewhat absurd, but as Goldstone works through the argument it turns out to be quite self-consistent. First, Goldstone demonstrates that the English king and many nobles were themselves capitalists of a sort. Studies of the management of royal lands show that the English crown did not oppose enclosure: by 1630 the king was probably England's largest enclosing landlord. Mounting fiscal pressure led the Stuart monarchy to take the lead in enclosure, turning wasteland into arable land, often in cooperation with local nobles. In northern England, after 1600 the king led the way in raising rents and entry fines, adjusting them to the highest market rents in the region. If enclosure was meant to raise rents, and if profit is a marker of commercial development, then judging by the king's behavior it is nearly impossible to portray the seventeenth-century English crisis as a conflict between a growing class of commercial nobles and a conservative, commerce-hating king.

Moreover, down to 1640 most elites—regardless of how recently they had obtained their titles—engaged in various commercial activities, because a century and a half of inflation meant that assets could easily shrink if they were not invested wisely, and landlords who had not commercialized their land rents saw their fortunes dwindle to nearly nothing.

Research on the loyalties of merchants in the English Revolution found that many "new capitalists" were not the king's enemies but his close allies. England's overseas trade was controlled by monopoly companies licensed by the crown. Those who profited from these monopoly trades generally supported the king, while it was the domestic traders who sided with Parliament. The king's supporters also included the "new families," who benefited from offices bestowed by the king, invested in land purchased from him for commercial gain, and joined the king in local projects such as marsh drainage and forest improvement.

Although the king later fell out with the citizens and gentry of London, from the mid-sixteenth century to the 1620s the royal government and the commercialized capital city supported each other, and both benefited from each other's growth. In the early Stuart period a close partnership developed between the king and the capital's commercialized oligarchy: the king relied on London's mayor and aldermen to raise loans and distribute royal bonds, and in return these oligarchs received tax concessions, royal monopolies, and trading contracts.

What shook this mutual-support system was the thorny fiscal predicament the English crown faced in the 1620s and 1630s. Charles I needed money, and everyone could see the flimsiness of the pretexts he came up with. Many monopolies were revoked or declared expired, and to obtain them again one had to offer loans or buy them in cash. This undoubtedly infuriated the commercial capitalists who had previously been happy to extend him credit and financing.

Further corroboration comes from the fact that many variables closely associated with the English Revolution—prices, social mobility and social competition, the potential for social mobilization—showed clearly opposite trends after the revolution. Before the English Revolution, prices kept rising, social mobility and the transfer of land were widespread, the urban population grew rapidly, and real wages steadily fell. But after 1660, prices gradually declined, the number of people entering the ranks of the nobility and the gentry shrank rapidly, urban population growth slowed, and real wages gradually rose. After the revolution, England's overseas trade—especially with Asia and the Americas—expanded dramatically, the pace of enclosure accelerated, and agricultural commercialization continued to advance. These trends are usually taken as proof that the English Revolution was a victory for capitalism. Yet even as economic development quickened, population growth was very slow, prices were relatively stable, the pace of urbanization declined, and social mobility in fact stagnated.

Goldstone therefore argues that in analyzing the long-term social causes of state breakdown in the English Revolution, demographic change—not the various consequences of capitalist development—must be placed at the center. In other words, what was closely tied to the socially and economically significant trends of early modern England was not the development of capitalism, but demographic change.

Between 1500 and 1650, England's total population more than doubled, growing from just over 2 million to more than 5 million. Population growth strained resources, including food, and produced chronic inflation, which in turn triggered a series of social crises, including the crown's fiscal crisis.

In the institutional system of early modern England, the key institutions that maintained social stability were the tax system that sustained the king's rule, the hierarchical system that regulated local authority and family relations, and the land and labor markets that provided employment and income. If these institutions failed simultaneously, society would be in serious trouble, facing the danger of disintegration. And that is precisely the situation that arose before the English Revolution. Because population grew faster than the economy, the land and labor markets could not maintain a stable equilibrium, and the result was rising prices—a situation already evident in the early sixteenth century. Burdened by heavy social-ecological pressures since the 1620s, many of England's institutions had become paralyzed.

What followed were state fiscal distress, elite mobility and competition, and social discontent. Wages had fallen to new lows, leaving workers and the middle and lower-income classes deeply anxious. Competition for professional positions not only left a generation of educated men with almost no hope of entering royal government or the church, but also drove the new elites of the various counties into fierce competition over precedence. The king sought new sources of revenue that would escape parliamentary control, thereby intensifying the conflict between the two.

Though no one wanted revolution, no one wanted to preserve the social conditions of the early seventeenth century either: to sustain itself, the king had to bypass Parliament and find all manner of ways to raise money; and as the number of elites kept growing—all of them scrambling for a place within an extremely unstable structure of social status and networks of interest—tensions steadily mounted among the many small cliques at court and in the counties. Poverty, unemployment, vagrancy, and crime worsened by the day.

Solving these problems required close cooperation between the king and the elites to reform the fiscal system, the system of elite recruitment, and the system of economic organization. But under the existing resource constraints, no solution existed from which all sides could benefit. When prices kept rising, royal lands were nearly sold off, and the elites refused to cooperate, how was Charles I to raise revenue? When the elites were so numerous yet so divided—granting royal favor to one elite family would alienate two others—how was Charles I to win their cooperation? And in those desperate times, if the elites did not unite sincerely behind the king's authority, how was Charles I to contain social unrest?

Social crises arise not because people wish for crisis, but because long-term social changes have made the existing political order untenable. After that, conflict and politics emerge around questions of who is to blame for the deterioration and how to clean up the mess.

At this point, a long-standing historical controversy seems to have formed a self-consistent explanatory closed loop. But Goldstone, not content to stop there, raises another question. If the Eurasian dynasties of the early modern world—England and France, the Ottomans, and the Chinese empire—all underwent similar state breakdowns triggered by demographic crisis rather than bourgeois revolution, then why did the Great Divergence still occur between East and West? Why did the East fail to embark on the capitalist path as the West had?

To this classic question, Goldstone—who has just explained the English Revolution with demographic determinism—turns to cultural determinism. He argues that cultural structures determine how people respond to state crises and shape state reconstruction. The different ideological legacies rooted in the process of state reconstruction after the seventeenth-century crisis had a profound influence on the later divergence in development between Eastern and Western states.

Revolutions that did not fully triumph over tradition but nonetheless went through a period of rejecting tradition and ideological innovation—such as the English Revolution and the French Revolution—left behind a rich and vital legacy for the societies that emerged from state breakdown. Although the Puritans and the Jacobins gradually declined after their revolutions, their ideas retained a place within traditional symbols, institutions, and thought. Reconstruction in these countries was therefore not a simple restoration: the demands that had been made to limit absolute power posed a continuing challenge to the restored regimes, and the compromise between the two consolidated parliamentarism, opening up institutional space for the later development of capitalism, while the growth of capitalist industry and commerce served as a stabilizer when England and France later faced renewed pressure from population growth.

By contrast, in countries where traditional forces were continually reinforced in the course of state breakdown—Ottoman Turkey and the Chinese empire, for example—the ideological response was an attempt to purify and reaffirm traditional institutions. In these countries, the crisis was blamed as a deviation from orthodoxy, and the new regimes strove to eliminate the diversity of existing cultural structures and purge elements deemed heretical. State reconstruction and the rebuilding of social institutions brought about a revival of traditional thought, but the price people paid was the weakening of the wellsprings of future social dynamism and the foundation for fundamental change. And so, when these countries later encountered crises driven by demographic pressure, the same drama played out again and again.

History is always "renovated" by new interpretations; this does not necessarily lead to historical nihilism, but rather allows people to become more deeply aware that both history and reality are forever trying to break free of simple, once-and-for-all interpretations, thereby continually renewing and enriching human understanding.
