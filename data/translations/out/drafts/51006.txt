In this paradigm of nationalism, new political elites created nationalism through deliberate policy.

Anything that has an "-ism" attached to it is bound to make people talk past one another, and nationalism is no exception.

At one end of the spectrum are the constructivists, who hold that nations and states are "constructed" by political forces and acts of imagination, rather than grown like living organisms. The constructivist camp is not itself unified, and splits basically into three schools.

One school is led by the anthropologist and philosopher Ernest Gellner, who believed nationalism is intimately bound up with urbanization and industrialization. Gellner pointed out that in Europe during the 19th and early 20th centuries, and in Asia and Africa after the 1930s, it was the people gathered in the new urban centers who were most likely to describe themselves as unified nations and to demand statehood. It was, for example, the jostling of Hungarians, Serbs, and Italians in 19th-century Vienna—their competition for resources and jobs—that hardened the distinctions between these "races" and drove their demands for national autonomy.

Gellner's theory is, in effect, a modernization theory of nationalism. In its view, nationalism traveled with the march of modernization from the West to the East, and then to the South, finally reaching Africa in the 20th century—the "last continent," ultimately permeated by capitalism and urbanization.

But Gellner's theory has a fairly obvious bug: many examples show vigorous nationalist movements arising in societies with very low levels of industrialization. For explaining these cases, Hobsbawm's account is more compelling. Hobsbawm held that nationalism is a product of the state itself, the pure result of the workings of power. In other words, nationalism came after the state, not the other way around. Latin American nationalism, for instance, took shape after the independent states were founded in the 1820s and 1830s, not before. And Belgian nationalism was fostered by the government after the Kingdom of Belgium emerged from a patchwork of linguistically mixed regions in northern Europe.

In this paradigm of nationalism, new political elites created nationalism through deliberate policy. The state promoted mass education, defined citizenship and its taxation, and in the process created "the Other"—excluding and imprisoning some of its people. The urgency of manufacturing nationalism was reinforced by the needs of capitalism, the rise of socialism, working-class radicalism, and the fear of unrest. Governments in this period began conducting regular censuses and used passport systems to control migration. All of these policies, wittingly or unwittingly, served to heighten the sense of nationalism, forcing people to choose a nation-state.

But this theory raises a new problem: in regions that had developed neither capitalism and industrial urbanism nor strong states, people nonetheless felt a powerful nationalism. How is that to be explained? Enter Benedict Anderson's anthropologically inflected account of the origins of nationalism. It emphasizes the role of imagination and shared feeling—rather than shifts in power and resources—in the invention of nationalism. The nation is an "imagined community" created by "print capitalism": books and newspapers spread across the world, engraving a sense of belonging in the minds of elites and, later, of ordinary people who read those books and papers.

Highly explanatory. Yet in the predominantly agrarian societies of Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa, nationalist movements mostly emerged before the spread of modern industrial urbanization and print capitalism. In those societies, urbanization rarely exceeded 10 percent before the end of the 19th century, and male literacy may have run to only a few percentage points. In fact, even in European societies, print capitalism's push on nationalism may have been weaker than Anderson imagined: records show that in 1848, a year of high revolutionary fervor in Europe, the leading Romanian nationalist newspaper had a circulation of just 250.

Perhaps this is the fate of all -isms: no theory can cover every fact, let alone one anchored at a single end of the spectrum. In some situations, the "old-style patriotism" kind of nationalism at the other end of the spectrum also has explanatory power. Late-19th-century thinkers and "patriots" believed nationalism had existed from early on in societies relatively homogeneous in language and religion, and that modern nation-states were therefore mostly born of earlier societies that shared a language and culture. Most cultural nationalists still hold this view today.

For the latecomers to the ranks of nation-building, this "patriotic nationalism" is also quite explanatory. Efforts to construct nationalism and nation-states met with both success and failure. Ruling groups in parts of Asia and Africa were able to rally a more vigorous nationalism in the late 19th century because it was rooted in a long-standing sense of shared purpose, reflected in a common language and old regional ties, and often reinforced by relatively long-lived centralized states and traditions of effective government.

Moreover, late-19th-century nationalism was not merely a top-down phenomenon, nor solely the product of states or their elites. People from poor and lower-class groups sought borders they could regard as those of their own country. Sometimes migrants from beyond the state's borders played an important role in stirring the aspiration to unify the national territory: Irish migrants to the United States or Australia, and Chinese migrants to Hawaii and Southeast Asia, each played a significant role in the emergence of Irish and Chinese nationalism, respectively.

In the middle of the spectrum lies the war-centered theory of nationalism's origins. Perhaps because it can have things both ways, this "war nationalism" seems able to bridge the old-style patriotic nationalism and the versions centered on urbanization, the state, and print capitalism, connecting European and non-European nationalisms from the angle of war.

This view holds that nationalism was the product of the wars, rebellions, and international conflicts of the 19th century. In that process, the state, urbanization, and print capitalism all played certain roles, but war was the origin of nationalism—although, later, nationalism increasingly became a source of war in its own right.

For those who hold the war-centered theory of nationalism's origins, the two interconnected world crises of 1780–1815 and 1848–1865 greatly propelled the initial formation of national identities.

Take the first world crisis first. British identity, for instance, was forged in the long wars against France, especially during the world crisis of 1780–1820. Napoleon's campaigns across Europe spurred the formation of German, Italian, and Russian national identities. Likewise, the French and Russian invasions made the Ottomans, Egyptians, and other North Africans keenly aware of their own weakness and determined to reorganize their societies.

Next, the second crisis of 1848–1865 further midwifed nation-building around the globe. Rapid social change and the refounding of the Union in the United States produced a more robust American national feeling. Before 1860, the United States had always had an ideal of patriotic devotion, but it was the Civil War, together with the country's growing engagement in international affairs afterward, that cultivated a stronger American nationalism. In Europe, the emergence of two new nation-states, Germany and Italy, along with the humiliation of two others, France and Austria, broke the continental balance of diplomatic and military competition within Europe's borders and fueled a new wave of national sentiment.

The mid-19th-century wars on the Eurasian landmass convinced the late-Tokugawa elites of Japan that the country had to strengthen itself. In India, the Great Rebellion of 1857 and renewed British incursions forced merchants and professionals in the coastal regions to rethink their place in the empire. Then Japan's unification, Britain's aggressive rule in South Asia, and Europe's push into the Middle East generated new nationalist movements among elites and merchants in Asia and Africa—which in turn produced new, openly articulated demands for autonomy directed at the colonial governments.

This global, indigenous nationalist backlash against colonialism, together with the intra-European contest of nationalisms, caused the pace of European empire-building to accelerate in the late 19th century—the new phase of imperial expansion coincided with the full emergence of nation-states in Europe, the Americas, and Japan, and with the rise of nationalist movements beyond Europe.

Then imperialism and nationalism entered a new round of interaction, and nationalism began a new relay race. In Eastern Europe, after the Balkan wars of 1878, pan-Slavism—encouraged by a newly self-confident Russia—began carving the European parts of the Ottoman Empire into small, combative Christian states. When Britain occupied Egypt in 1882, it faced opposition from a coalition of army officers, Muslim clerics, and landowners—foreshadowing a new national identity in a southern region formerly under Ottoman rule. In Asia, the founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885, and the sharp criticism that coastal and overseas Chinese leveled at the Manchu-Qing government in the 1890s, both expressed the new Asian elites' desire to build their own budding nation-states.

Of course, for those who hold the war-centered theory, the heightened and more widespread sense of nation was not only the result of surging global warfare; it also reflected new opportunities for interregional exchange and ideological adaptation. In the 1820s, the Indian reformer Roy could read commentaries on the post-Napoleonic European revolutions in English-language newspapers in Calcutta and write essays on national self-determination. Before 1914, Ho Chi Minh read treatises on the ideas of Thomas Jefferson, a founding father of the United States, in his French textbooks.

Moreover, ideas and models of nationalism and nation-building did not flow only from the West to other regions: by the 1880s, Japan's own hybrid modernity had already become a powerful example for other Asian and African nationalists to emulate.
