F1: A Sport's Political Correctness, and Its Two Enemies

One enemy is environmentalism; the other, it must be said, is itself.
@@QD_BODY@@
One enemy is environmentalism; the other, it must be said, is itself.

April 9 was race day for the F1 Shanghai Grand Prix. From morning onward, crowds began gathering outside the Shanghai International Circuit, and by around eleven they had finally formed a long line outside the security checkpoint. It took at least another half hour of waiting to actually get into the venue.

Before the race, 12 of the 14 price tiers for ordinary tickets on the official F1 ticketing website had already sold out. On race day, the entire main grandstand was nearly full — even the platinum section, the most expensive at 2,760 yuan, appeared to be more than ninety percent occupied.

The scene at the track looked lively enough, but one fact that cannot be ignored is that F1's influence is no longer what it once was.

![The main grandstand during qualifying on April 8](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170412020738QRNtMSOl5xyZV7du.jpeg-w600)

The figures released after the race by the organizer — Jiushi Group, which also operates the Shanghai International Circuit — proved the point. Over the three days of the F1 Chinese Grand Prix, the venue received a total of 145,000 spectators. Although that was 5,000 more than in 2016, it clearly cannot compare with the 250,000 the event drew when F1 had only just arrived in China.

Zhang Kai has followed F1 for many years, and this was her third consecutive year attending the race in person. While she felt the overall atmosphere seemed much the same, she said: "Looking at the different fan camps, 'Shuailai' (the fan nickname for Ferrari driver Kimi Räikkönen) still had quite a lot of people, but they no longer had the organized banner-spelling activities they used to. Maybe it was also because the weather wasn't great — the momentum just didn't feel that strong."

"The fans I met at the track had all been following it for more than ten years. It feels like very few newcomers have taken an interest in the sport all these years," Zhang Kai said. "F1 as a whole, in China, probably isn't doing as well as before."

China is not the only place experiencing F1's ebb; for the sport, this is a global problem.

[According to data released by F1 itself](http://www.f1fanatic.co.uk/2016/04/20/f1-has-lost-one-third-of-its-tv-audience-since-2008/), the sport still had 600 million television viewers worldwide in 2008, but by 2015 that number had slipped to around 400 million. And [in Britain, where the first F1 race was held](https://f1broadcasting.co/2016/12/15/formula-1s-uk-viewing-figures-drop-significantly-year-on-year/), F1 lost 1.11 million viewers in 2016 alone — a decline of 29.7%.

Although F1's total revenue reached a staggering $1.77 billion in 2015, enough to make it the world's third-largest sporting event after the World Cup and the Olympics, the crisis was staring it plainly in the face — one that [former F1 supremo Bernie Ecclestone could not help but acknowledge](http://sports.qq.com/a/20151020/000905.htm).

![Red Bull driver Max Verstappen, who finished third in Shanghai](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704120208473ZPn4N7uTgzmbQvB.jpg-w600)

This may be the first time the sport, founded in 1950, has run into trouble this big. Over its nearly seventy-year history, F1 had enjoyed smooth sailing, only beginning its slow decline over the past ten years or so. That trajectory seems to mirror the history of the automobile industry over the past hundred-plus years — and that is no mere coincidence.

The world's first car was born in 1886. After two years of experimentation, the German Karl Benz successfully mounted a gasoline engine on a three-wheeled carriage and used it to power the vehicle. Soon, more and more manufacturers began producing cars, including the German company Daimler and the French company Peugeot.

As the auto industry grew, motor racing came along with it. In 1894, the first motor race recognized by historians was held in France, running from Paris to the small town of Rouen, a total distance of 126 kilometers. At the time, however, the organizer, Pierre Giffard, did not treat it as a contest of speed but as a test of the reliability of the cars then being produced.

As automotive performance improved, racing gradually shifted its focus to speed. In 1901, Henry Ford, who had founded the Ford Motor Company, drove a race car of his own design around challenging all comers, hoping to build the Ford brand's fame through victory and thereby sell more cars.

In the 1920s and 1930s, the car completed its spread through the middle class. This owed much to Henry Ford, who designed the Model T and the assembly-line mode of production, drastically lowering the price of cars. General Motors then established what the auto industry looks like today, along with the public's understanding of the car, by pursuing a multi-line policy that produced a range of models at different market positions.

![Henry Ford](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170412021132ESYNoXR5Le2pHgrI.jpeg-w600)

Then the world entered the Second World War. Although the war forced many automobile factories to shut down and the entire industry nearly ground to a halt, military needs gave birth to a host of new technologies, including automatic transmissions, hydraulic brakes, and aerodynamic design. After the war, as car factories resumed production and applied these new technologies to their vehicles, the global auto industry exploded into full bloom.

The 1960s were an era when automakers around the world frantically chased performance. The Big Four — General Motors, Ford, Chrysler, and American Motors (AMC) — all launched their own horsepower-focused "muscle cars." The 1962 Dodge Dart had an engine displacement of 6.8 liters and ran the quarter mile in just 13 seconds. In 1964, the Ford Thunderbolt used a 7.0-liter engine, and Chrysler followed suit with its own competing 7.0-liter Hemi engine.

By early 1970, the horsepower race had peaked. The 1970 Chevrolet Chevelle SS 454 was even fitted with a 7.2-liter V8 producing up to 450 horsepower.

It was in this atmosphere that F1 established its standing as a sport: the pursuit of speed became its selling point, and it entered the realm of competitive athletics.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170412021428PBfWHNRxzSqAvGhj.jpg-w600)

In 1950, the first official F1 race was held at Silverstone in Britain. During that decade, the teams competing in F1 were mostly works teams from automakers, including Alfa Romeo, Ferrari, Maserati, Mercedes-Benz, and others. Their goal was identical to Ford's back in the day: by applying their own newly developed technologies on the track and winning, they could tell the world that they were the best carmakers around.

From the late 1950s onward, F1's history has been marked by one technological advance after another. In 1958, the Cooper-Climax became the first car to adopt a rear-engine layout — a technical revolution in F1 history that was fully universalized two years later.

Subsequently, Team Lotus made a breakthrough in aerodynamics: a race car needed to generate more downforce through its body design in order to perform more stably on the track. This theory quickly became the guiding doctrine of F1, and to this day aerodynamic design remains one of the most important criteria for judging an F1 car.

In 1967, Team Lotus broke through again, this time in engine design. It was the first of all the F1 teams to use a four-valve, dual-overhead-cam engine, a design that went on to dominate the entire 1970s.

As technology kept improving, F1 cars grew ever faster. It was in this process that F1 acquired a meaning beyond what it had before. For the auto industry as a whole, F1 represented the most advanced technology in the field — the ultimate expression of the car's evolution in performance. For ordinary spectators, F1 stretched the human imagination of speed; the roar of the engines sounded like a bugle call to charge, one that never knew defeat.

Zhang Kai feels this keenly. This time she brought a friend along to the circuit. Amid the roar of the engines, the cars flashed past and vanished into the corners. During overtakes, the entire grandstand shouted and cheered. The atmosphere at the track won over this friend, who had never known anything about F1 before. "After we got back, she told me to make sure to call her for the next race," Zhang Kai said.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704120215461H7w6TFMcJnWjAvS.jpg-w600)

After the breakneck growth of the 1970s, the wind in the auto industry shifted. In 1978, the United States passed the fuel tax act, requiring automakers to keep their cars' fuel consumption to around 13 liters per 100 kilometers. This was considered a heavy blow to American carmakers, who had traditionally favored big-displacement engines.

But even without the American law, the first oil crisis, which erupted in 1973, drove the price of oil from $3 a barrel to $13. Soaring fuel prices also pushed automakers to pay ever more attention to energy efficiency. They began to realize that the era of blindly chasing big displacement was over.

On top of that, the global auto industry had long gone without a breakthrough, revolutionary technological innovation. Gasoline-combustion engine technology had almost hit a ceiling.

One important measure of an engine's quality is thermal efficiency — the proportion of chemical energy converted into mechanical energy through the combustion of gasoline. The highest-thermal-efficiency mass-production gasoline engine today is Toyota's hybrid 2ZR-FXE, which claims a peak thermal efficiency of 41%. According to Toyota's figures, thermal efficiency in gasoline engines has risen from 20% in the 1960s to roughly 39% today.

In the broader consumer market, demand for power has largely been satisfied. Even a cheap family car costing just tens of thousands of yuan has at least around 100 horsepower — enough to reach the 120 km/h speed limit, and a single tank of gas covers at least 500 kilometers. Spending more money to improve engine efficiency no longer seems worth it.

When the auto industry lost its imagination, F1 — which reflects the state of the car business — also began to slow its technological progress. Car design shifted its focus ever more toward aerodynamics, giving rise to designs like the high nose cone and the X-wing.

And just as rising oil prices forced the auto industry to rethink big-displacement cars, a single accident changed F1. In 1994, F1's legendary driver Ayrton Senna was killed in a crash at the San Marino Grand Prix. After that, F1 elevated safety to an unprecedented level and introduced a series of rules to slow the cars down, including reducing engine power, narrowing the cars, and switching to grooved tires.

Speed was no longer F1's sole pursuit; at times it was even sacrificed for other considerations. Reasonable as these changes may have been, they meant F1 could no longer serve as the auto industry's emblem of imagination.

![In the Red Bull pit, ExxonMobil staff explain fuel issues to a driver](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704120212311aIfErZ8gQCqAyHY.jpg-w600)

The final blow to F1 came from environmental concerns.

Ten years ago, when she was still in high school, Pang Qi was already a devoted F1 fan. Her school required students to return to campus on Sunday evenings, which was hardly friendly to F1 lovers, but even so she and her friends organized an F1 club.

But in the past couple of years, she has quit the fandom. The reason, Pang Qi feels, is that the overall rule changes in F1 these past two years and the emphasis on safety have made the racing less and less fun to watch.

Older fans may still remember the 2004 French Grand Prix. Under the rules then, when cars made mid-race pit stops, they refueled and changed tires at the same time. Making three pit stops during the race was considered the most classic tactical arrangement at the French Grand Prix.

At the time, Michael Schumacher, the reigning master of the sport, was at his peak — but unfortunately he failed to take pole position in that race and had to start second, behind Fernando Alonso. Since Schumacher's Ferrari team believed Alonso's defense was tight and that overtaking him on the track would be no easy task, they decided to get past him by adjusting their pit-stop strategy.

In the end, Schumacher chose a four-stop strategy. Although each stop cost some time, the extra stop kept his car running with a light fuel load throughout the race. Moreover, because not all the tires he switched to were brand new, they happened to be in their optimal working condition, allowing Schumacher to complete laps faster on the track. Lap by lap, the gains accumulated, and after his fourth and final stop, Schumacher successfully overtook Alonso and locked up the race win.

Today, you will never see a scene like that again. F1's governing body introduced new regulations for the 2010 season banning mid-race refueling, meaning all cars must carry enough fuel for the entire race before the start. The tactical option of adjusting pit-stop timing and count through fuel load was eliminated.

There have been many rule changes of this kind. The V10 engine originally in use was replaced by a V8 in 2006, which in turn gave way to a V6 in 2014. The numbers refer to the cylinders in the engine; fewer cylinders meant lower output, slower speeds, and a roar as the cars flashed past that was no longer as stirring as before.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170412021636DBq9wG2WLtTP3lyN.jpg-w600)

What drove F1 to make these rule changes was precisely environmental considerations.

The problem first surfaced in the early twenty-first century. Statistics from the FIA at the time showed that an ordinary civilian car consumed about 9 to 11 liters of fuel per 100 kilometers in urban driving conditions, while an F1 car burned through more than 70 liters over the same racing distance. Although the fuel consumed in each race is negligible compared with the energy consumed worldwide every year, it still drew protests from environmentalists.

Even a veteran fan like Pang Qi has her complaints: "The cars burn way too much fuel. It's not environmentally friendly."

Under pressure, on December 9, 2006, the FIA released the framework for F1's technical rules for the next five years. The reform focused on emphasizing energy reuse and environmental protection in the cars. Cutting fuel consumption and improving energy efficiency became F1's guiding direction — and that is how rule changes like reducing engine cylinder counts came about.

Rather than pressure on F1 alone, this is better understood as pressure on the entire auto industry. As a genuine demand, environmental protection is changing legislation governing the car business in a number of countries. The European Union, for example, sets limits on automakers' average CO2 emissions per kilometer, pushing them to pursue energy efficiency rather than speed.

These environmental demands even gave birth to a racing series called Formula E (FE), also sanctioned by the FIA like F1. Major manufacturers such as Jaguar, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, and Ferrari have either announced their participation in FE, partnered with existing FE teams, or are evaluating the possibility of joining.

For manufacturers, taking part in FE pays off. On the one hand, it demonstrates to the public their strength in the relevant technology R&D and raises brand awareness. On the other hand, FE has become a technology testbed for high-end electric vehicles, which offers no small benefit to the manufacturers themselves.

Based on these considerations, Audi announced that it would withdraw from the FIA World Endurance Championship (WEC), which it had contested for 18 consecutive years, after the 2016 season and switch to the Formula E championship.

For F1, this is as clear a threat as they come. If manufacturers all go to FE, their investment in F1 will shrink accordingly. Imagine if the F1 grid lost Ferrari, lost Mercedes-Benz, lost Renault — the value of F1 itself would evaporate.

F1 owns no automotive technology of its own, so its existence has always depended on the automakers themselves. When manufacturers face a new round of technological change and need to reorient their R&D, F1's test arrives. It is a dilemma: speed or the environment — choosing either one will cost the sport a portion of its audience.

F1 seemed to change a bit in 2017. The fastest lap record of 1:32.573 set by Schumacher in 2004 was finally broken by Lewis Hamilton thirteen years later. After F1 relaxed its limits on body and tire widths, the cars gained more than three seconds overall in outright speed.

Although F1 remains one of the most-watched sports in the world, the problem persists: how much patience do automakers still have left for the internal-combustion powertrain that F1 represents?
