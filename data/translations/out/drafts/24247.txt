How Did Vans Become the Icon of Skate Shoes Over the Past 50 Years — and How Did It Turn That Into History?

## "We have the most unshakable 'critics' in the world (young people), and they will judge whether we stay true to ourselves."

Regular article

One month before its 50th-anniversary celebrations, skate shoe brand Vans enjoyed another stroke of good fortune.

All through February, the American internet was flooded with images of Daniel Lara, a 14-year-old from California, and his white Vans shoes. In a 30-second video that racked up more than 16,000 likes within 48 hours of being uploaded, Daniel strolls nonchalantly down the street in his Vans while, in the voiceover, his friend keeps repeating "Damn Daniel" — meaning "Daniel, you're so cool."

The video was viewed 8 million times, "Damn Daniel" became the new catchphrase, Daniel and his friend were invited to appear on The Ellen DeGeneres Show, and Daniel's white Vans model was briefly flipped for sky-high prices on eBay... The unexpected viral fame left Vans, which had starred in the video without knowing it, both flattered and startled. Kevin Bailey, the company's current president, called "Damn Daniel" a wonderful moment — the kind of social-media-fueled instant popularity that even the brand itself probably couldn't manufacture.

Even if the way it wins over young people has changed — Daniel didn't make his entrance on a skateboard — such "lucky moments" are hardly rare in Vans' fifty-year history.

The 1970s saw the second wave of American skateboard culture. Born out of surfing, the sport reached its peak when California was hit by a rare drought in the 1970s. And the drought drained more than swimming pools and drainage ditches: with the U.S. economy mired in stagflation, vast numbers of public spaces were abandoned, California's public education system ran into trouble, and young people began dropping out of school to join the ranks of street skaters.

![](/uploads/image/201603/d61b367295f3.jpg)

When this crowd of skateboarding kids took notice of Vans, the company — caught up in the same wave — had no idea what was happening, because it had never thought of itself as a skate shoe brand. At the start, founder Paul Van Doren's ambition was simply to make a durable, inexpensive shoe for the masses.

Before skate culture came along as its savior, Paul ran his business in a rather aggressive way. He opened his first store in Anaheim, California, on March 16, 1966 — before the factory had even delivered its first shoes — and in Vans' early days he rushed to expand, at his most extreme opening a new store every week. [His son Steve recalled](http://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/the-history-of-vans/#11): "My father's accountant once came to him and said: 'Six of our ten stores are losing money.' And my father said: 'Fine, then let's open ten more stores.'"

Paul had oversimplified the business. Fortunately, his brother James Van Doren was a mechanical engineer who, after earning his degree at Northeastern University, also worked at a rubber and athletic shoe factory. The rubber compound James helped Paul develop would become the key to winning over skateboarders: Vans' famous "waffle" sole.

This magical rubber formula delivered the grip that most so-called skate shoes of the time could not, keeping riders from slipping — Vans' rubber sole let them stand firmly planted on their boards. [Steve, who had by then started helping run the business, only realized this in hindsight](http://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/the-history-of-vans/#11). He simply said: "At first we sold a few tons of the classic #44, and then all kinds of custom orders started pouring in."

![The Vans #44, later known as the Authentic — meaning the original, the real thing.](/uploads/image/201603/b79a66d0dda4.jpg)

At the time, many top skaters wouldn't wear anything but Vans. Tony Alva, Stacy Peralta, Jerry Valdez, and the entire Z-Boys skate crew treated Vans almost as their "team uniform" — the famous mid-1970s skate collective that invented many of the foundational aerial tricks. Vans itself caught on and began actively courting the scene. ["We wrote Stacey Peralta a $300 check to travel around the world wearing our shoes. Every store manager would supply shoes to seven or eight skateboarding kids."](http://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/the-history-of-vans/#11)

In 1976, Vans launched a deep collaboration with Tony Alva, the most famous skate star of the day. Together they developed a new shoe, the #95 (also known as the Era), designed specifically for skateboarding. The phrase "off the wall," used by Tony Alva and Stacy Peralta, was then adopted by Vans as its logo, and the brand bound itself tightly to the sport. [In the words of American musician Henry Rollins](http://footwearnews.com/2016/focus/athletic-outdoor/henry-rollins-band-vans-sneakers-ozzy-osbourne-black-flag-201244/), "Vans is an icon, they're a visual language — (wearing Vans) lets other people know you understand this culture."

As a sport born of street culture, it was, to some degree, exclusionary — Nike, for instance, ran into difficulty when it later tried to enter the skate shoe market, because skate shops stayed loyal to the niche brands. But Vans' early fans had grown entirely out of skaters' spontaneous advocacy: you'd see a highly skilled skater on the street wearing Vans, or, [as Tony Alva put it](http://time.com/4261248/vans-50th-anniversary-tony-alva-steve-van-doren/): "Vans are the true shoes for skateboarding. They are the best ever."

[Vans' footwear color and trends director Rian Pozzebon said](http://www.complex.com/style/2016/03/how-vans-became-a-fashion-sneaker): "The popularity (of skateboarding) made everyone know Vans as a skate shoe brand. It expanded Vans' market and showed what the brand stands for." Overall, the technological edge of the waffle sole gave Vans a firm foothold in this vibrant new culture.

![The collaboration between Tony Alva and Vans continues to this day. Here is a line of skate shoes he launched with Vans in 2012.](/uploads/image/201603/20426fad2776.jpg)

As enviable as it may sound, Vans soon stumbled into another heaven-sent opportunity. In the 1982 teen film *Fast Times at Ridgemont High*, a pair of checkered Vans worn by star Sean Penn instantly became a smash hit.

Sean Penn liked to show up in Vans everywhere. In fact, even before the film's release, checkered Vans were already hugely popular — some underground record covers had begun using the shoe's pattern — and after the film came out, Vans' sales grew from $20 million to $45 million, more than doubling.

"My father had only planned to sell his shoes in California. Now he had no choice, because the whole country wanted these shoes," [Steve said](http://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/the-history-of-vans/#11). "In the end we sold several million pairs of checkered Vans."

![This is Sean Penn, wearing the famous checkered Vans skate shoes on his feet.](/uploads/image/201603/0c0b6d07c929.jpg)

James, who had introduced the checkered Vans, now made a wrong decision — but his prestige at the time was so high that nobody questioned it: Vans stepped out of skateboarding and began making basketball shoes, soccer shoes, baseball cleats, football shoes... every sport you could think of, they wanted to try. In James' mind, Vans would be the next Nike.

Rushing into so many new markets dragged the company down. Even though skate shoe sales remained solid, the dismal performance of the other categories became a huge burden for Vans.

This is actually easy to understand. In those other categories, Vans had no inherent advantage at all — its core competitiveness, the rubber compound that offered superior grip, didn't apply to other products. At the same time, it faced competition from far more professional sports brands like Nike, Adidas, and Puma.

In 1984, Vans declared bankruptcy — barely two years after the wave sparked by *Fast Times at Ridgemont High*. Of that episode, [Steve has a pithy summary](http://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/the-history-of-vans/#11): "James did a great job, but he made one mistake: he wanted Vans to become a kind of brand that Vans could never be."

After filing for bankruptcy protection, Paul took the company back. He slashed every expense, stopped advertising, and Vans' operations became the simplest possible cycle: produce, sell, produce again. Drawing on the technology and brand equity Vans had accumulated, the company paid off its entire $12 million debt within three years. Perhaps having lived through this ordeal, Paul — who was about retirement age anyway — sold Vans to the San Francisco bank McCown De Leeuw & Co. in 1988 for $75 million and stepped out of day-to-day operations.

![](/uploads/image/201603/1909d12ef91c.jpg)

At the 2016 Grammy Awards, 21-year-old rapper Allan Kingdom made his first red-carpet appearance. Unlike the stars decked out in various luxury brands, he wore a casual tan suit by Joseph Abboud, a white shirt by Avion Clothiers, and a pair of white Vans Sk8-Hi shoes. In an interview, he said he first learned about Vans from a 2009 rap song of the same name by The Pack, when he was still in middle school. ["All my friends started wearing Sk8-His, and I thought that was cool."](http://www.complex.com/style/2016/03/how-vans-became-a-fashion-sneaker)

Allan has never skateboarded in his life — and by now, Vans is no longer just a skate shoe brand.

The shift can still be traced back to Vans' acquisition. In 1993, its performance took a nosedive, and the following year's profit was only $4 million. Skateboarding hit a low point in the early 1990s — professional skating was seen as a worthless career, and the graphic on legendary skater Tony Hawk's final signature board, released in 1993, was a giant ship sinking to the bottom of the sea. Meanwhile, running shoes from Nike and Reebok became the trend of the moment.

"People forgot about our classics. The trends changed, and we were far away from those trends," [Steve said](http://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/the-history-of-vans/#11).

Vans could have chased that trend by making running shoes again, but R&D capability remained its biggest weakness — it couldn't develop mature products or a production line. At the same time, California's strict environmental laws kept certain new materials out of production. Gary Schoenfeld, the company's president at the time, made what may be the most important decision in company history: Vans' corporate philosophy would no longer be about manufacturing — it would become a marketing company.

Gary wanted to stage a national amateur skateboarding competition to put the sport back in the spotlight. When he called show organizer Kevin Lyman asking for help, he got an "unexpected surprise," and ended up deciding to sponsor a national rock tour festival Kevin was planning. Nobody remembers that skateboarding competition anymore, but the Vans Warped Tour, held every year from April to August, went on to become America's largest touring music festival, with shows taking place in cities across the country every single day.

![The 2016 Vans Warped Tour kicks off on March 22.](/uploads/image/201603/5a0d15be61f5.jpg)

Looking back from 2016 at that decision, Vans began to stop depending on the rise and fall of skate culture, stepped out of that circle, and went looking for "the next wave" — or rather, the next growth point. ["We tied ourselves to punk rock, and musicians in lots of bands started wearing Vans,"](http://www.sneakerfreaker.com/features/the-history-of-vans/#11) — in 2002, Vans even launched its own record label, though it was mostly a marketing exercise.

From then on, marketing campaigns grew more numerous and more varied. In 1996, Vans reached a long-term collaboration with streetwear brand Supreme, considered a key factor in driving growth. It went on to release co-branded collections with fashion designers like Rebecca Taylor and Luella Bartley. Through music and fashion, Vans won a broader market: in 2001 the company's revenue reached $340 million, with profit approaching $15 million.

[*Harvard Business Review* turned Vans' performance in those years into a case study, and its assessment and worries read remarkably prescient](https://www.google.com.hk/url?sa=t&rct=j&q=&esrc=s&source=web&cd=1&cad=rja&uact=8&ved=0ahUKEwj495K4gdLLAhUM2GMKHQX3DoMQFggaMAA&url=%68%74%74%70%73%3a%2f%2f%68%62%72%2e%6f%72%67%2f%70%72%6f%64%75%63%74%2f%56%61%6e%73%2d%2d%53%6b%61%74%69%6e%67%2d%6f%6e%2d%41%69%72%2f%61%6e%2f%35%30%32%30%37%37%2d%50%44%46%2d%45%4e%47&usg=AFQjCNHwpERC5x3AFXswAwC2Ab3nY2wnsg&sig2=JJF1xzNBmTxjjyHP_-3-qQ): "In recent years, CEO Gary Schoenfeld appears to have revitalized the Vans brand. However, it is not yet clear whether the company has fallen into a dangerous position: as it moves into the mass market, it is losing its core consumers."

But Vans didn't seem to think it was surrounded by peril — especially after VF Corporation, the longtime American apparel group, acquired Vans for $400 million in 2004, at which point mass-market-ization became inevitable. If you know how VF operates (we once wrote about the predicament of another outdoor brand in its portfolio, The North Face — an internal employee once joked that "The North Face is sold to Beijingers who have never even left the Fifth Ring Road"), the channels and marketing resources it owns can help Vans expand into new markets. But while the group expects Vans to make money, the brand's DNA is also changing.

In 2005, Vans teamed up with Marc Jacobs on a co-branded collection. Jacobs was then creative director of LV, a big name in the fashion world, and his collaboration with a skate brand from California amounted to a signal of approval for those in the know.

![Vans x Marc Jacobs](/uploads/image/201603/0f25d48e4196.jpg)

In fact, Vans had been a piece of many designers' youthful memories — they wore it when they used to skate — and the Vans Slip-on silhouette (commonly known as the "slip-on") is so simple in shape that it leaves enormous room for creative reinvention. Pendleton, Kenzo Takada, Diemme, WTAP, Rei Kawakubo, including The North Face — whether luxury giants or niche street labels, all were happy to collaborate with Vans.

These moves didn't seem to stray from the old track, and they worked. "When we first took over, Vans was a $325 million-a-year business that barely made any money," said Steve Rendle, VF Group's chairman and COO. "Fast-forward to today, and Vans is one of the four most profitable brands in our group, with $2.2 billion in revenue."

Rendle believes that making Vans "fashionable" has brought the brand enormous benefits (he means, of course, the ability to make money) — especially in places like Asia, where skate culture is not that strong and there is plenty of room in the footwear market, Vans' new strategy can help it quickly win over hordes of young people. [Brian Smith, Vans China's marketing director, said](https://jingdaily.com/skater-goes-chic-in-china-vans-taps-into-chinese-creative-class/): "In China, what we focus on is creativity. Anyone connected to creativity — whether art, music, skateboarding, or street culture — is our customer."

Of course, there is a very practical calculus behind this: even in the U.S., skate culture has shown further signs of decline compared with the 1990s. [According to survey data from the Outdoor Foundation, under the Outdoor Industry Association](https://www.google.com.hk/url?sa=t&rct=j&q=&esrc=s&source=web&cd=1&cad=rja&uact=8&ved=0ahUKEwjMs8jJgtLLAhUI42MKHVfFDIkQFggbMAA&url=%68%74%74%70%3a%2f%2f%77%77%77%2e%73%74%61%74%69%73%74%69%63%2e%63%6f%6d%2f%73%74%61%74%69%73%74%69%63%73%2f%31%39%31%33%30%38%2f%70%61%72%74%69%63%69%70%61%6e%74%73%2d%69%6e%2d%73%6b%61%74%65%62%6f%61%72%64%69%6e%67%2d%69%6e%2d%74%68%65%2d%75%73%2d%73%69%6e%63%65%2d%32%30%30%36%2f&usg=AFQjCNFak_SxFS7L7KYwchZHHh4mHieOzw&sig2=mM_rX_vDOmeV8SWrhlFHmw), 8.75 million American teenagers were skateboarding in 2006; by 2013, that number had fallen to 3.48 million. The professional skate shoe market is shrinking, and for Vans to survive, going mass-market is almost unavoidable.

In 2014, Vans launched its new season of marketing under the theme Living Off the Wall. In the 14 short films released with it, the content was remarkably diverse — from motorcycles and punk to tattoos and the gypsy lifestyle. The style Vans promotes is still "cool," and skateboarding is certainly still there, but it takes up a much smaller share of the screen. Looking at the product line, Vans also released more shoes beyond the professional skate category, like the Mountain Edition Collection, in an attempt to attract "people outside the circle."

![The most widely shared image from Vans' Living Off the Wall campaign.](/uploads/image/201603/bab05350377c.jpg)

Commercially, this strategy has undoubtedly succeeded. In 2014, Vans' revenue reached $2 billion, marking double-digit growth for the fifth consecutive year. But among Vans' once-core consumers — skateboarders — Vans is no longer the one and only choice, or even the top choice. Ask people in skateboarding and they'll reel off a long list: 3E (ES, Etnies, Emerica), Lakai, Globe, D&C, Fallen. Among them are brands like D&C, which also makes snowboard boots and sells itself on extreme sports, and companies like Emerica, which has focused on skateboarding for over a decade — the latter even launched a Workwear product line specifically for skater Andrew Reynolds in 2013. In the promo video at the time, he said: it will never really fade away. It's never a case of "oh, now we're going to do this, now we're going to chase that new trend" — we just stay true to skateboarding.

The entry of sports giants like Nike and Adidas has also brought new pressure on Vans. They have the ability to apply sports technology from other fields to skate shoes — Nike SB and adidas ZX introduced "new parameters" to this street sport, such as easy on-and-off, protection, and impact absorption on landing.

Just last week, Vans kicked off its 50th-anniversary events in ten cities around the world — from New York and London to Hong Kong and mainland China — offering DIY workshops, shoe customization, a 50th-anniversary retrospective exhibition, and music performances. This full combo is by now a familiar playbook. Launched in tandem were the 50th-anniversary black-and-gold commemorative collection and the pro classics commemorative collection, serving the different needs of everyday wearers and professional skaters respectively — the pro line combines the latest technology with reinforced cushioning and upper durability. Of course, they didn't forget the fashion world either: the pink co-branded Vans x Opening Ceremony collection with the top-tier boutique will go on sale in the coming days.

Looking back at Damn Daniel's accidental viral fame a month ago, young people didn't flock to him because of Daniel's masterful skateboarding skills — they simply found the video hilariously absurd; it made them laugh.

What people care most about is still that old question: has capital let Vans win the market in the short term while losing itself in the long run? When fashion trade publication WWD interviewed Kevin Bailey, Vans' current president, not long ago, it asked exactly that question: has Vans' customer changed? Faced with such doubts, Bailey seemed a bit dismissive.

"We're not just a skateboarding company — that's only part of the story. Vans is a youth culture brand... We have the most unshakable 'critics' in the world (young people)," he said. "They will judge whether we stay true to ourselves."
