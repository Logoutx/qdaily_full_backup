Can so-called professional social networking sites really help you out? If you want to meet people, what's the best way to go about it?

There's a saying that circulates in Silicon Valley: "If an engineer updates his LinkedIn profile in the morning, by lunch he'll have five job offers."

Chinese office workers and entrepreneurs took this saying to heart long ago. Like Reid Hoffman, founder of the professional social platform LinkedIn, they firmly believe that getting to know strangers in the workplace can help employees change their careers — and help companies change themselves. Another oft-told story concerns the video company Netflix. In 2012, Netflix named 170 star employees, only 9 of whom were not registered on LinkedIn, and the CEO pressed every one of them to sign up, because he wanted employees to be able to "find the nodes of the company's network" through LinkedIn.

Through the LinkedIn website, you can filter for the professionals you want to find by criteria such as school, company, and professional background — you can even direct-message your target company and build your professional network. In the United States, this model is open, egalitarian, and effective. People keep exploring which parts of the model can be copied to China and succeed.

For years now, the desire to create a similar professional networking platform in China has been strong. Tianji.com, Dajie.com, Ushi.com, and Wealink appeared one after another, but some you may never have heard of, and others shut down before you ever did. Tianji merged with Viadeo in 2009, hoping to build up users by leaning on this global professional social network for non-English-speaking countries, but it terminated service at the end of last year. Dajie once partnered with Sina Weibo to launch features like micro-recruiting and micro-networking, but its impact was underwhelming.

On Zhihu, the Q&A platform that can likewise be used to meet strangers in the workplace, people ask from time to time: do domestic social platforms still stand a chance? A [Zhihu user](https://www.zhihu.com/question/22845899) named Xia Zhenqiang summed it up: "Most of China's current professional social networking sites face problems like low user stickiness and an unclear profit model."

When the much-watched LinkedIn finally entered China in 2014, it too fell short of expectations. Shen Boyang, president of LinkedIn China, admitted in an interview with *QDaily* last year that more than a year after launch, LinkedIn still could not attract the majority of Chinese-language users, and he blamed the bottleneck on the product itself — shortcomings on mobile such as "you can't register with your phone, and you can't share via QR code."

But the problem seems to run deeper. The need for workplace socializing is still there; we're just not used to the LinkedIn way of doing things. Its "ticket of admission," designed for meeting strangers in the workplace, requires, beyond a basic command of English, a professional résumé detailing your work history and a high degree of trust in strangers.

In China, could the "ticket of admission" to professional networking — built on detailed professional profiles and an egalitarian, open platform — be replaced by something else?

To answer the question "How do Chinese professionals expand their workplace networks?", we interviewed 20 people and picked out some of the most valuable responses.

## **Wang Xin, former Vice President of Kingsoft / Founder of Mentou Business School**

**In China, what's a relatively effective way to get to know strangers in the workplace?**

> In China, one-on-one stranger socializing is a "fake" need. Chinese people are better suited to group socializing — for example, Chinese alumni associations and hometown associations. We former Kingsoft employees even have a "Jiu Jinshan Club" (Old Kingsoft Club). At an alumni gathering there's a pretext for saying hello: what year you joined, what year you left, who your boss was back then. Mentou Business School's students are also classmates in a virtual classroom, a class community, and for one-on-one relationships, you can't get around WeChat. "Mentou" (mantou) comes from the English word "mentor." We teach courses online, mainly courses for internet PMs (product managers), and afterwards we pull the students into WeChat groups to discuss the course content with one another. Since Mentou was founded, it has had more than 400,000 users and over 100 WeChat groups.

**How is this kind of professional networking different from a business school in the usual sense?**

> After working at Kingsoft for 10 years, I went to China Europe International Business School (CEIBS) for an EMBA. A business school will devise all kinds of ways to expose you to different people. In my cohort, the CEIBS classmates from Beijing and Shanghai often organized activities together — sports meets, various forums, and so on. This kind of cross-boundary exchange breaks up your class and lets you meet people from different classes and different fields.
> But a business school easily costs 700,000 yuan, or 400,000 to 500,000 yuan — far beyond most people's budgets. The courses a business school teaches are better suited to people who have worked for 5–10 years; fresh graduates have no channel for gaining knowledge and building a network.

## **Yu Dongyu, Consultant at Ogilvy**

**Where do you get to know strangers in the workplace?**

> After returning to China, I often attend events put on by the University of Southern California (USC) Beijing Alumni Association — basically once a month. There are dinners, hikes, salons, startup forums, and more. Last year I also took part in a film and television industry salon initiated by Ji Erwei, a director of Ruige Media and an alumnus, and hosted by the alumni association; more than 100 people attended, and I met people from all kinds of industries. Big events like these draw over a hundred people, small gatherings have twenty or thirty, and once in a while you run into industry veterans from BAT (Baidu, Alibaba, Tencent). The alumni association also co-hosts events with other schools — in April, for instance, we'll hold an offline CS showdown with our archrival UCLA.

**Do you think this approach works?**

> Yes — mutual help among alumni matters a lot. Because the number of overseas students returning to China has grown over the past two years, the Beijing office organized online job fairs for returning students on demand, inviting 80 domestic and overseas companies to take part. In August there will also be two offline job fairs in Beijing and Shanghai just for returned overseas students. We have our own WeChat group too, where alumni regularly post job openings from their companies, and alumni refer résumés to one another.

## **Li Changye, Pushi Yingcai Management Consulting (Executive Search)**

**Where do you get to know strangers in the workplace?**

> I search on Zhihu by professional information, such as company and job title. If that information isn't public, I'll search through Zhihu topics, tags, and post replies.

**Do you think this approach works?**

> Relatively effective, because Zhihu is still a platform fairly focused on knowledge sharing. Some people feel an approach like this intrudes on them — after all, this isn't a social recruiting platform. But generally I don't use it much; I'll only give it a try when I really can't find someone, mainly because Zhihu users' profile information is incomplete — users can fill it in or not — and its authenticity and timeliness aren't great either.

**Do you meet these people offline?**

> Whether we meet offline mainly depends on whether both sides can help each other's professional growth. If the opportunity I'm offering is something he's interested in, and he's qualified for the position I have, then we'll get to know each other and meet offline.

## **Cao Xiaocong, Directing Department Student at Shanghai Theatre Academy**

**Where do you get to know strangers in the workplace?**

> I answer questions on Zhihu — questions about drama, film and television, and acting. I've racked up nearly 3,000 upvotes, and once one of my answers made it into Zhihu Daily; after that, people added me every day, and for a while it was pretty much four or five people adding me on WeChat every day. Among the people who added me, exam candidates made up a portion, and film and television companies and classmates in related majors at various schools made up the rest.
> I also attended offline salon events at the "Zhihu Salt Club," and when I got there I found that everyone actually "knew each other indirectly" — which just shows how small the drama circle is.

**Do you think this approach works?**

> Because my work includes education and training as well as taking on scriptwriting commissions, Zhihu has still helped me. For example, that short story of mine that made it into Zhihu Daily — I posted it hoping to find an interested publisher, and that's how friends at film and television companies added me. But people you add on Zhihu won't necessarily lead to a working relationship right away, and Zhihu isn't a headhunter; it can, however, serve as a pool of contacts to develop, and on top of that you get to meet a lot of peers.

## **Shi Beichen, Chief Content Officer at Zhengyang New Media**

**Where do you get to know strangers in the workplace?**

> Last May I joined Zaih, where I teach "How to Build a Personal Brand Through Writing." I've become friends with many of the people I've met, and I still have meals with lots of my students now — we hit it off and have plenty to talk about. Because writing is a fairly skills-oriented topic, the students are a very mixed group: people from state-owned enterprises and foreign companies, entrepreneurs, people from private companies, and fresh graduates, among others.
> Over the past 10 months I've talked with 100 people in total (Editor's note: that number ranks 11th on the Zaih platform in the career development category — not typical), earning 100,000 yuan in pocket money. At first it was 100 yuan per hour; now it's 2,000 yuan per hour. I think there's a lot my students can teach me — everyone has their strengths. I teach them about writing, but they can teach me about other fields; we're mentors to each other. Zaih is a relatively ceremonial format for meeting. Students who come to see me bring a notebook and a pen, and they'll send me their questions in advance before the meeting; often they go home with several pages of notes. It's very formal.
>
> ![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702173218SpHXou67rE2ULT1I.jpg-w600)
> Shi Beichen's Zaih page

**How is this different from just meeting someone online and striking up a conversation?**

> In professional socializing, filtering people matters, and there are two ways to do it: the first is money, and the second is rules — Zaih sets a rule. Many people have social dilemmas: a friend, or someone you don't know that well, wants to book you for a meal or coffee to pick your brain for a whole day. Some people can't bring themselves to say no, so they agree to entertain these people. And the person coming to consult you may not have prepared adequately; after chatting with many of them, you know they got nothing out of it — time was spent, but there was no value. The beauty of Zaih is that, to a degree, it forces the friends around you to learn to respect your time. I've set a price for my time, and I also tell you what kinds of questions I can answer. This rule tells you how I can help you, and it's a very good way to filter people.

**What kind of person is this approach suited to?**

> I think it suits entrepreneurs quite well — say you're completely unfamiliar with a particular field; Zaih can solve that problem in one shot, getting you familiar with and up to speed on an unfamiliar field. It's a bit like a 101-level introductory course, because one hour can't teach you how to write; you'd probably have to try writing 10 or 20 pieces yourself before you could learn.

## **Fu Yuan, Senior Business Development Manager (company name withheld at her request)**

**Where do you get to know strangers in the workplace?**

> I saw a platform called "Wo You Fan" ("I Have Meals") on Weibo, followed it on WeChat, and went to try it out. My first time was last April. They invite big names from various industries to a private-kitchen dinner party limited to 6 guests, where you socialize over a meal. At the dinners I've attended, the hosts have included a chemical engineer and an architectural designer — that designer even has a small courtyard of his own design by Tanzhe Temple in western Beijing. The guests at these dinners also come from different industries; I've met diners from finance and investment circles, as well as university students. Some were fresh on the job, others were senior executives or startup CEOs.

**How much does it cost to get to know these people?**

> At the dinners I've attended, some cost 99 yuan, others 200 or 300 yuan, and the pricier ones can reach 498 yuan.
>

**Do you think this approach works?**

> Before, I might have just watched movies or gone shopping — it was hard to meet new friends. All my friends were people from my own work circle. This approach has let me get to know some new friends, and after the dinners we'll even make plans to go to the vegetable market or the flower market together, or attend more "Wo You Fan" dinners.

## **Hu Wenqian, Uber Shanghai HR**

**Are there opportunities to meet strangers in the workplace that most people aren't aware of?**

> Uber holds a Happy Hour almost every month — it's been held in Beijing, Shanghai, Wuhan, and Guangzhou. Especially when we enter a new city, or when a city has open positions to fill, we make H5 pages and posters, but Weibo and WeChat remain our two main publicity channels. We review the applications submitted and select 50 to 60 people to attend the happy hour.

**What format does the happy hour take?**

> It's usually held at a casual little bar. After everyone signs in, Uber employees share some of the company's current situation and the positions it's hiring for. Then we divide everyone into small groups, each with 1–2 Uber employees and around 10 candidates. We give everyone topics to discuss freely, and there's also a Q&A session. During this process we observe how they perform and how they interact with others. At the end, we have one-on-one chats with the people who interest us. Once candidates pass the happy-hour stage, they're invited into the formal interview process.

## **Qi Tejia, Maimai Product Director**

**Maimai is a Chinese professional social and recruiting platform similar to LinkedIn. Have you discovered any particular characteristics of the Chinese market?**

> Maimai started out doing anonymous workplace socializing and recruiting, but we found user stickiness for the recruiting function was low — after all, how many times a year can a person change jobs? The anonymous gossip, though, got a great response (Editor's note: you can vent about companies, ask about company salaries, and so on). But that alone was incomplete, because anonymity is only one part of workplace life, so we launched real-name posts. We gradually discovered the value of socializing, and so we gradually increased the share of social features. Workplace socializing in China has only just begun.

**What kinds of professional social scenarios do you think could be effective?**

> We've set up two typical scenarios. (One is) we group people by the industry they work in and the career direction they're headed, which means each person has a higher probability of seeing the things, people, and content within their own circle. When everyone's in the same circle, they can post real-name status updates and also comment on and reply to other people's updates, which leads to new friendships — this one is about the people.
> The other scenario is industry news and points of interest, where everyone discusses what's happening in their industry. Compared with the getting-to-know-each-other scenario just mentioned, industry news is about the content: everyone discusses it together and vents about unreasonable phenomena, and so on.
>
> ![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702173220gfwU3o1myiTOZktp.jpg-w600)
> Maimai's real-name feed

**What kind of person is this approach suited to?**

> One type of user is primarily focused on recruiting, with a clear goal of finding a job. The second type has an unclear goal and needs to gradually find the right people and see the right content through social activity. The ratio of these two types is roughly 1 to 5.

## **Chang Meng, Strategy Research Lead at Kanzhun.com / Boss Zhipin**

**What's it like getting to know strangers in the workplace on Boss Zhipin?**

> You don't have to endure a long wait after sending in your résumé. On **Boss**, you just need to see a position and you can recommend yourself, go up and greet the employer, and send over all your information. **Boss** does recruiting in a disintermediated, direct-communication form. To some degree, this approach puts the job seeker in the client's position, because a large number of bosses initiate the chat themselves.

**Where do you yourself get to know strangers in the workplace?**

> I have a dozen or so industry WeChat groups. The most numerous are TMT (technology, media, telecom) industry research groups, as well as experience-exchange groups. In groups like these TMT ones, people mostly discuss trends, where the wind is blowing, and even some very nuts-and-bolts, practical operational matters. Often someone bursts into a group asking whether anyone knows so-and-so at such-and-such a company because they want to reach out about a partnership or some communication, and in the end someone gives them a contact.

**Do you think this approach works?**

> It depends. I'll stay in a group for a while to judge the group's activity level, the caliber of the people chatting, and the quality of the conversation. If it's OK, I stay; if it's not OK, I'm out.

## **Liu Xin, Founder of Manxin Technology Co., Ltd.**

**I heard you also joined a workplace group of strangers. What's different about it?**

> I paid 5,000 yuan to join the "Grow Together" Alipay group. The group looks pretty much like a WeChat group; the only difference is that each group has a set joining fee and comes with an administrator. (Editor's note: Groups of this kind usually set two rules. First, you pay an annual fee, which rises as the group's membership grows. Second, you must commit to sharing your experience once within a year, in any industry. To some extent, the mechanism of ever-rising prices actually encourages you to join as early as possible, but from a time-cost perspective, this practice is not without its logic.)

**Do you think this approach works?**

> I think so. After I joined the group and shared my experience, more than a dozen people in the group added me as a friend, and I pulled these people into another Alipay group I had set up, with a lower price of 598 yuan — I made back my 5,000 yuan right away."

**This mechanism sounds a bit like a pyramid scheme. For meeting strangers in the workplace, where do you think the value of the group lies?**

> I think the value of meeting even one right person in the group far exceeds that 5,000 yuan. For example, someone in the group became an employee of my company, and someone else became a business partner of mine. Every day on WeChat a lot of people apply to add me as a friend. At first I would agree to meet them offline for a chat, but a lot of what these people said never materialized into concrete cooperation. But when you arrange an offline meeting with someone from the Grow Together group, because you already know in advance what that person is good at, the conversation can immediately turn into something concrete — like cross-promoting each other's official accounts, which is something you can execute right away and easily.
