At 66, the Japanese Graphic-Design Magazine *IDEA* Has an Exhibition in Shanghai — Still Avant-Garde, Still Inspiring

One point that came up in the conversation: Japanese society is becoming increasingly inward-looking. Young people are unwilling to go abroad, content with the status quo in study and work alike. They think they are the ones who are different — but that is a dangerous idea.

On March 9, a small exhibition devoted to the venerable Japanese graphic-design magazine *IDEA* (アイデア) opened inside Banana Fish Bookstore on Shiquan Road in Shanghai’s Putuo District. For half a century before the rise of the internet, the magazine had been regarded as a bible by a generation of Asian graphic designers. Back then, visual references were not easy to come by.

[*IDEA*](http://www.idea-mag.com/en/idea_magazine/) was founded in 1953 as a bilingual quarterly devoted to graphic design and typography. Through it, the pioneering visual languages of other countries were introduced into Japan, enriching the country’s rising design scene. Its most enduring traits have been abundant design creativity, exquisite paper and printing, rigorous typography, and in-depth discussion of its subjects.

Inside the small glass-walled gallery, 45 issues of *IDEA* selected by different graphic designers span the years from 1960 to 2018. Each issue is built around a single theme; some were sourced from old Japanese bookstores, others bought by the designers when they were students. Taken together, the magazines have strikingly varied covers and thick pages, and even the earliest issue still looks boldly avant-garde today.

“*IDEA* belongs to a different kind of magazine. It does not rely on advertising revenue; it survives on sales. Looking at the Japanese magazine world over the past decade, many magazines have disappeared,” former editor-in-chief Kiyonori Muroga [once said in an interview](https://www.topys.cn/article/17672.html). He was one of the magazine’s central figures, leading *IDEA* from the late 1990s into the new millennium for 19 years.

Among the rare early special features on display is [Issue 44 of *IDEA* from 1960](http://www.idea-mag.com/en/idea_magazine/044/), with a cover by Japanese graphic designer [Ikko Tanaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikko_Tanaka) (Ikko Tanaka). This issue was planned to mark the tenth anniversary of the Japan Advertising Artists Club. Founded in 1951, the [Japan Advertising Artists Club (JAAC)](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%97%A5%E6%9C%AC%E5%AE%A3%E4%BC%9D%E7%BE%8E%E8%A1%93%E4%BC%9A) was the design organization that has influenced Japanese design most deeply; beginning in 1953 it called for public submissions from across Japanese society, bringing many design talents to the fore. The 1964 Tokyo Olympics then pushed the graphic design of the JAAC onto the world stage. But the global student movement of 1968 fed campus unrest, and in 1970 the JAAC was forced to disband.

Still, after the 1964 Olympics, talented and boldly experimental Japanese graphic designers began to emerge in large numbers, absorbing international visual elements and combining them with native traditions. Ikko Tanaka, who designed cover posters for *IDEA* in its early years, was one of them.

“His work feels modern yet deeply shaped by Japanese culture, with a distinctive fusion of Japanese calligraphy and Western typography that set him apart from his peers,” [poster artist Paul Davis once said](https://www.nytimes.com/2002/01/24/arts/ikko-tanaka-71-japanese-graphic-designer.html). As a contemporary Japanese designer active from the late 1950s, Tanaka was known for modernist restraint, using strong geometric blocks as bold communicative images and suffusing his work with a Japanese sensibility. In his posters one often finds painted faces and calligraphic brushwork: “strong, clean, colorful, playful, precise.”

In the 1980s Tanaka, working as a graphic designer, [developed the minimalist concept covering clothing, furniture, and food for Muji’s first Tokyo store](https://www.nytimes.com/1991/11/28/garden/if-you-want-to-make-an-understatement.html). Ten years later, in 1991, the first Muji store outside Japan opened in London: “A place for minimalists: they like wastepaper baskets made of corrugated cardboard, shirts of plain but well-made cotton without extra trim or corporate logos,” the *New York Times* [reported that same year](https://www.nytimes.com/1991/11/28/garden/if-you-want-to-make-an-understatement.html).

Another displayed issue, [*IDEA* No. 46](http://www.idea-mag.com/en/idea_magazine/046/), has a cover by Hiroshi Ohchi, the magazine’s first art director, who worked on many covers. The cover is one of his representative works, showing translucent, colorful pebbles scattered irregularly across the surface. On the cover of [*IDEA* No. 45](http://www.idea-mag.com/en/idea_magazine/045/), Ohchi used thick three-color stripes that run across the image, forming oscillating variations in the center with a clean, playful quality. This issue was published in January 1961.

Besides these, many recent issues are on display. For example, 2018’s Issue 380, [*Recent Works by Tadanori Yokoo Since 2010*](http://www.idea-mag.com/en/idea_magazine/380/), offers an in-depth essay and interview with Tadanori Yokoo; 2017’s Issue 376, *Graphic Designers and Exhibitions*, examines the relationship between graphic designers’ current conditions and curation; and 2007’s Issue 323, *Wim Crouwel*, [features the Dutch graphic designer and typographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wim_Crouwel) who co-founded the design studio Total Design in 1963 and designed the Dutch pavilion at the 1970 Osaka Expo.

On the magazine’s shifting audience, Osaka-based curator and graphic designer Tetsuya Goto told QDaily, “Compared with the generation before the internet, young Japanese today no longer care about foreign design. If an issue introduces an overseas designer, it means sales will definitely fall.”

As a graphic-design magazine that claims not to rely chiefly on advertising, *IDEA* faces challenges not only from the decline of publishing but also from the changing visual language of younger designers, shifts in information channels and reading habits, the explosion of information after the internet, and even changes in business models that have affected the way graphic designers work. The impact is enormous.

On changes in the design industry, former *IDEA* editor-in-chief Kiyonori Muroga [said in an interview](https://www.topys.cn/article/17672.html) that *IDEA* had of course considered electronic media, but that would mean losing the craft and technique of print, so it has not yet reached the stage of concrete implementation. He acknowledged that the decline of bookstores is unavoidable.

[Kiyonori Muroga](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/culture/2015/09/19/books/publishing-age-pervasive-design-interview-idea-magazines-kiyonori-muroga/#.XIkHDhMzbq1) believes the trend for magazines is to draw ever more from content that has not yet been discovered in daily life. “In the past, sales volume was an important measure of a magazine’s quality, but now content is becoming increasingly niche, fields more fragmented and numerous. That means a lot of interesting content has not been discovered by the magazine industry. Only magazines made this way can become interesting.”

Another extension of the exhibition is “Yellow Page,” a column that originally ran in *IDEA* Issues 365 through 373, written by Tetsuya Goto.

Because Goto “felt that younger generations in Japan were no longer concerned with design from outside the country” and lacked understanding of graphic designers in other Asian countries, in 2011, as guest editor of the Japan Typography Association publication *Typographics ti:*, he visited seven Asian cities — Seoul, Hong Kong, Singapore, Taipei, Shenzhen, Bangkok, and Beijing — interviewing graphic designers who had begun their practice in the mid-1990s, such as Beijing designer Xiaomage, Taipei designer Yung-Chen Nieh, and Hong Kong designer Javin Mo.

The purpose of the trip was to understand the current state of graphic designers in each place and to talk about their daily lives, sources of inspiration, and collected works. In the end, first-person interview records of the seven designers were published in *IDEA* over a two-year span under the name “Yellow Page” (inspired by the “Yellow Pages” directory). At the same time, Goto held several exhibitions in Osaka and Kyoto, presenting the collected Asian designers’ works, everyday objects, and typography to the Japanese public.

During this Asian research trip, Hong Kong designer Javin Mo served as Goto’s translator and project companion. He is the founder of the Hong Kong design studio Milkxhake and the curator of the Shanghai exhibition *IDEA: Yellow Page*. The magazine has already published the “Yellow Page” material.

In addition, we spoke with Tetsuya Goto and Javin Mo about details of the trip, the current state of Japanese graphic design, and the impact of the internet on a generation of young designers.

Q = QDaily

**Q: In the *IDEA* magazine “Yellow Page” series, the seven graphic designers’ first-person accounts are quite detailed, almost like stories. What specific questions did you ask during the interviews?**

This was different from discussing the local design industry directly. We focused more on “as a graphic designer, how do you live and work?” — for example, what time do you get up, where do you work, what time do you finish, what music do you like to listen to, what does your workspace look like; or where you come from, your experiences, how you came into contact with design.

It is a broad picture, yet very detailed. We tried to reconstruct a graphic designer’s real situation: inspiration and work. Of course the original aim was to let Japanese readers understand what their peers in other parts of Asia were doing, because Japan is an island nation with relatively few ties to other regions. And now Japanese society is becoming increasingly inward-looking; young people are unwilling to go abroad, content with the status quo in study and work alike. They think they are the ones who are different — but that is a dangerous idea.

**Q: The designers come from different countries and regions, and therefore different cultures. Which design-related phenomena caught your attention?**

Compared with the mainland, the relationships between book publishers and printers in Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Singapore are different. We were quite surprised, for example, by Beijing’s Artron Printing, a very large art-book printer that will print small-run independent publications. In Hong Kong or Taiwan, such things are handled by small teams; there is no such space. That was something we found quite interesting.

For graphic designers across Asia, the situation is remarkably similar: lifestyles, rhythms, and styles all resemble one another. Beijing graphic designer Xiaomage may be slightly different because he works for a publishing house; the others are mostly freelance. But apart from that, everyone’s life is essentially the same, listening to similar music. To some extent this is closely tied to globalization: the internet connects everyone, and the same is true for design — we are entering “global styles.”

**Q: In “Yellow Page,” you mentioned Seoul-based designers Sulki & Min once used posters in a Kyoto workshop to inspire design students to think during the process. In Japan, as a professor in art and design, how do you teach your students now? What observations do you have?**

I am still trying to find better ways to inspire students. Posters are one way, or postcards, but they amount to the same thing. Another thing is that I only know Japanese students, so I can only talk about them — the younger generation in Japan is becoming increasingly passive. When I was young and still a student, after the professor assigned a design topic in class, we were usually very curious, asking lots of questions, even naive ones, so that in the process we developed all kinds of ideas, or overturned them. This quality is extremely important for designers.

But now Japanese students no longer challenge. They always wait to be told what to do next, as if the teacher is feeding them knowledge, or they are always looking for answers — but in fact there are no answers. Design is a way of thinking, but they think there is a standard answer. I think this has to do with the fact that you can always find “answers” on the internet; people often go online to look for answers. The internet is influencing the next generation of Japanese designers.

**Q: You have both served as editor-in-chief in the topic selection of graphic-design magazines — *Typographics ti:* and *Design 360*. In the current landscape of graphic design, when producing a magazine, how do you approach topic selection? Do you consider sales?**

In fact we don’t care about sales, though maybe that is only because we are not the magazine owners. If sales look good, of course everyone is happy. For a graphic-design magazine, the main goal is still the visual, or the design core. If you look at old issues of *IDEA*, you can find a great deal of visual content, countless posters, and fewer words, because at that time there were no other channels for the latest information. For designers, magazines were almost the only source, especially for tracking the latest developments from the United States and Europe.

For example, if you saw a poster on the cover of an *IDEA* issue back then, it was probably by a famous designer. But now the speed of the internet lets you browse countless visual images in a minute; people no longer need a print magazine to relay information from the U.S. or Europe. So more in-depth content has become infinitely important for making a magazine today. *IDEA* is doing just that, focusing on increasingly refined typography and paper, things that electronic devices cannot do. But the difficulty is that typefaces differ; the gap between Chinese and Japanese typography is large. How to handle typography and layout is what *IDEA* values most right now.

Beyond that, another thing I care about is how to solve the identity of the graphic designer so that making books and designing posters no longer limits their function. Maybe we can do something else. Because the visual has always been important, past and present.

**Q: Are there successful cases at present?**

There are many. For example, Korean designer Na Kim designs the graphic magazine *Graphic* while providing full visual schemes for a local Seoul art museum and also holding exhibitions as an artist. This is not to say that being an artist is better than being a graphic designer, but that graphic design/visual work is not only for posters and covers; it can become something with a core, even though people usually do not view graphic design that way. Today, more graphic designers are working individually to design complete visual identities for city museums or art institutions over many years, becoming a recognizable identity. It belongs to the museum and also to the designer.

**Q: In the late 1990s, after the internet appeared, digital, dynamic, and massive amounts of visual information began to pour in. Today people can see huge billboards just standing on the street. Do you think the advantage of graphic design still exists? Is it declining, or more important?**

Any digital image form you can see is visual, which means conventional design is still important, and its core still returns to graphic design. Concept is the most important thing, and it comes from the most original idea — that is the core of graphic design. To use an analogy, the idea a poster conveys is the purpose; the poster is only the functional medium, and it can be replaced by any technology.

So to sum up, the importance of visual language has become ever stronger. If we talk about books, they have indeed weakened, but visual communication/visual language is growing. Perhaps in the future, instrumental graphic design will be replaced by vision consultations.

**Q: When I tried searching for this publication online, I noticed an interesting phenomenon: this veteran magazine still in operation has very little information and commentary online, which seems to confirm the distance between the internet and print media. When you were younger, what impression did this magazine leave on you?**

When I was studying in Hong Kong, the school library had a reference collection that included a complete run of *IDEA* from the 1970s through the 1980s, so I often went to browse them — that was mid-1990s Hong Kong.

After graduation I entered my first design studio; the boss was an active Hong Kong designer from the 1990s, and his generation had already started reading *IDEA* in the 1970s and 1980s and was far more deeply influenced by it. The magazine’s angle on choosing topics was very different. Although the *IDEA* editorial staff kept changing, the content dug deep; that was remarkable.

**Q: Are there similar graphic-design magazines in Japan you would recommend?**

[*+81*](http://www.plus81.com/?category=2), which is more visual; and *Illustration Note* (*Irasuto Nōto*), suitable for beginners.
