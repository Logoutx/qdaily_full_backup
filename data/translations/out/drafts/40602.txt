“The essence of culture is about people. If the people are gone, there is no value left.”

More than ten years later, Shu Haolun found himself thinking again about the footage he had shot in the alleyways.

He grew up in Dazhongli on Nanjing West Road, a community first built in 1925. It rose, then declined, and by 2008 it had been completely demolished; only one old assembly hall was “relocated” elsewhere. Those 83 years, Shu said, “ran parallel to the entire history of Shanghai’s development — they are a microcosm.”

“1925 happened to mark the start of Shanghai’s whole urbanization process, and its most golden era. By the 2008 Olympics, Dazhongli was erased against that backdrop — it was a highly condensed version of the entire history.”

Shu Haolun is a director, best known for a documentary called *Nostalgia* (《乡愁》), which was filmed in Dazhongli before its demolition and was called by American public radio NPR “a cinematic poem about a vanished Shanghai.” He went on to make four more films, including *Struggle* (《挣扎》) and *Young Blood* (《少年血》), but most people first heard of him through this documentary.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509104501ZSHNVXmU8k5JblGt.jpg-w600)

Yet even for someone who spent his entire childhood in Dazhongli, piecing these fragmented bits of history back together from memory has become increasingly difficult. Dazhongli has become HKRI Taikoo Hui, one of the largest commercial complexes in the Nanjing West Road shopping district, and its distinctive lane grid — three crossbars branching off a main stem — has been wiped clean. Old neighbors scattered to Shanghai’s outskirts or emigrated overseas when the demolition came; residents had long been predominantly elderly, and many have since passed away.

The old assembly hall, originally part of Minli Middle School and “relocated” to the south side of the mall, has become an upscale club within the complex called Cha House — named in honor of Cha Jimin, founder of HKR International. Shu recalls that he once took classes, ate in the canteen, and practiced gymnastics there alongside many middle school students from the nearby lanes, and he remembers the creaking floors and desks of the “old building.”

Photographer Wang Gangfeng was invited to document the entire relocation process of the “old building.” His photos, together with the documentary Shu shot on his own initiative, were archived by the developer as historical material that could be used for publicity in the future.

![The relocation of the Minli Middle School assembly hall underway. Image via [Shanghai Observer](http://www.shobserver.com/news/detail?id=13553)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509125650qCbMjf9QTvphYnuU.jpg-w600)

But what Wang Gangfeng dwells on more fondly are the old wooden cabinets, bamboo baskets, and spittoons he painstakingly salvaged from the Dazhongli construction site, already reduced to rubble — and how the last holdout was cleared out:

“At the time they took a fire escape ladder and propped it straight up to the third floor, carried a woman in her nineties out through the window, along with everything in her room, and sent her to the new apartment she was supposed to move into but hadn’t yet. Worried something might happen to her at her age, they had an ambulance waiting below, and the notary was there too. The police were furious the moment they saw me — basically saying, ‘You show up at a time like this and I’ll arrest you.’”

Also raised in an old house near Nanjing West Road, Wang spent decades in business learning how to deal with all kinds of people: smile and apologize when trouble arises, hand over a pack of cigarettes, make the rounds with gifts at every holiday, and if all else fails, simply give the place a wide berth. This skill set allowed his [independent photography studio](http://www.gangofonephotography.com/), which serves mostly foreign clients, to survive in Zhang Garden — an old community near Nanjing West Road with more than a century of history — for over ten years without incident.

![The entrance to Zhang Garden at the end of Taixing Road. Image via [Er Geng](http://chuansong.me/n/1554895152123)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509125746v8pU9dwx2MiFczZI.jpeg-w600)

The entrance to Zhang Garden hides at the end of Taixing Road, which splits Wujiang Road in two, only a few hundred meters from Nanjing West Road, the main street of the shopping district. If you’ve never been before, you may find it confusing today: several restaurants flank the main gate, but inside it still looks like a residential compound.

Zhang Garden used to be quite glamorous. Before it became the lilong (lane) housing it is now, it was home to Arcadia Hall, the tallest building on the Puxi side of the river before the appearance of the Sassoon House on the Bund, and inside it was an entertainment complex predating the Great World. Shanghai’s first set of electric lights, its first photo studio, its first manned balloon show, and its first film screening all debuted here.

Now that Dazhongli has been torn down, Zhang Garden is one of the few places near Nanjing West Road where you can still see a complete Western-style community layout from 1920s Shanghai. The lavish specifications of some of its houses are almost impossible to find anywhere else. In Wang Gangfeng’s words, “only people with more money than they could ever spend would design a place like this.”

But preserved from the Republican era to the present, it has also become the most typical example of Shanghai’s old-style “seventy-two households under one roof” crowding: the standalone mansion at No. 41, originally designed for the family of the Mobil comprador stationed in Shanghai, now houses 46 families. Many elderly residents, unwilling to squeeze into tiny rooms, prefer to spend their days at the community service center built around a courtyard, or eating lunch in the spacious “Zhang family great hall.”

![Inside Zhang Garden](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705091258467KJTFYuq38d1trsj.jpeg-w600)

Starting in 2013, the Jing’an district government also began treating the area as a potential tourist attraction and commercial project near the shopping district. Reportedly, the project company involved has assembled a total of 6 billion yuan from investors including state-owned capital, private enterprises, and individuals. But the development plan has remained unsettled for years, seemingly hesitant. So far only one old house near the Taixing Road entrance has been renovated; it was named “Zhang Garden 99” and now houses several restaurants.

Then, just before the 2017 Lunar New Year, notices appeared like a gust of wind at every lane entrance of Zhang Garden: “To advance Phase II, household survey teams will soon visit to compile reports.” It seemed to push things forward in a solid, substantial step. At the time, Wang Gangfeng felt that nearly everyone in the community was “smiling despite themselves”: a sanitation worker who held usage rights to his unit calculated aloud to Wang that he could get more than 5 million yuan; and the landlord of Wang’s studio had cut off all contact since late April, not even bothering to collect the tens of thousands of yuan in annual rent, “as if just waiting for the relocation.”

Most people still living there were eagerly awaiting this once-in-a-lifetime chance to escape poverty and get rich quickly. Since 2002, Jing’an district’s compensation standard for relocating residents from dilapidated housing had already stood at over 10,000 yuan per square meter, and housing prices in the surrounding blocks have since multiplied more than tenfold.

HKR International, which in late 2002 invested HK$1.306 billion hoping to build residential towers on the Dazhongli site, stumbled over exactly those high relocation costs. In 2006, HKR International sold a 50% stake in the Dazhongli project to Swire Properties; the two sides agreed to turn the site into a commercial complex to be held and operated over the long term, and later added an 8 billion yuan bank loan before the demolition work could proceed smoothly.

A former Dazhongli project department employee who asked not to be named said that during the year or two she worked there, “because construction hadn’t started, there was almost nothing to do day to day. But every week we had meetings with the government and the subdistrict to work on demolition and stability-maintenance issues.”

But Shu Haolun believes Zhang Garden’s situation today is still much better than Dazhongli’s. When Zhang Garden was built in the 1920s, it already had relatively modern sanitary facilities such as bathtubs and flush toilets, whereas Dazhongli never got its chance at government-led renovation before relocation.

![The communal kitchen in Dazhongli, with an individual water meter on every tap](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509130558pPT1cSMsJ8ge6b4j.jpeg-w600)

When it was first built, Dazhongli housed senior employees of Shanghai’s foreign trading houses; rent was paid in gold bars and residents came and went by car. But nowadays only ayis and manual workers — who can’t afford high rents yet must stay close to the downtown restaurants and companies where they work — will accept such living conditions.

These newcomers occupy the homes left behind by the first wave of Dazhongli departures. If they don’t keep to themselves, they have to learn to get along with the elderly who had lived there for decades, and with middle-aged people down on their luck who had already come to terms with their lot. And the news of the demolition, back then, cast a shadow of one degree or another over these original residents’ moods.

Shu’s grandmother was already in her nineties and, attached to her native soil, deeply reluctant to move. Every day she still played a few rounds of mahjong with her neighbors, then tallied the wins and losses on sheets torn from an old-style vertically printed letter pad. Among her mahjong partners was Uncle Wei, already over sixty, who had been at peace with life — telling the camera, “I run ten kilometers a day; others earn 10,000 yuan a month but aren’t necessarily as happy as I am” — but soon he, too, began to worry: the apartment allotted by the government was in Taopu Ninth Estate, two subway stops past places like Nanxiang — “the suburbs” — and moving there meant cutting himself off entirely from “downtown” life.

![A mahjong game among Dazhongli residents](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705091259153MWyUFdg9TCfaJve.jpeg-w600)

According to the relocation documents at the time, former Dazhongli residents were resettled in batches into subsidized housing across Jing’an, Baoshan, Jiading, Putuo, Minhang, Pudong, and other districts. The older residents who signed first got some perks and then turned around to become persuaders, and the demolition work picked up pace.

By the time Shu returned in 2008 to film some final-phase demolition footage as material, fewer than 20% of Dazhongli’s houses were still standing. The whole construction site looked “like it had been bombed.” Only the mailman insisted on wobbling through the ruins on his bicycle, delivering letters to the few remaining households.

“The scene was surreal. My friend Ma Liang, who was with me at the time, saw it too, and he used it as creative inspiration — he found someone to play the mailman and made a work of his own.”

![Ma Liang’s photographic work *The Postman* (《邮差》), based on the story of the Dazhongli mailman. Ma Liang, a Shanghai contemporary artist and the first Chinese photographer to win the gold medal at the World Black and White Photography Awards, also works as an art director for commercials and a short-film director alongside his independent art practice. Image via [Fotomen](http://fotomen.cn/2009/04/postman/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509142956ahlZre9ymMfd0Np8.jpg-w600)

By then, Dazhongli had slowly been surrounded by the office towers rising around it. As for what it would become, everyone was guessing at the time: HKR International’s line was that it would become one of Shanghai’s five most expensive top-tier luxury residential areas, rivaling the projects around New York’s Central Park in price and specs; the manager of the neighboring Four Seasons Hotel said it would be remade in the style of Xintiandi, which foreign guests loved most for its old-fashioned scenery; and after Swire Properties came on board, the story shifted again — it would become the kind of top-end mall they had built in Hong Kong…

Shu had no interest in any of these ideas. He now lives in Xujiahui and works in Baoshan. He rarely goes out shopping; whenever he returns to the Dazhongli area, he prefers to sit for a while at the “Chabrol Café” run by a friend inside Jing’an Villas. And since the 2013 joint law-enforcement crackdown, Chabrol has been one of the few remaining shops in Jing’an Villas still operating normally.

![Shu Haolun while filming *Nostalgia*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509104750nxWHAXGau8bBJ1yY.jpg-w600)

He feels that too many original residents have been driven out of the area around Nanjing West Road by commercial projects, leaving it listless: gone are the cinemas, sports grounds, children’s bookstores, and children’s palaces of his childhood, gone are the grain shops and wet markets where parents would queue to do their daily shopping, and even gone is the messy vitality of Jing’an Villas a few years ago, when it was packed with small shops.

“This is something I really don’t understand — why the government and the developers never get it. Or rather, they don’t want to get it, or they pretend to. Look: the vitality of a shop inside a mall is different from the vitality of a place like Jing’an Villas that has never been developed as one big block. One big reason is that the latter is spontaneous and intertwined with the community. A mall doesn’t have that feeling, and it can’t quickly replicate a community’s memory, because a community takes time to form. And the people who experiment within a community simply can’t afford the mall’s rent or the risk of failure. If a shop knows it won’t move for 20 years, then I’ll plan my business model around 20 years. If you’re going to move me in 2 years, then I plan around 2 years — and there’s no way to make any big investment.”

Wang Gangfeng, by contrast, takes these changes in stride — even though the continued development of Zhang Garden is bound to smash his favorite “backdrop” of the past dozen years in Shanghai.

![Independent photographer Wang Gangfeng](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509130004BZ8jUOc7rLQvIbDF.jpeg-w600)

In 1989, using a solo exhibition abroad as his opportunity, he escaped the boring benchwork of a factory job and a life spent sharing an old house on North Maoming Road with a “Ningbo granny” and a “Suzhou grandpa.” He went to Canada to study commercial photography and obtained permanent residency. And when, several years later, he hauled all his belongings and photography equipment back home in 14 suitcases, he happened to run into the special needs of the first wave of foreign executives in the Nanjing West Road shopping district — beyond shooting commercials for their companies day to day, could he find a place with Shanghai character for some family portraits?

After experimenting with tourist spots like the Bund, the City God Temple, and the Jade Buddha Temple, he still liked taking his foreign clients to Dazhongli and Zhang Garden near Nanjing West Road. Meetings were usually set in the lobby of the Four Seasons Hotel, sparing clients from getting lost in the maze of lanes; clothing was supplied by the clients themselves, while props like bicycles, birdcages, and bamboo baskets were stashed at his studio inside Zhang Garden.

And after Dazhongli was demolished, he can now complete an entire professional family portrait session — several dozen photos — entirely within the familiar corners of Zhang Garden, throwing in a leisurely alleyway tour along the way:

Every lane has its own name, and some of the auspicious phrases carved into the lintels draw allusions from *The Analects* that ordinary people can’t even recognize; No. 2, Lane 106 was the private property of Zhou Qingyun, the majority shareholder of the old Taiping Shipping Company — after the 1949 Taiping steamer disaster, the mansion was besieged by two to three hundred family members of the victims demanding compensation, and Zhou ultimately had to give away his entire fortune and leave “with nothing but the clothes on his back”; China had its own so-called baby boom, when splitting rooms with curtains and building loft beds were all the rage; and if you want an overhead view of Zhang Garden, don’t take the main road or the big balcony — the best way is to climb the small service staircase on the side of Building No. 41, step by step, up to the fourth floor…

![A panoramic view of Zhang Garden](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509130143AyqmenJjHDrI60TW.jpeg-w600)

Now you, too, can find this [“wild tour guide” program](http://www.fourseasons.com/zh/shanghai/services_and_amenities/) led by Wang Gangfeng on the Four Seasons Hotel’s official website. Laced with large amounts of historical material and discussion of Shanghai’s current social conditions, the full walk through Zhang Garden takes three hours, and what started as free tours to drum up clients has become a paid offering — a business in its own right.

Whether for photos or tours, most of these clients — foreign executives — come on word of mouth. They have worked in Shanghai for years yet know nothing about local residents’ lives. They usually live with their families in international communities in Jinqiao, Pudong, with cars for the daily commute and kids in international schools; but during a Zhang Garden photo tour, if it happens to be lunchtime, getting to eat a few stir-fried fava beans — a home-style dish — with Wang’s help makes them enormously happy.

“For a cultured, well-educated person arriving in a new place, you certainly want to understand how people live there — not to see how pretty it is; they’ve seen the Bund already — but to come here, behind the pretty streets, and see real life.”

But what rankled him was not entirely the foreign visitors peering at Zhang Garden locals like a sideshow. It was the time he took high school classmates who had once lived nearby on a tour of Zhang Garden and recounted the same vivid historical anecdotes, only to be met with the indifference on their faces. “I felt there was no reaction on their faces — it was like looking at a blank white wall.”

![A rendering from the development plan for the Zhang Garden project. Image via [Davost](http://www.davost.com/case/detail/3737-0c6ebdd794.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170509105307azw82dvYNtAsF3qm.jpg-w600)

According to the general manager of the Zhang Garden company, in the future the ordinary, undistinguished houses here will be demolished; the valuable mansions will be merged together on rails, the way the old Minli Middle School assembly hall was. As for Gonghui Hospital, which locals consider great value for money and where they queue every day to fill prescriptions and get IV drips, it too will be demolished — only the one building within the hospital designed by the famed Hungarian architect Hudec will be preserved.

“The one thing they haven’t figured out yet is how many residents to keep here, to maintain something like the traces of everyday human life as before. Because take Xintiandi, which foreigners think is so great — imagine if there were really a residential community in it, think how chaotic it would be. And the chaos and vitality of Tianzifang come precisely from having kept all of its original residents. This is the new problem they have to tackle,” Wang said.

To sell the project to the young people who pass through the Nanjing West Road shopping district and occasionally walk past Zhang Garden’s gate on Wujiang Road, the government and the project company have spent years on preparatory work — which includes blending together the history of Zhang Garden across every era and blurring the background of its origins as a product of Shanghai’s pre-liberation “concession era.”

“The old things become a symbol, a consumer product, waiting to be grafted onto somewhere. Those newly built things were actually worthless to begin with; grafted onto the old, they gain value. The content inside has been hollowed out. The essence of culture is about people. If the people are gone, there is no value left,” Shu said.

Shu Haolun wants to carry out a longer-term project. In the manner of a TV series or a film, “take Dazhongli’s entire history from 1925 to 2008 as an index through which to view Shanghai’s whole development process.” In his view, there is no other place he knows better, or where he feels more strongly the “merging with the course of the entire nation and its people.”
