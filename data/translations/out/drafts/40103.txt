In fact, the participants of the theater festival are themselves wrestling with one question: how to effectively distinguish "self-indulgent fun" from "creation."

At 3 p.m. on April 14, the "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" began their performance on the garbage heap in Moujiayuan Village.

Shirtless, Li Ning slammed a four-meter wooden stake hard into the ground. The performers beside him beat with all their might at abandoned fuel tanks, scattered steel frames, and rust-eaten trash bins using branches, as if playing some kind of instrument.

![Li Ning stands in the middle holding the wooden stake, with performer Chang Jiang to his right and A Wen to his left.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424214120OFRUIq1gjcyvksVZ.jpg-w600)

They ate chicken and gnawed on wood atop the garbage heap, while the villagers watching on furrowed their brows, their faces full of bewilderment.

The second Rural Theater Festival of Weifang, Shandong, was underway.

As one of the very few theater festivals in China staged in a purely rural setting, its birth was entirely accidental.

In April 2016, the pear blossoms at the home of Mou Changfei, a Moujiayuan villager, were in full bloom. He suddenly had the initial idea of inviting a few friends to perform opera in the pear orchard. But on second thought, audiences who enjoy traditional opera tend to be elderly; young people like himself preferred more playful theater. So Mou Changfei began mulling how to bring urban theater into the village. Over lunch that same day, he posted a few hundred characters on WeChat, inviting contemporary theater performers with ideas to come to the village and create. That afternoon, he received a registration call from Li Ning.

Li Ning comes from Jinan. His "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" is devoted to creating experimental theater and has performed at the Beijing Youth Theater Festival, the Wuzhen Theater Festival, and the Avignon Festival in France, earning a modest reputation in China's theater circles. He spent his childhood before the age of seven in the Weifang countryside with his grandmother, so the moment he saw Mou Changfei's call for participants he signed up, hoping to recapture a feeling of coming home.

At first, Mou Changfei positioned the Rural Theater Festival as a self-amusement event, expecting maybe 10 friends to come and have some fun. He never imagined it would end up inviting performing guests from Beijing, Shanghai, Zhucheng, Shenyang and elsewhere, bringing the village plays, documentaries, music and dance, Shandong Maoqiang opera, and even body workshops — and so the first Rural Theater Festival came together just like that.

![Mou Changfei sits on a motorized tricycle watching a performance.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424213543JhvyAZMkNmpWgXV5.jpeg-w600)

In Mou Changfei's vision, the festival runs once a year, with two editions timed to spring sowing and autumn harvest. Last spring's first edition, themed "Flowers," built word of mouth among the performers' WeChat Moments; when the autumn edition themed "Weeds" was promoted in October, it immediately drew a dozen or so performing troupes and attracted the attention of media outlets including People's Daily and The Paper.

This spring was already Mou Changfei's third Rural Theater Festival. Over the three days from the 14th to the 16th, the edition themed "Sprout" brought more than 100 performers and audience members from across the country to this obscure little Shandong village.

It was Li Ning's third visit. He says he is not interested in people, only in objects, which is why the "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" focuses mostly on physical theater. They specialize in site-specific performance; for this festival they deliberately arrived 10 days early to live and create in the village, using local materials and designing the show around Moujiayuan Village's natural environment.

The piece they brought is called *My Land, My Body: Rough Reality DJ*《吾土我身之糙现实 DJ》, billed as their "most brutal, roughest, most explosive, and dirtiest" work in years.

![The "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" performing.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424201759rltiI5xXBJ1CE97p.jpg-w600)

Performer Chang Jiang said the dump was chosen because the afternoon sunlight gives it a kind of battered beauty. But in fact, rehearsing was no picnic: the actors had to roll around on the garbage heap, spiders kept jumping onto their shoulders, and they could only flick them off gently.

For a better effect, director Li Ning also arranged scenes of eating chicken and gnawing wood to match the setting, stimulating the sense of taste to create contrast. In addition, they dug a pit in a sandy patch nearby, propped up a hollow chamber with bricks around it, laid two large stones on top, then covered them with soil — creating a man-made acoustic chamber underground, so that when the wooden stake struck the ground, it produced a reverberating resonance.

According to Chang Jiang, the "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" perform without a script; the content often emerges from mutual inspiration during practice and is assembled mainly by joining key segments. "For example, when we work on the sound piece, one actor is practicing and another will unconsciously join in, and it gets really, really fun. When we reach a consensus, a lot of things come out of it. If the director thinks a section works, he keeps it and assigns the performers their positions." In addition, their shows feature few lines and little music; the sound often comes from the actors beating their own bodies.

Novel as such performances are, for the villagers watching, it is not easy to understand what on earth they are doing. Chang Jiang admits: "Words fall short — there's no way to articulate our performance."

![The "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" mid-performance.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424214511Iv6AONknh9S73u4t.jpg-w600)

Li Ning believes that, in many cases, art can help people rediscover the value of objects from another angle, and that audiences should trust their own intelligence and not lose confidence. "What you see is what it is. Our physical theater work just builds you a ladder; whether you climb up and grab an apple or grab a peach, whatever taste you savor is up to you. This is an equal era — there's no need to instill anything or educate anyone. That's the character of contemporary art."

Lang Jianfei, attending the Rural Theater Festival for the first time, holds a similar view of contemporary theater: "Don't categorize contemporary art. Categorization always comes after the thing is made."

![Lang Jianfei, photo from Baidu.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424225357auyQmOLcC421plI8.jpeg-w600)

Lang Jianfei is an instructor with the art troupe of South-Central Minzu University. His "Jianghu Theater Troupe," founded in 2009, currently has more than 40 amateur actors, all of whom work, study, or live in Wuhan. They describe themselves as "respecting tradition without being bound by it, eager to interpret human beings and the world in a more contemporary way." This time, Lang Jianfei included, five members came to the Rural Theater Festival.

On their first day, they strolled around the villages near Moujiayuan to get familiar with the rural environment — but this overturned all the performance content they had earlier imagined from photographs, because "a 360-degree live site is different."

Lang Jianfei said he could plainly feel how few young people there were in the village. "'Sprout' means everything budding, everything growing. The grass and trees are growing — but of the people who grow up here, how many stay in the countryside?"

So their actors dressed in black, dragging long bolts of red silk, and performed *Fence on the Country Lane*《陌上篱》 in Mou Changfei's family pear orchard, endlessly chanting singer Chen Jiannian's "How High Is the Mountain":

> The mountain high / the road long
> A bend of flowing water / wildflowers' scent
> The mountain high / the road long
> With me walking beside you, you are not alone

However, one audience member told Lang Jianfei that it looked a bit like big red bats flying.

![The "Jianghu Theater Troupe" performing *Fence on the Country Lane* in Mou Changfei's family pear orchard.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424201438tgLKwq3nHEDyYG6b.jpg-w600)

Lang Jianfei told QDaily that the red actually represents China's farmland, its culture, and its bloodline. Lang Jianfei said they had originally planned to borrow costumes from the villagers and blend in with them, only to find that villager clothing was meaningless in the piece, "because it can't stand out — it would easily be swallowed up by the environment. Red cloth works better."

During the performance of *Fence on the Country Lane*, a villager parked an electric scooter inside the performance area. Lang Jianfei called out for the owner for ages with no response, so he pushed the scooter out himself — but his left foot got scraped, and the nail on his big toe immediately flipped up. He jogged around in the dirt, decided it was fine, and straight away joined the next piece, *Like Grass and Trees*《将如草木》.

At the opening, Lang Jianfei was still plowing through the wheat field, but mid-performance he found the actors of the "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" rushing in. The others kicked up clouds of sand, their movements forceful; Lang Jianfei, even as he began thinking about what rhythm and positioning to use to compose the scene together, tumbled through the earth along with everyone.

![The "Jianghu Theater Troupe" performing *Like Grass and Trees*.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424225752SypXEtadoI4Cf2x3.jpeg-w600)

![The "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas" joining the performance of *Like Grass and Trees*.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424204526J1CMkvO5ViEQKl3c.jpeg-w600)

By the time the show ended, his toenail was standing fully upright.

But two female students who study "applied theater" and "Chinese drama" had come all the way to Moujiayuan Village to observe the performances, and they said they did not like watching the actors rolling in the dirt and pinching each other. "It feels like their self-indulgent fun. And you come to the countryside to perform for the folks, and the folks are completely baffled. From now on they may think: oh, so this is what theater is — if you can't understand it, it's theater. But that's obviously not the case."

![On day three of the festival, dancers from the Qianlie Modern Dance Troupe perform in the wheat field.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704242308562egQrPNZ0bJqioO6.jpeg-w600)

"Who comes to watch them perform in this village? It's not a big city; the atmosphere is different, the level is different. Maybe they pour their hearts into performing and expressing themselves here, but many of the villagers just don't understand them." Zhuang Xiuhua, Li Ning's high school classmate, came from downtown Weifang to watch the shows. Working in hydrology, she harbored similar worries: "After they put in this kind of effort, they themselves get something out of it emotionally, but many people simply feel nothing."

But Wang Yongsheng, a painter from Songzhuang, disagreed, applauding the actors: "Their reflexes are quick, and the creativity of young people really impresses me."

Zhang Xiaoting, a photography enthusiast from Weifang, thought the Rural Theater Festival was well done: "It livens up rural life. At the very least it cultivates the mind and leaves body and soul content." Zhang Xiaoting watched the performance of the Zhangqiu Bangzi Opera Troupe from Jinan together with more than 20 friends from the university for seniors, and took quite a few photos.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424230400HvrANlYcKFtLygom.jpg-w600)

![Actors of the Zhangqiu Bangzi Opera Troupe from Jinan.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424213059o4fTRjOMBIYc8ts5.jpg-w600)

"Snail" from Zhejiang stayed at the festival for three days and particularly loved the way performances were staged here: "Once you step out of the theater, the natural light is wonderful. Here you can see the soil and the needle tips of the wheat. In this most ordinary of ordinary villages, theater is happening every moment." "Snail" owns a bayberry orchard in Taizhou and also organizes artsy events.

![Zhou Qing of Beijing's Shourui Theater Society dances ballet in the pear orchard.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424234622mQl5npYHUEZeq18T.jpg-w600)

Mou Changfei's uncle Mou Guohua said the festival was a good thing: "At least it lets people know what there is in this place called Moujiayuan. Everyone comes and sees the old houses, the pear trees, the cherries, the greenhouse vegetables, the fields — this is the land of northern Weifang."

But he also pointed out that villagers not understanding what they hear and see is a real problem. "A troupe came last year — the moment it came to rolling on the ground, everyone got it, but when it came to what opera they were singing, nobody understood." Mou Guohua said that although the festival includes some traditional programs — last year's nuo opera from Hunan, for instance — dialect differences make them hard to follow. And nowadays, villagers over 75 prefer Peking opera, while those slightly younger favor the model operas of the 1960s and 70s; mobile phones and television also compete for everyone's attention.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704242304538mNI5Q0GjZbwhTqz.jpg-w600)

![Villagers watching the performances.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424214008lwAFLMufHkE1YQox.jpg-w600)

"But whatever you do these days, there will be opponents," Mou Guohua added.

"Changfei has done a good deed for the old folks," said a Mr. Sun, a general branch Party secretary in Gaoli Town, who watched this year's festival. However, he had only been put in charge of this community less than a week earlier and did not know the specifics.

According to him, the government has no plans or programs for the festival's future development; it remains a purely privately organized theatrical event.

A Mr. Du, an official in the Hanting District propaganda department, knows more about the festival's progress. He watched the ballet danced by Zhou Qing, an actor with Beijing's Shourui Theater Society, in Mou Changfei's family pear orchard, and noticed that few local villagers came to watch. He suggested the festival could be divided into multiple sections to meet the viewing needs of different age groups. "If you only stage modern, avant-garde stuff here, people can't accept it. I'm thinking — is it basically actors watching actors perform? Where are the ordinary people watching the actors?" He also told QDaily that, to welcome the festival, the village had given an extra cleanup to spots that were usually poorly swept.

![A scene in Moujiayuan Village.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424205021gchFMElf8KWSbiUR.jpg-w600)

Yet it was precisely this cleanup that led to the dismantling of a vegetable trellis a old lady at the village entrance had put up, which left Mou Changfei feeling "as if he'd swallowed a fly." He quickly apologized to her and gave her 100 yuan.

Mou Changfei said his bottom line is not getting cursed out by ordinary people.

And Li Ning's "Lingyunyan Physical Guerrillas," having failed to communicate in time before the show, occupied a sandy patch next to the garbage dump, so the landowner splashed pig manure over the performance area — and Mou Changfei spent another 100 yuan making amends.

"Ordinary people aren't greedy for money. In her world, a single scallion or a single clove of garlic matters," Mou Changfei later reflected. "We can't hold everyone hostage because of the theater festival, demanding that everyone support whatever we do. The festival still has to stay grounded in the village; it's not that whatever they can't understand is somehow lofty. That's not how it is."

Mou Changfei, 30, grew up in Moujiayuan Village. In 2009 he went to Yantai Nanshan University to study fashion design, then spent two years in Beijing's Liulichang learning calligraphy-and-painting mounting. After returning to Weifang, he spent three years shouldering a gun as a bank escort, then quit to work for two years at the Guo Weiqu Art Museum. Now he runs his own "Jianshan Studio," doing calligraphy, seal carving, and brush writing, and has settled in downtown Weifang.

But Mou Changfei says his understanding of the world, including the formation of his values, has been profoundly shaped by this small patch of land, Moujiayuan Village, which often leaves him, living in the city, troubled by a feeling of being "pulled in two directions," of being "torn." He has been constantly searching for "a way back to the countryside."

While in Beijing, he once visited Zou Xueping's studio. In 2009, Zou Xueping, fresh out of the New Media Department of the China Academy of Art, joined the "Folk Memory Project" in Caochangdi. The project asks participants to take video cameras back to their own villages and listen to old people recount the history of the "three years of hunger." Zou Xueping thus made the first documentary about the home village, Zoujia Village in Yangxin County, Shandong — *A Hungry Village*《饥饿的村子》.

Mou Changfei thought such oral history was not difficult and that he could do it too. In 2015, he took a DV camera back to the village and interviewed more than 100 old people, listening to their past histories. The experience left him marveling that even the most ordinary old villagers had once had their high-spirited youths, had once tried to break free of the shackles of life, but were ultimately swept up in the currents of a great era and rendered obscure.

In less than three years, a third of the old people he had filmed for the oral histories had passed away. Mou Changfei felt the problem of rural out-migration suddenly become urgent, and he gradually shifted the focus of his attention to rural construction. "I've followed many domestic and international cases of community building and rural construction — the Bishan Project, the Wuzhen Theater Festival, Japan's Toga Festival, and the Echigo-Tsumari Art Triennale. But those places have natural advantages to begin with; they can be built up slowly. What about a most ordinary village like Moujiayuan?"

![Mou Changfei plays program announcements over the village broadcasting system.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170424212600apK0W1t3CLnHRdMy.jpeg-w600)

Mou Changfei wanted to use the festival as an "experiment" to see whether an obscure northern village could develop through theater.

From time to time he flips through Xiong Foxi's *The Experiment in Popularizing Drama*《戏剧大众化实验》.

In the 1930s, the National Association of Mass Education Movements led by James Yen launched a rural reconstruction movement with Ding County in Hebei as its pilot site, covering literacy, economics, health, and the arts. Xiong Foxi was invited to lead some teachers and students from the drama department of China Art College to Ding County for a five-year experiment in popularizing drama. They had the villagers build their own theater and write their own plays to perform, and also taught locals to grow cotton, decorate their homes, and resolve family disputes.

"Although the drama experiment of that era ended in failure when the war of resistance broke out, it is highly instructive for us," Mou Changfei said. "Many of China's big problems are decided by the countryside. What we're doing now still falls far short — it needs to be more grounded, more concrete."

In Moujiayuan Village, farmers' income depends mainly on wheat and corn; beyond that there are greenhouses, orchards, poultry, and migrant work. Through introducing theater, Mou Changfei wants to gradually help the village with things like school education, house renovation, and the teaching of agricultural techniques.

But thinking is one thing — he has no source of funding. The two festivals last year were sponsored by a local Weifang culture and communications company; the first year cost just over 10,000 yuan, the second around 130,000 yuan. This year, the sponsor's funds fell through at the last minute, and performers who had already bought their train tickets had to spend all three meals at Mou Changfei's house, eating free flatbread rolled with eggs and pancakes with xiaodoufu.

On the third day of the festival, a rose estate in Weifang approached Mou Changfei about collaborating, but Mou Changfei told QDaily, "They're struggling too. Artsy young people can't save other artsy young people."
