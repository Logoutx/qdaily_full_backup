China's streetwear scene is far livelier than it was ten years ago. But trendiness itself is only a signifier — it is not the same thing as youth culture.

At Shanghai Fashion Week, the showrooms that serve as buying events are searching for their own positioning. Tube showroom takes a "small but beautiful" avant-garde art route; the main venue, mode, builds professional authority through industry talks; and Ontimeshow plans to go the youth-culture route — rappers, DJs, runway shows with "non-professional models," and streetwear installations have all been moved to the West Bund Art Center in Shanghai.

On the last day of Ontimeshow, a conversation about youth culture was held. Li Chen, founder of NPC; independent designer Shangguan Zhe; Su Wukou; Adam Chen, editor-in-chief of i-D China; and Wu Meng, vice president of gaming company Giant Network, talked about the past, future, and present of Chinese youth culture.

Does China really have youth culture? If you define it as subcultures like hip-hop, punk, and rock, it has no solid foundation in China — many people know trends without understanding culture, and many understand culture without ever having truly felt or experienced it. But what cannot be denied is that China has its own youth culture. It might be the not-so-cool gaokao culture that Shangguan Zhe described; it might be "Heijie," the youth organization in QQ among a younger generation that [VICE once covered](http://www.vice.cn/read/bad-boys-are-good-guy); it might be the danmu (bullet-screen) culture of online video. None of these, however, has become mainstream trendiness.

The people in today's conversation are all post-80s; none of them count as truly young anymore. But each is leading China's youth trends in his own field — so let's hear how a game maker, a streetwear entrepreneur, and a designer look at youth culture.

### How are today's young people different from young people in the past?

Li Chen became interested in youth culture at a very young age; back then he would buy out-of-season Japanese magazines, and there weren't many places or people to exchange ideas with. He realized he was beginning to influence young people when he started working as a Channel V host. And today, "young people's idols have changed, the way young people chase stars has changed, and the way young people learn about the world has changed."

"I've been running NPC for eight years now, and I believe our entire consumer base has changed. The people who liked our brand in our store back then have gone through a complete change in social identity, status, income, habits, and dress. And now a whole new generation of consumers is arriving," Li Chen said.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704112308140DBd5FfwlOJAgHX2.jpg-w600)

Shangguan Zhe, a "late bloomer," only embarked on the path of fashion design after graduating from university, and step by step from zero he has been at it for nearly a decade. His biggest takeaway is that young people today know very early on what they want. "For example, I remember that when I was fairly young, I only figured out what I wanted to do after finishing high school or college; before that, the script of your life was basically an imitation of your father's generation or of whatever you could see. But I think a lot of the younger, earlier generation now know in middle school or high school that they want to make music or do design — that they don't want to waste their time. I think that's actually a pretty cool thing," Shangguan Zhe said.

![Shangguan Zhe at the Ontimeshow 2017 autumn/winter exhibition](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170411230835ydIKDONquUex1X5f.jpg-w600)

Adam Chen thinks this is because young people today have seen more, so they naturally question authority: they scale the Great Firewall, discuss things with others, and look things up online themselves — unlike before, when all they could do was read magazines like milk and assume "whatever's in it must be good."

Su Wukou, in his baseball cap and baseball jersey, looks less "flamboyant" than the things he does: he made a Fake series that plagiarizes various brands — and builds the act of plagiarism itself into the clothes. He collaborated with Shangguan Zhe on a "Non-Cooperation Series" shown at Xintiandi, and hung a piece from his own brand in a Uniqlo store in Guangzhou — a white one with the words "please steal this cloth" written on it.

Talking about youth culture, Su Wukou said his biggest impression was that "they like to say no, they like to resist." "And then I thought seriously about why say no, why resist, and found it quite interesting. In the days of dynasties and emperors, saying no back then was revolution. If you look at it from a higher vantage point, it's a process of renewal and iteration — a single word that condenses the force driving an entire civilization forward. From a biological perspective, it's simply evolution... So from that angle, there's no point asking how it was in the past, is now, or will be in the future. I've been trying hard to figure out what it is, and just when I feel I'm about to know, it turns into something else."

Wu Meng, coming at it from outside the fashion industry, offered an outsider's perspective. He feels young people today are quicker to ditch the old for the new; unlike when he was a kid, spending a long time studying sneakers and saving up for ages to buy a pair. "We used to be loyal to many things. Now information changes too fast — no one can stay focused, and no one can stay unchanged toward you for 100 years." And young people today are actually very lonely inside, which is why they like sharing and hanging out with others online.

### With an endless stream of new styles and hit products, fashion has become overabundant. What do independent designers make of it?

Su Wukou: The surface form keeps changing, because what people affirm is the form. Once you become genuinely interested in the inner substance of something, you find it may share common ground with other things — it turns out the value of being a doctor and making art is about the same. Once you discover that logic, you can settle your mind and really look at something; if you keep chasing the surface, which moves very fast, you're forever running after it, and you get lost and restless.

At first I thought design was about learning software — that once I'd learned it all I'd be invincible. But the moment I finished learning, I felt software didn't cut it; it was just a form. So I immediately trained myself in drawing, and when I'd taken drawing as far as it goes I felt it was basically the same, so I went looking for the next thing. Then I came across architecture, which gave me a tremendous amount — a wall, a brick: there's so much to explore inside it. I found that fascinating.

Then when I got into architecture I found I really disliked the industry, but I was very interested in making concepts, so I wondered whether I could graft that kind of concept directly onto clothing. ...So my whole process has been one of continually shedding what I called that surface form of myself.

Of course I still have some of it on me — my own "Non-Cooperation Series" is itself a formula, a gimmick: make the clothes and they sell. So I'm still inside it. But this slow process of shedding form is fascinating. Whenever I reach the next stage and feel that what I made before isn't good anymore, I'm actually happier. Saying my own work isn't good like this — it won't sell.

![Shangguan Zhe and Su Wukou's Non-Cooperation Series](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704112309559HROTJL1j7gh5NDS.jpg-w600)

Shangguan Zhe: The internet's disruption of culture has given different young people the ability to make their own voices heard. Music is the same — it used to be elites who created it, and now every individual can publish work online. So at that level, I think youth culture within pop culture actually has nothing to do with creativity anymore. I think most of it is very boring stuff with no real meaning; it has nothing to do with creativity, design, or art — it's purely a matter of influence.

So for me, when I started SANQUANZ and did the first few collections, I was focused on the youth-culture direction, including subcultures. But one day I suddenly found it all especially boring, especially tiring — I couldn't be moved anymore. I think it's mostly cloned stuff. Young people don't really need to appreciate good things; they may just need an outlet for their surplus time, and to meet some boys or girls through a brand or an event. It's the venting of hormones, so I think it has nothing to do with creativity.

Of course there are exceptions: within small-scale subcultures there can be very avant-garde things — music, design... but they definitely aren't mainstream; they're enjoyed by a relatively niche crowd. Still, I think those more niche things are pushing this world's development forward.

### If Vetements was a revolution

Adam Chen believes Vetements uses very cheap-to-make things to rebel against the established luxury industry. "I don't fully agree. I think it's not something practical, not an advance in craft, but something metaphysical."

Shangguan Zhe, meanwhile, believes concept is precisely the most important thing in fashion right now. "I think Vetements making the DHL T-shirt is really the idea of the contemporary artist Marcel Duchamp — 'the artist decides what is art.' At this point the fashion designer decides what is fashion. The DHL T-shirt is their work uniform, but because the context changed, inside Fashion Week it is fashion. It looks like a joke, but I think behind it is a very powerful move. And only they could do it — Zara and H&M couldn't pull this off. This is a very historic shift in fashion, because tradition didn't emphasize ideas; it emphasized 'my material is rare, my handcraft takes a huge amount of time.' But that concept of luxury is already being replaced.

For our time, the most luxurious part of fashion is human creativity itself — that through your ideas or creativity you can make cheap things full of life. So I think that's the core of youth culture — not that I just wear one very oversized piece of clothing."

But Demna Gvasalia himself may think exactly the opposite: compared with concept, he believes the product matters more. He has said he does "not try to push the boundaries of fashion," and added that "fashion should not be something you dream about." He decodes and reconstructs underground, street elements and operates them within the luxury-fashion system; hyped by the media and industry insiders, it became a revolution. As the editor-in-chief of Vestoj put it, this revolution's true name can only be "Vetements, the brand."

### Talk about some fundamental questions, do some small things

Adam Chen feels that within China's youth culture, "there is relatively little that sparks people's imagination." "For domestic designers, fashion itself is the root of subculture — it's not that music, film, or other art forms directly become your design inspiration."

But innovation can't be yanked up out of nothing. "When we talk about innovation, we first have to understand what our nation is before we can know what our young people are," Wu Meng said. "Innovating for innovation's sake puts the cart before the horse. Take my work in games: I probably need to ask three questions. First, why do people need to play games? Second, what games do people need to play? Third, how do I make the games they need? Ask the same questions of clothing: why do people need clothes, what clothes do they need, and how do you make the clothes they need?"

Wu Meng thinks about it with his typical merchant's logic: make whatever clothes match whatever demand exists, rather than cooking up an idea out of thin air in a studio — that is "being completely immersed in your own world, indulging in wishful thinking; nobody wants what you make, and it's probably all inventory."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170411231326TCg5OPdvUBaz2ftD.jpg-w600)

But when these three questions are put to independent designers, the answers diverge wildly. "Luxury goods were never really a functional thing to begin with — it's about whether they look good, and right now looking good has become a rigid demand," Su Wukou said. "Making clothes should be about good fabric and good craft — why don't we do that? Instead we play with ideas, all head-in-the-clouds. Actually, I'm weighing some of this from the market's angle: with other people already making it that way, how do you compete with them? Otherwise I might as well take a few steps in this direction myself..."

"The functionality of clothing in people's daily lives doesn't influence me much. I don't much care whether other people dress well, or whether they need my stuff," Shangguan Zhe said.

When will China finally have a trend culture that young people around the world love? Li Chen feels someone always has to walk out in front; even if it's small and immature right now, it has to be done. "Optimistically speaking, designers today are already excellent — we just lack a better platform and media environment for everyone to accept them. Foreign media are already paying attention," Li Chen told QDaily. "But maybe we're all just paving the road," he added.
