In every version of the fighting-prowess rankings, why do Northeasterners almost always come out on top? If "fighting prowess" is even a real thing, what does it depend on? And which part of China truly deserves the title of "fighting nation"?

Among China's "map cannons" — regional put-downs — the most widely circulated is the ranking of fighting prowess by region. The versions differ, but their overall conclusions are strikingly consistent: fighting prowess declines steadily from north to south, with Northeasterners at the top of the food chain and Shanghainese at the very bottom — though some particularly politically correct versions throw Taiwan into the mix as well, where Taiwanese score negative points.

This is probably the regional stereotype Chinese people find easiest to agree on; it even insidiously permeates mainstream discourse such as the CCTV Spring Festival Gala. But the question is: if China really had regionally differentiated fighting prowess, would this claim hold up?

## **Does Loving to Fight Equal Fighting Prowess?**

Almost every map cannon, when describing each region's fighting prowess, uses how quickly a dispute escalates into fisticuffs as the criterion. The faster a place moves to blows, the higher the score; the slower it is to start, the lower the score. Shanghainese get zero because when a dispute breaks out among them, it almost always stays at the level of a war of words.

This is obviously mistaking a lack of civility and manners for fighting prowess.

If that were the measure of fighting strength, then the Russians — a people known as a fighting nation — would rank somewhere below the Shanghainese on China's fighting leaderboard, since they can barely even manage a verbal spat over a trivial matter the way Shanghainese do. When something on the street annoys them, Russians typically show a mildness no worse than that of Taiwanese or Hong Kong people.

So these map cannons are entirely bait-and-switch. An exaggerated physical-conflict script like "What are you looking at? Yeah, what are you looking at? I'll beat you to a pulp..." — even if true — would only show that Northeasterners are more prone to fighting, which is in no way the same as being good fighters.

![**Cheer slogans at a stadium in Liaoning**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702115329TpHxA65bPuKlwnIv.jpg-w600)

That Shanghainese don't like to throw punches doesn't mean they are weak fighters; it simply means they are the group least willing to settle disputes with physical conflict. Overall, Shanghai and the surrounding Wu-speaking region are the most mild-mannered part of China — and that mild image has even been inflated to stand in for all southerners.

The reason may be simple: for a long time the Wu-speaking region was China's wealthiest and most developed in education and letters. Deep-rooted Confucian codes of propriety and a highly developed commercial culture both strongly suppress a brawling spirit and breed the habit of seeking third-party arbitration or judgment in disputes — if only to win the support of bystanders.

Shanghai's street-corner verbal disputes are essentially contests of who can argue their case most reasonably, not who can curse the best — even after two hours, a Shanghai-style dispute will never veer off into the other party's ancestors and the women of his family. That was a skill Northeasterners of the past were relatively good at.

Moreover, regions with a stronger traditional-society character usually have a stronger culture of honor than regions steeped in commercial culture. Malcolm Gladwell discusses in *Outliers* how the American South has a stronger sense of honor than the North, and southerners are more easily made to feel violated and enraged. The internet's exaggerated portrayal of the Northeastern temper applies just as well to the American South.

![**A prejudice map of the United States drawn by Bulgarian artist Yanko Tsvetkov**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201607021153301I7YQPi0H4o8w5Et.jpg-w600)

Shanghainese aversion to physical conflict is even more pronounced for another reason: Shanghai was China's first modern city and has consistently been the country's most densely populated city; today it is also the city where the Chinese government administers most capably and efficiently (see Elephant Guild Society's "Why Does Shanghai Lack Entrepreneurs?"《上海为什么缺少创业家?》), which in itself means a greater likelihood of fair arbitration, while violence carries an extremely high cost.

It is easy to observe that the more economically developed a big city is, the more strangers who quarrel in it tend to win the sympathy of onlookers by each arguing their case, whereas people in small towns or the countryside are more inclined to display their gift for improvising a lurid history of the other party's family.

The regional fighting-prowess rankings circulating online are unreliable, but the average "fighting prowess" of different parts of China can actually be compared.

Judging which of two people can fight is easy: usually one look at their build tells you the outcome. Except for sumo, combat sports such as boxing, wrestling, taekwondo, and judo all divide competitors by weight; below heavyweight, a new division is carved out roughly every two to three kilograms.

![**A table of professional boxing weight classes**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702115330mywaAH8cNUTJ1EZz.jpg-w600)

There are no reliable nationwide height-and-weight statistics today from which a fighting-prowess ranking could be simply extrapolated. That said, northerners being physically bigger than southerners is beyond doubt. In the 19th century, the German biologist Carl Bergmann summarized a rule: within the same species, the colder the region, the larger the individual, because greater volume retains heat better — volume increases with the cube of linear dimensions, while surface area increases only with their square.

In terms of sheer size, putting Northeasterners at the top of China's fighting prowess is roughly right — but Shanghai would surely rank fairly high too, because in a China with enormous income disparities, where most people only recently solved the problem of putting food on the table, nutrition has a huge effect on height, and Shanghai is the city with the highest per-capita income in China, where nutrition is the last thing people lack.

But is it really only because Northeasterners are bigger and relatively more willing to fight that China today believes they are the strongest fighters?

Clearly not. The "fighting nation" impression Northeasterners have left across China actually owes a great deal to their illustrious record of martial exploits. Luo Yonghao once said: "Almost every major criminal case north of the Yangtze was committed by us Northeasterners." Northeasterners do indeed stand out in violent-crime cases in northern China, especially organized gang crime.

![**Qiao Si, a Harbin native and one of the leaders of the largest organized-crime syndicates in Harbin and all of Heilongjiang in the late 1980s**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702115331GOd9arLsD1MZ4xl8.jpg-w600)

The Northeast's propensity for violent gangs is usually explained away as "fierce folk customs" — and the logic seems self-consistent: present-day Northeastern society was formed by large-scale spontaneous migration in modern times, which, compared with orderly state-organized migration, more readily produces a jungle-law folk ethos in which whoever has the harder fists can grab more resources.

But being bellicose does not in itself explain the presence of violent gangs in nearly every part of China, and as we will discuss later, whether in bellicosity or in the habit of closing ranks against outsiders, Northeasterners are far from remarkable in China.

The structural feature of Northeastern society that most clearly sets it apart from other regions of China is perhaps the most important reason all of China came to believe "all Northeasterners are gangsters."

Strong trust and tacit understanding are the most important organizational bonds of a violent gang. Typically, the emotional bonds among a gang's core members come either from blood ties or from trust built up since childhood. Growing up together from a young age within a relatively closed social organization readily produces deep emotional attachments.

China's large factory and mining enterprises of the planned-economy era were closed, self-contained societies. The children of factory workers lived together in the same space from kindergarten onward; not only did they share the deepest collective memories, they also developed a strong internal cohesion — even more so in the island-like Third Front enterprises deep in China's interior.

In a factory enterprise with an extremely uniform age structure — where the workers are nearly all of the same generation and their children are too, unlike a society with a continuous age distribution — people raised in such an environment develop a stronger sense of community. At hormone-driven ages, factory-compound youth more readily band together against the outside world; if they fail to integrate into society smoothly as adults, violent gangs readily breed.

The Northeast is the region where China's large factory and mining enterprises were most concentrated. Unluckily, those who reached working age in the 1980s easily became "youths waiting for work," and those who reached working age in the 1990s were even unluckier, hitting the wave of layoffs from state-owned enterprise restructuring. The Northeast's highly undiversified economy left these surplus workers no choice but to leave the Northeast in search of opportunity.

![**The documentary *West of the Tracks* reflects the bitterness and helplessness of workers in factory-dense Northeast China after the massive restructuring of state-owned enterprises**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702115331sVpay7JfQWlCd5UI.jpg-w600)

The Northeast is probably the region with China's most peculiar pattern of population movement: other regions lose rural population, but the Northeast loses large numbers of urban residents. These people were naturally unwilling to take jobs in coastal factories or hauling cement on big-city construction sites the way migrant workers from the interior do.

In fact, the children of Third Front enterprises in the interior or of factory compounds surrounded by farmland band together and fight more than their local peers — perhaps even more strongly than in the Northeast — but unlike Northeasterners, they cannot be easily picked out by outsiders from their accents and customs and slapped with a regional label.

Taiwan may be the best reference point. Nearly every famous organized-crime group in Taiwan today grew out of the military dependents' villages, whose social form is almost a carbon copy of the mainland factory compounds. The reason China's own military compounds have not become a similar breeding ground for gangs has much to do with the young people there having far better development opportunities than their counterparts.

Are Northeasterners fierce?

In the Northeast after large-scale state-owned enterprise restructuring, today's social structure is no longer a hotbed for violent gangs. Though in Chinese people's minds Northeasterners remain a fighting nation, the younger urban generation is in fact a thoroughly mild one — the Northeast is the region with China's lowest fertility rate, and only-child Northeasterners are a completely different species from the legendary Northeasterners.

As for Northeasterners in the countryside, they may love to brag, and occasionally strike carnivore poses so as not to disappoint outsiders' admiration, but in fact they can be considered the most honest and docile farmers China has ever had.

If we search keywords like "mass incidents" and "armed clan fights," we find that the legendary fierce-hearted Northeast offers barely a few large-scale brawls — what fills the whole screen instead is South China, which sits at the bottom of the fighting rankings.

The following news events would be unimaginable in the Northeast:

The March 23 Incident in Dongfang, Hainan, in 2009: dissatisfied with how a schoolyard fight had been handled, villagers from two local villages smashed and burned down the township government and the police station within a single day. Afterward, the municipal government immediately sent more than 120 people in four work teams into the villages to conduct ideological education among the masses. But the next day the two villages erupted into a large-scale armed fight involving over a thousand people, until the police deployed a comparable contingent of regular officers and armed police, at which point the masses one after another declared their emotions stabilized.

The leaders of Dongfang City were fairly lucky, because what they faced was not the villagers of Liantang in Shantou, Guangdong. In July 2014, several gamblers in Liantang were caught by police; to pressure the station into releasing them, the villagers not only besieged and smashed the police station — two months later they also stormed the municipal party committee and beat the staff badly. Even more outrageous, they once held a duty officer hostage and paraded him through the streets for 13 hours, and put out word that they would burn him to death.

![**Shantou residents storm the municipal party committee office building**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702115332QAamrGFiY5h4WSqC.jpg-w600)

The Chinese Academy of Social Sciences' Institute of Law's *2014 Report on China's Rule-of-Law Development* 《2014年中国法治发展报告》 confirms southern strength: from 2000 to 2013, among mass incidents nationwide involving more than 100 people, South China accounted for 36.6%, with Guangdong alone taking first place at 267 incidents, or 30.7% of the total. By comparison, the three Northeastern provinces together had only 27 incidents — one tenth of Guangdong's.

Which part of China is the true "fighting nation"?

Guangdong, Fujian, and even southern Zhejiang — bottom of the various fighting rankings — are the real fighting nation, and have been for several hundred years.

## **The Power of the Clan**

Compared with the sensational ferocity of the South China countryside, the northern countryside looks much quieter, because it lacks a strong fighting tradition: armed clan fights.

Armed clan fights appeared as early as the Ming dynasty; by the Qing dynasty, the Zhangzhou–Quanzhou region of Fujian and the Chaoshan region of Guangdong had become battlefields of frequent armed fights. The causes of conflict were mostly trivial, but the fighting often dragged on for years, with a brutality far beyond an ordinary mass brawl. In 1907, for instance, villagers in Hui'an County, Fujian fought over gambling debts, drawing in dozens of villages of two large and small surname groups and lasting three years, with more than 160 deaths. In the late Guangxu period, Yutian and Dongdu villages in Changle County near Fuzhou fought over water sources for as long as four years.

The armed fights of the Zhangzhou and Quanzhou regions placed enormous stability-maintenance pressure on the Qing court. In 1734, the emperor specially issued an edict strictly prohibiting armed fights, which noted: "The two prefectures of Zhangzhou and Quanzhou... have throughout history supplied no small number of people who served the state with distinction. Only their fierce and unruly customs, known to all under heaven, are likewise despised by all under heaven."

![**Amid frequent armed clan fights and raids by bandits and wokou pirates, earthen-walled tulou architecture flourished in Longyan and Zhangzhou in Fujian and Chaozhou in Guangdong**](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160702115332kyU9qEbaoYuXZrve.jpg-w600)

In the Song dynasty, the lineage was only in embryo; by the Ming dynasty, out of the need for social control, the state combined lineage organizations with the lijia system as the foundation of local social order, and Neo-Confucian classics such as *Zhu Xi's Family Rituals* 《朱子家礼》 provided a complete set of guiding theory for lineage construction. Official and popular forces together gradually built a strict lineage system encompassing clan law, ancestral halls, lineage lands, lineage schools, and genealogies.

Northern China repeatedly suffered wars and saw several great population migrations in its history, while southern lineages maintained relatively stable bloodlines; hence southerners' clan identity is far stronger than northerners'. To this day, southern villages still contain many single-surname villages, while the vast majority of northern villages are mixed-surname communities.

Beyond stronger clan identity, the north-south difference in agricultural production is another reason armed clan fights appeared mainly in the south. North of the Yangtze the staple crop is wheat, which depends little on irrigation; apart from specific seasons, a single household can mostly farm on its own. Southern rice cultivation depends more on irrigation and demands more cooperation, so blood ties are further reinforced, and clans more readily come into conflict over water and farmland. Moreover, the southern coastal regions are far more densely populated than the north, making inter-clan competition for survival fiercer.

After centuries of brutal fighting, some places became so highly militarized that even regular armed forces were no match for them. In 1924, a fight broke out among the Huang, Yan, and Zhang clans in Anhai, Fujian; Kong Zhaotong, a Beiyang warlord stationed in Quanzhou, sent troops to suppress it, only to be met with armed resistance from the villagers, and 27 soldiers were killed.

After 1949, the CCP achieved tight control over rural grassroots society for the first time, and the lineage system collapsed. North of the Yangtze, clan identity had never been strong and never revived once the political environment loosened; but the powerful lineage culture of the south swiftly rose from the ashes after 1978. The frequency and intensity of armed clan fights quickly returned to Qing-dynasty levels, and the scale grew even larger — the fighting parties expanded from different clans to different surnames, and sometimes "friendly surnames" would be brought in as allies.

In many places, armed clan fights even evolved a sophisticated set of rules of engagement. The combatants would first swear a collective oath in the ancestral hall, then enter the battlefield in uniform dress, wielding every kind of edged and blunt weapon. The fighting ground was mostly confined to the field boundaries of the participating surnames, and combat ranged from open-field attacks to positional warfare holding fortified works — in some places the trenches dug as earthworks were up to three meters wide and two meters deep.

Regions with frequent armed clan fights all had complete systems of social mobilization and support: the war dead would be laid out in the ancestral hall as martyrs, with sacrifices offered before burial; their families, children, and those who had lost their ability to work in the fighting would be supported by the clan; and if key participants were later pursued by the police, other clansmen would voluntarily take the fall for them, even serving prison sentences in their place.

Because of the rule that "people of the same surname do not intermarry," the different clans and surnames involved in a fight are inevitably related by marriage. For this reason, the rules of armed fighting explicitly stipulated that everything yields to the clan's interest, and that relatives and acquaintances on the battlefield are to be beaten all the same. To prevent anyone from being unable to strike, many fights were scheduled at night, and some more cautious fighters even applied makeup before entering the field.

With such a powerful fighting culture behind them, northerners stand almost no chance against southerners in a genuine mass brawl. On "the neutral battlefield of Japan," organized-crime groups from Fujian not only left Northeastern gangs with no say at all — even Japan's native yakuza deferred to them.

Among southerners, who are the fighting nation of the fighting nation?

The most famous in history are probably the Hakka, whose militarily fortress-like architecture is the direct embodiment of their fighting-nation character. Even today, from the shape of the buildings in different Hakka settlements you can infer which was a forward base and which a small stepping-stone.

The Taiping Rebellion, which caused the largest population loss in Chinese history, had five Hakka among its six founders, and its core armed force was made up of Hakka. During the Taiping army's northern expedition, more than 20,000 Hakka fought all the way from Yangzhou to Zhili, while hundreds of thousands of Qing troops could do nothing against them.

In the same period, the Hakka also set the most horrific record in the history of armed clan fights: the fight between the Hakka and the local natives of Guangdong in the late Qing, during the Xianfeng and Tongzhi eras, spanned 13 years, covered 17 counties, and conservatively claimed more than a million lives.

Perhaps, however, because the casualties were so appalling, the Hakka have been inclined toward conservatism ever since, and rarely show aggression.

In Cantonese eyes, the most fiercely formidable people today are undoubtedly the Teochew (Chaoshan) people. But unlike Northeasterners, who like to tattoo reptiles on their bodies, Teochew people mostly look completely harmless.

They generally will not ruin a Northeastern big brother's face just because they looked at him one extra time in a crowd.
