Rather than a code of conduct, it is a product of parental anxiety in the wake of changing social mores.

In May 2018, Canadian composer Nicole Lizée staged a special kind of musical theater performance. She made songs from a list called the "Filthy 15" the central thread of the work, composing it as a protest against music censorship.

Music censorship is often tied to critiques about "corrupting young people." In 2013, Miley Cyrus's "We Can't Stop" had parents worried their children would take ecstasy under its influence. More recently, London authorities blamed a surge in crime on a violent rap genre called "Drill."

Controversial elements like sex, violence, and drugs have been strongly present in music since the blues and rock eras, and in pop and hip-hop — genres beloved by teenagers — their presence is now greater than ever. In 2017, [two-thirds of the songs that topped the Billboard Hot 100 contained "explicit" content.](http://www.newsweek.com/songs-explicit-lyrics-popular-increase-billboard-spotify-583551) Between 1958 and 2001, only five songs containing explicit content had ever reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100.

But the way these elements exist in music, and people's attitudes toward them, have not remained constant. They peaked as a force of subversion amid the emotionally charged 1960s, fell silent with the arrival of 1980s conservatism, and have now been revived alongside newly risen music genres and media.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607011246jbzNKpo6Fe0OMVsH.png-w600)

The "Parental Advisory" label has become the accepted, conventional standard for defining explicit content. It was proposed with the aim of purifying music, but in essence it is a product of self-censorship — and it later even became a business opportunity. Explicit content has no very clear definition, yet it never strays far from sex, drugs, and violence. And as social culture and the music industry have shifted, controversial elements have acquired new forms of expression and existence. In a sense, these dangerous elements in music can be seen as a prism refracting society.

### What Is the Parental Advisory Label?

The "Filthy 15" that Nicole Lizée so detests came about like this: in 1983, Tipper Gore — wife of Senator Al Gore (later vice president under Bill Clinton) — was horrified to discover her daughter listening to a song containing a depiction of masturbation. Two years later, she and the wives of Washington officials founded the Parents Music Resource Center (PMRC).

The PMRC listed the 15 pieces of music it considered the most deviant — the "Filthy 15" — works containing elements regarded as heretical: sex, violence, nihilism. Among them were songs by legendary artists such as Madonna, AC/DC, and Prince (whose "Darling Nikki" was what the Gores' daughter had been listening to).

The PMRC also demanded that the music industry establish a rating system like the film industry's. Although the U.S. Congress ultimately passed no music-rating legislation, the demand gave rise to the sticker known as the "Parental Advisory" label — that black-and-white mark reading "Parental Advisory – Explicit Content." From then on, record labels would decide, based on their review and understanding of the content, whether to affix the label to an album.

"I like a lot of the artists on the Filthy 15, and I can't imagine the consequences of someone telling me I couldn't listen to them without my parents' permission. That's absurd," [Nicole Lizée said.](https://www.theguardian.com/music/2018/apr/26/may-contain-masturbation-the-woman-weaponising-pops-filthy-15) For satirical effect, she used a synthesizer to weave into the musical the PMRC members' pronouncements about how Prince's work would corrupt teenagers.

The 1980s, the decade that produced the "Filthy 15," also seem to have been the peak of parental complaints.

In 1984, 19-year-old John McCollum shot himself dead while lying in bed. A year later, 18-year-old Raymond Belknap and 20-year-old James Vance also attempted suicide: Raymond Belknap shot himself and died; James Vance did not die on the spot but was left gravely injured.

![Black Sabbath](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607011300dibMasW0qgjLPXzy.jpg-w600)

Heavy metal bands were all dragged into these suicides. John McCollum's parents believed their son had killed himself because he listened to Black Sabbath's "Suicide Solution." They felt "Suicide Solution" contained hidden satanic messages — played backwards, the song would reveal a voice urging listeners to "pick up the gun and shoot." The two 1985 suicide attempts, meanwhile, pointed to "Better by You, Better than Me" from Judas Priest's album *Stained Class*, a song accused of inciting listeners to kill themselves. Black Sabbath and Judas Priest were consequently subpoenaed to appear in court.

The parents of teenagers hated the filthy stuff in music.

### How Did the "Parental Advisory" Label Get Dirty?

Look closely and you'll find that, although unclean elements and different music genres don't correspond one-to-one in any strict sense, certain genres are bound especially tightly to certain elements because of the music's own properties and the context in which it was born.

Blues has an inextricable connection with sex — so much so that it produced a dedicated subgenre: dirty blues, saturated with sex, was already wildly popular before World War II. "In blues singing, the last word of a line is often deliberately dropped, to suggest either tremendous sexual self-control or feelings too intense for words," American critic Greil Marcus writes in *Mystery Train*.

Rock, which rose alongside the hippie movement, is the grand aggregator of heretical elements — sex, violence, language, drugs; you name it, it all appeared in the melodies of the 1960s generation. By the 1990s, the rise of hip-hop made the expression of drugs and violence even fiercer.

Nor is an artist's expression of a particular element, or what it signifies, uniform across the board.

Take drugs as an example: they were already present in the melodies of the jazz and blues era, but back then only in the literal sense. Once musicalized into the rock of the 1960s, they became a symbol of counterculture. Ask the "flower children" to recall the Summer of Love and Woodstock, and their memories will inevitably be laced with the roar of rock and the smell of marijuana.

Drugs in hip-hop hew closer to a portrayal of the artists' own lives. When hip-hop was born in the late 1970s, street crime in New York ran persistently high, and for many hip-hop artists who came out of the ghetto, street dealing was a means of making a living in their early years.

Artists naturally recount such experiences in their raps. In a specific work, it may be a tale of a narrow escape, warning people just how dangerous such a life is; or it may be a proud look back, because it represents their real past and highlights the contrast between their status today and their humble origins.

An article in Billboard observed that the newly risen hip-hop describes drugs and addiction a great deal, and that an interesting phenomenon is at work: pop songs don't merely condone drug use — they make it a core element of the lyrics. In a sense, the pop world hasn't been this high since the Beatles and the Doors of the 1960s.

The evolution of the sex element shows up more in how artists describe it.

One survey, dividing artists by gender, found differences in how male and female artists express sex: in the 2000s, male pop singers' works contained 10% less "dating" content than in the 1960s (59% versus 69%), while content directly related to "sex" was more than five times that of the past, rising from 7% in the 1960s to 40%. By contrast, female artists' expression of sex also grew — from 6% in the 1960s to 21% in the 2000s — though their enthusiasm for dating barely changed, at around 80% in both eras.

"(In expressing sex) Elvis was already quite an active presence, but even he can't compare with many artists today," [said Andrew Smiler, one of the survey's researchers.](https://melmagazine.com/how-pop-music-came-to-embrace-hookup-culture-ce79e4cf5d0c)

### What Did the Music Video Change?

The birth of MTV in 1981 gave music a visual form. This brought about an explosion of sexual imagery in music videos, since it was a way to grab viewers' attention. A 1987 study tallied 30 hours of MTV content and found that 50% of it contained touching, and that many women were dressed provocatively. In the 1990s, Madonna's "Justify My Love" video was even banned by MTV for depicting sex too explicitly.

[The New York Times wrote in one article](https://www.nytimes.com/1991/07/07/arts/pop-view-as-mtv-turns-10-pop-goes-the-world.html) that what television can sell are pop stars with pretty faces; the music video created another kind of star. Take Madonna: she only had to casually sway her hips to send shockwaves through the media.

Whether it's rhythm and blues, inherently provocative, or fresh, wholesome country, anything touched by the visual medium is, one might say, pervaded by sexual elements.

A 2008 survey found that 73% of music videos contained sexual elements to some degree. Broken down by genre, 90.09% of hip-hop/R&B hybrid music contained sexual elements; even rock and country had a 40% and 37% likelihood, respectively, of containing them.

Much like sex, violence — also a visual element — grew ever more common in the music-video era. The difference lies in the effect: although both draw criticism, sexual elements make videos and songs more popular, while violence tends to make viewers uncomfortable.

Compared with the music video's fairly obvious influence on sex and violence, you might not expect that the rise of streaming is also one of the reasons profanity is increasingly present in music today. Unlike radio, streaming platforms carry both edited versions (clean versions) and versions with every swear word intact (explicit versions).

Among works tagged "explicit" on Spotify, the first in history to top the Billboard Hot 100 was Prince's 1984 single "Let's Go Crazy" (its lyrics contain references to drugs). Until 2001, only three number-one singles carried the "explicit" tag. In the first month of 2017, three of the four number-one singles were tagged "explicit."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607011216mRNrjtMDcyqPI25E.jpg-w600)

"Chart success used to depend on radio play, and artists' work had to be edited before it was aired, whether by bleeping or swapping words," [said Larry Bartleet, a reporter for the British music magazine *NME*.](http://www.newsweek.com/songs-explicit-lyrics-popular-increase-billboard-spotify-583551) "Now, thanks to streaming, artists can create on their own terms — they don't have to worry about music censorship."

### Does "Filth" Have Borders?

More often in the United States, the handling of elements like profanity, drugs, and race in music is not an outright ban of the entire work, but editing in the form of muting or word substitution.

Media apply different standards depending on the audience. The Disney Channel, aimed mainly at children, is stricter than other channels and bleeps out profanity; some radio stations broadcast unedited original versions during nighttime slots.

One of audio engineer Joel Mullis's jobs is removing profanity from songs. Speaking of MTV, which faces all of America, and BET, whose main audience is Black, he said engineering teams need to produce different versions to suit different platforms, and sometimes the band is even asked to re-record a song with alternative lyrics.

Outright bans do happen. The Madonna video mentioned earlier is one such example. Another: in 1992, "Cop Killer," written by rapper Ice-T for the metal band Body Count, was condemned — for declaring intent to kill police — by then-President George H.W. Bush, PMRC founder Tipper Gore, and others. Ice-T defended it as a protest work, but ultimately withdrew the album containing the song.

Worth noting is that the First Amendment's protection of artists' expression means the United States cannot regulate music at the national level; the censors are the platforms, the record companies, or the artists themselves.

That the Filthy 15 affair escalated to the national level in the 1980s — an era when music had in fact been thoroughly commercialized and domesticated — was tied to the overall atmosphere of the times. Chang Tieh-chih writes in *Sound and Fury* (《声音与愤怒》): "The 1980s were the heyday of the New Right in postwar Anglo-American history. The neoconservatism of Margaret Thatcher and Ronald Reagan became the new Zeitgeist: economically it emphasized laissez-faire and the omnipotence of the market, while the corresponding social values were individualism and an emphasis on traditional morality and family values — so any seed of resistance was a thorn in their side."

Much like in the United States, in Britain it is the BBC itself that sets the standards for banning songs. Works involving dirty elements all face the danger of being banned. Among the songs the BBC once banned were the Beatles' "A Day in the Life," which alludes to drug use, and "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds," suspected of the same; "Hard Headed Woman" by "the King," Elvis Presley, which discusses the Bible; and George Michael's "I Want Your Sex," with its sexual content.

According to the BBC's statements in recent years, it no longer bans any songs.

One example, unrelated to forbidden elements but illustrative of how platforms hold the power of life and death over music: some American radio stations recently decided to stop playing any Kanye West songs — not because there was anything wrong with the works themselves, but because of his remark that "slavery was a choice."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201806070114475W8RteG1qiAYal4k.jpg-w600)

When a ban comes from the national level, it tends to be broader in scope and arrive more suddenly.

In 2009, Jamaica's Broadcasting Commission issued a ban on songs in a style known as "daggering." Although the term literally suggests attacking with a knife, the dancehall-craze songs it refers to are sexually suggestive works. The ban was not limited to sexual elements; it targeted "any recording, live performance, or music video that promotes or glorifies guns or other weapons, and any recording, live performance, or music video that promotes or glorifies acts of violence against persons, such as murder, rape, robbery, or arson." The ban expressly stipulated that broadcast media such as radio and television may not air filtered versions that have been edited.

Australian pop singer Kylie Minogue's album, when released in China in 2008, contained only 10 tracks — three fewer than the overseas version. The three missing tracks were "Nu-di-ty," "Speakerphone," and "Like a Drug."

In 2015, [the Ministry of Culture published a blacklist of 120 banned songs,](http://ent.sina.com.cn/y/yneidi/2015-08-12/doc-ifxftvni8986037.shtml) because "these online music products contain content that promotes obscenity, violence, or criminal activity, or that endangers social morals, in violation of Article 16 of the Interim Provisions on the Administration of Internet Culture. They have been placed on the online music management blacklist, and no entity or individual may provide them."

The intriguing part is that bans tend to stoke listeners' curiosity about the content, turning the forbidden into a coveted object. Many records now voluntarily carry the Parental Advisory label, hoping to ride the wave of young consumers' rebellious psychology. Before Ice-T withdrew the controversial album mentioned earlier, it had become a hot commodity in stores that had not pulled it from shelves of their own accord.

### Should Music Be Held Responsible for Real-World Behavior?

The two camps in this debate seem unable to fully persuade each other.

Some have tried to settle the question with data. The Rap Research Lab compared criminal elements in hip-hop with real-world crime and found no direct correlation between the two. "The data shows crime and lyrics are barely correlated. For example, from 1993 to 1995, crime rates fell significantly, while over the same period mentions of crime in hip-hop steadily climbed," said researcher Emmanuel Kohdra. That said, some portions of his data did show a positive correlation.

![Image from: [staplecrops.com/emmanuelkhodra/](http://staplecrops.com/emmanuelkhodra/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180607004331CeabF8wvZEQhgANM.png-w600)

When London authorities linked crime to Drill, they presented data showing a rise in crime numbers. But Amnesty International criticized the London police's database for "stigmatizing young Black people based on the music they listen to or their behavior on social media."

Still, some consensus has gradually emerged over the course of the debate. First, controversial behavior is the product of multiple factors acting together. Take the teenagers who chose suicide after listening to metal music: their lives had not been going well to begin with. Raymond Belknap and James Vance had both been abused since childhood; Raymond Belknap, who grew up in a divorced family, also struggled with alcohol, and his sister had attempted suicide twice (she was not a metal fan); James Vance had a history of drug use, and his father was plagued by alcoholism and drug addiction. The court ultimately dropped the charges against both bands.

Moreover, directly linking music and crime, to a degree, blocks the search for answers to more complex questions. Sam Dunn, an anthropologist and metalhead, says people are sometimes just looking for a scapegoat: "I think people slap all kinds of labels on metal just to find simple answers to complex problems."

Setting aside cases where music is used as a propaganda tool, artists are simply pursuing expression; they do not truly encourage any extreme behavior. Judas Priest's manager, defending the band against the "hidden messages" claim, said that if the band were going to bury a message in its music, it would be one telling fans to buy more albums — not to kill themselves.
