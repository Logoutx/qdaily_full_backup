The impact of a sluggish economy is harsher than you think — a look at young people in Japan's low-desire society today

@@QD_BODY@@
Japanese young people have lived through prolonged economic stagnation unlike anything their peers in other developed countries have experienced. This has had a huge impact on their consumer psychology. The suppression of the desire to consume may have no necessary connection to poverty, but it has become a matter of consumer consciousness.

Shunning fashion, shunning designer brands, shunning car ownership, shunning drinking, even shunning romance… Japanese young people are walking away from everything that looks like "consumerism."

In 2015, Japanese economic commentator Kenichi Ohmae, in his book *The Low-Desire Society* (《低欲望社会》), dubbed Japan — a country made up of young people who detest consuming — a "low-desire society." A few years earlier, in 2009, consultant Hisakazu Matsuda published *A Study of the Consumption-Averse Generation* (《厌消费世代的研究》), an examination of how young Japanese consumption behavior was changing; the blurb on its obi was deliberately provocative — "Isn't buying a car something only idiots do?"

In July 2016, Toyota launched the new version of its small sports car, the Toyota 86, in Japan. At the press conference at the time, Toyota executive Hirotaka Saga put it this way: "If young people no longer see owning a car as a dream, this industry will struggle to survive."

The Toyota 86, priced around 2.62–3.25 million yen, was designed as an entry-level sports car "affordable for everyone," yet 60% of its buyers are over 40. Similarly, more than 80% of buyers of Honda's lightweight roadster S660, priced at 1.98–2.28 million yen, come from the same age group. A 2015 survey by the Japan Automobile Manufacturers Association found that 59% of young Japanese who do not own a car said they "don't want to buy one."

![Toyota 86. Image from Yahoo](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170524202643gG31DPCWu4XShvq9.jpg-w600)

After the collapse of the bubble economy in the 1990s, Japan sank into a slump in which economic growth nearly flatlined. Anxiety and unease about the future, compounded by the growing pressures of a low birth rate and an aging population, have increasingly suppressed young people's desire to consume.

They have, for instance, also turned away from golf and skiing, the pastimes their fathers' generation loved. Golf consumers dropped from 13 million to 7.6 million, and the overall market shrank from 19.6 trillion yen to 9.01 trillion yen; in the worst year, 2014, 40 golf courses announced they were closing. The 1987 film *Journey to the Snow Peaks* (《雪岭之旅》) ignited Japan's ski market — highways heading to ski resorts were jammed every ski season, and weekend lift queues took an hour — but the seasonal winter trains that once ran to the ski resorts have become practically dispensable.

The izakaya market has changed too: office workers have gone from two drinks after work to nursing a single glass, and the low-priced izakaya chains tend to perform better. After Japan's consumption tax rose from 5% to 8% in 2014, Japanese people increasingly preferred to drink at home — after all, buying a can of beer at a convenience store plus a snack to go with it costs considerably less than a budget izakaya.

Public data from the Japan Department Stores Association shows that the department store industry's full-year revenue in 2016 hit a 36-year low, falling below 60 trillion yen for the first time. Japan's largest department store group, Mitsukoshi Isetan, saw its 2016 profit fall by 48.6%. Although the department store decline is a global problem, in Japan the key issue does not come from e-commerce shocks.

In 2016, Japan's GDP growth was only 1.3%, people aged 65 and over already made up 25% of the population, and young people embraced a spend-nothing-if-you-can mindset.

But go back 50 years, and Japan was an entirely different scene.

Japan's most popular advertising slogan in 1970 came from Fuji Xerox — "From fierce to beautiful." Japanese scholars Ryūzō Uchida and Munesuke Mita regard that year as a great turning point in Japan's consumer society.

During the high-growth era of the 1950s–1960s, Japanese office workers made the company their home and prided themselves on being "fierce company men" who labored for their employer body and soul. Black-and-white televisions, washing machines, and refrigerators were the dream goods within reach of ordinary Japanese in the 1950s through hard work; in the following decade, the "new three essentials" became the "3C" — color television, cooler (air conditioner), and car. Durable consumer goods that added convenience to life carried the wealth dreams of the Japanese during the high-growth era.

The 1970 national census showed Japan's population reaching 100 million for the first time. Under the lifetime employment system, the middle class gradually took shape, and "100 million, all middle class" became a Japanese national self-image. In the bubble-economy 1980s, the Japanese reached the peak of a "high-consumption society."

Scholars such as Ryūzō Uchida attributed the phenomenon — even high school girls imitating adults by carrying designer bags — to "conspicuousness," but noted that, unlike the hobby-like consumption patterns of the privileged few at the end of the 19th century, Japanese brand mania had a mass character. Designer brands such as ISSEY MIYAKE, COMME des GARCONS, and Y's were all born against a backdrop of universal middle-class status and young consumers beginning to seek differentiation from others; department stores such as PARCO became "incubators" for young designer brands.

![Shibuya, Tokyo during the bubble economy era. Image from Mainichi Shimbun](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170524202438vYeBoh3fCGIyjdZ2.jpg-w600)

The "consumption as virtue" market, driven by young people, flourished for 20 years — and then the Japanese lived through the "Lost 20 Years" after the bubble burst.

"During the Lost 20 Years, Japanese young people went through a great deal," [said Yūsuke Shimada, associate researcher in the Research Department of the Japan Research Institute](http://diamond.jp/articles/-/111372). In their childhood, young Japanese lived through the bubble collapse and the Great Hanshin Earthquake; in their youth they were hit by the 2008 Lehman shock and the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake. More importantly, Japanese young people endured prolonged economic stagnation that their peers in other developed countries never experienced. "This has had a huge impact on their consumer psychology."

Kazukiyo Higuchi, a professor at Hosei University, also told QDaily: "Young people today staying away from car ownership may have no necessary connection to poverty — it has become an issue of consumer consciousness." Higuchi previously served as director of the Kyushu Bureau of Economy, Trade and Industry and is the author of books including *Consumer Issues in Japan* (《日本消费者问题》).

Miyu Shiraishi, born in 1991, graduated this April from Meiji University. Young Japanese like her, born between 1987 and 1996, are known as "the relaxed generation" — that is, the generation raised under "pressure-free education."

In her senior year, Shiraishi job-hunted for a while, but not wanting to enter the working world so soon, she stayed on at the same university for a two-year master's degree. Japan has no strong culture of grad school, and only two people in her class went on to graduate study. "My parents covered all my tuition. Doing four years of undergrad and then two years of master's at a private university really put a lot of financial pressure on them."

Still, Shiraishi counts as quite lucky — at least her parents were willing to pay her tuition, and she didn't fall into the trap of graduating with millions of yen in loans. But she feels that "if you measure college by the tuition-to-value ratio, going to university isn't a very cost-effective thing." To reduce the financial burden on her parents, she took a part-time front-desk job at a skating school during grad school, earning roughly 50,000–60,000 yen a month.

Part-time work is extremely common among Japanese university students; one report shows that as many as 72% of university students have jobs. Japanese university students work an average of 12.5 hours a week, and because late-night shifts pay more, 20.7% of university students work between 10 p.m. and 5 a.m.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170524214748ZFOi5uMVSNdxDskW.jpg-w600)

Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications once published the National Survey of Actual Consumption Conditions, which showed that over the 30 years from 1984 to 2014, the average annual consumption index of single Japanese men and women fell from 86.5 and 96.3 to 67.6 and 87.9 respectively. Everything saved from daily consumption went straight into savings.

To encourage consumption, the Abe administration pumped in tens of trillions of yen, and the Bank of Japan even adopted negative interest rates in the hope of lowering Japanese people's willingness to save — yet all to little effect. Among young people aged 25–34, nearly 30% work for companies on non-regular employment terms.

One consequence of unstable income is that even when young people want to marry, they dare not: they don't have enough money to cover new housing and child-rearing costs. Japan's lifetime unmarried rate has risen year by year, reaching 23.3% for men and 14.06% for women. The Japanese describe the state of people who have lost their parents and never married as "alone at the ends of the earth."

And with a highly aged population leaving Japan's pension system teetering, young people choose to save more money to guard against unknowable future risks.

Shiraishi earns 210,000 yen a month at her job, and by living with her parents she saves a large chunk of rent. "I set aside 20,000–30,000 yen as spending money, and I plan to save everything else. Though I'm still discussing with my parents whether I should chip in for some household expenses."

The traits common to Japanese young people — avoiding car ownership, avoiding fashion — are all present in Shiraishi.

"I'm a Tokyo native myself, and Tokyo's transit is convenient enough. Trains and buses already cover my travel needs, so I don't feel any need to own a car," Shiraishi said. Japan Automobile Manufacturers Association data also shows that 40% of respondents who don't buy a car say they "can get by without one."

The average age of the cars owned by Japanese multi-person households is 8.1 years. If you assume the Japanese replace their cars every 8 years, the Toyota 86 mentioned earlier (in its base configuration) comes to about 330,000 yen a year. A 2015 "National Survey of Actual Automobile Life" by Sony Assurance showed that Japanese people spend about 170,000 yen a year keeping a car, meaning owning a Toyota 86 actually costs about 500,000 yen a year.

According to the latest statistics from Japan's National Tax Agency, the average annual income of young Japanese aged 25–29 is 3.52 million yen. Feeling no need for a private car, young Japanese are unwilling to take on a loan to buy one that would swallow 14% of their annual income. It's also worth noting that the pay of college graduates has barely changed over the past decade.

Yet Japan's car-sharing market is growing fast: it was worth 15.4 billion yen in 2014 and is expected to reach 29.5 billion yen by 2020. Take Times24, Japan's largest car-sharing company: the monthly fee is 1,030 yen, plus 206 yen for every 15 minutes of use.

[Yōhei Harada, director of the Youth Research Laboratory at Hakuhodo Brand Design, believes](http://media.lifenet-seimei.co.jp/2016/04/22/6529/): "Big-ticket consumption has all been broken into small payments. (The car-sharing boom) fits the demand to pay just a little when you feel like spending."

In 2000, Uniqlo launched a fleece jacket priced around 2,500 yen — roughly half the going market price. That same year, ZARA and H&M entered the Japanese market; before that, buying clothes for a little over 2,000 yen was simply unthinkable. In 2015, GU released the best-selling item in the brand's history, wide-leg pants (priced at 1,490 yen), selling 3 million pairs in just two months.

The website Mynavi once surveyed Japanese male university students: 23% of respondents considered a 5,000-yen piece of clothing quite expensive, using Uniqlo as their yardstick — "if it's more expensive than Uniqlo, that counts as expensive." Now GU, which is even cheaper, is actually outperforming Uniqlo, and GU president Osamu Yunoki has said he aims to lift sales to 1 trillion yen.

During the bubble era, the "one-point luxury" approach of saving up for a single luxury bag was popular, but walk through Japanese streets today and you'll find plenty of young people carrying canvas bags from brands like DEAN&DELUCA and anello.

"Fast-fashion brands have gotten a lot better in quality, and fashion changes all the time — I won't spend a lot of money on expensive clothes. I pay attention to fashion, but I'm not too fussy about it," Shiraishi said. Buying relatively large quantities of cheap fast fashion fits exactly what Yōhei Harada calls the "fragmentation of consumption."

Japanese young people born into an era of economic depression actually live in a time far richer in material goods than their parents' generation; from birth they have been surrounded by abundant products rolled out in response to deflation. Though deflation may sound negative, Japanese merchants rack their brains to sell quality, low-priced deflation-era goods.

Cheap eyeglasses, convenience-store private-label products, 100-yen shops that multiply year after year… these saturate Japanese commercial society. Since the cheap ones are good enough, there's no need to chase pricier goods. Japanese young people no longer see "consumption as virtue" the way their parents did; in their eyes, non-essential needs that don't matter can simply be excluded from their consumption options, and better value for money is what they pursue.

However, Professor Kazukiyo Higuchi told QDaily, "Despite repeated natural disasters and economic crises, Japanese society as a whole has remained stable. So, on another dimension, the lives of Japanese young people still appear affluent. And young people's consumption still tends to be driven by feeling, decided in the moment. Young people keen on spending big on mobile games are one example."

Yano Research Institute once published a *Survey of Japan's "Otaku" Market*, where the so-called otaku market covers every facet of subculture including anime, manga, figurines, and idols. Take just the idol market within it: in 2015 the Japanese idol market grew 30.7% year on year to 155 billion yen, and the average "idol fan" spends about 80,000 yen a year on their idol.

Low-desire young people are nonetheless willing to splash out on their hobbies. A survey by the Regional Distribution Economics Research Institute likewise shows that among the things Japanese young people care most about, "hobbies" ranks second at 20.3%, just behind "work" at 27.8% in first place.

And the buzzwords that periodically appear in Japan — things like "the ◯◯ type" or "◯◯ girls" — seem to show that "nationwide phenomena" are becoming harder and harder to produce, while culture and goods that cater to a specific group and carry a more palpable sense of identity are more likely to win a devoted following within that group.

In June 2016, Japan's Dentsu held a "Human Research Council" at which companies including Coca-Cola Japan and Shiseido all agreed that low-desire young people had become harder to read. Low desire is not a total loss of material desire; the key to getting "consumption-averse" young people to open their wallets is making them feel the utility or the fun factor that makes them want to pay.

In 1988, the Japanese energy drink brand Regain ran the tagline "Can you fight for 24 hours?" Compared with their fathers born in the Shōwa era, the "Heisei generation" (young people born after 1989) are widely seen by HR departments as somewhat "self-indulgent": they no longer want to devote themselves body and soul to work the way their fathers did, and they dislike the extension of work — the semi-mandatory company drinking parties.

One survey found that 55.43% and 78.55% of Japanese middle-aged office workers aged 50–59, respectively, were willing to sacrifice private time for their salary and to attend after-work drinking parties; by contrast, more than 67.79% of young Japanese office workers chose "private life" over "salary first," and 72.78% would decline an invitation to a drinking party.

"I hate drinking, and if possible I'd rather not attend those colleague drinking parties at all. They cost money and waste time — I only feel miserable, with no benefit whatsoever," Shiraishi said. Private time matters enormously to young Japanese office workers today; once off work they just want to hurry home and spend the time on their personal hobbies, and the ideal of the "corporate warrior" who works for the company 24 hours a day is fading fast.

When Shiraishi applied for her current job at the insurance company, the position she put in for was the "general track," which doesn't require daily client visits and cold calls like the "sales track" — her reason being "I don't want to be too tired." A survey by Japan's Cabinet Office shows that although their incomes have fallen, Japanese young people aged 20–29 report higher satisfaction than other age groups across multiple dimensions, including leisure life.

In his book, Kenichi Ohmae attributes this phenomenon to the "low-desire society" stripping young people of the desire to get ahead. That claim sounds like an exaggeration, but since the bubble burst, Japan has produced no more highly influential, innovative companies capable of changing people's way of life. Globally speaking, Japan's start-up index is now essentially the lowest in the world.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170524220350VmNs5rXxw8It0LeC.jpeg-w600)

Sony, which once changed how young people around the world listened to music with the Walkman, has been quiet for a long time. Before the Sony Building in Ginza was demolished, Sony held an "It's a Sony" exhibition looking back on its history, and on the wall where visitors could leave their thoughts, we found numerous messages from Japanese people reminiscing about the golden age of manufacturing.

![Message wall at the "It's a Sony" exhibition. Photo by the author](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170524201636S2mg4T960cDNlZHq.jpg-w600)

Young people in Europe and America actually face the same problems of economic malaise and "getting poorer." The average personal wealth of America's "millennials" — $29,530 — is 52% lower than that of the baby-boomer generation, and their net worth is 56% lower; the share of American young people owning their own homes fell from 46% in 1989 to 43% in 2013; and in 2017, British parents are expected to lend a total of 6.5 billion pounds to buy their children's first homes, up from 5 billion pounds in 2016 — a 30% increase.

Yet at least Westerners still talk about the "millennial" economy, and China, whose overall population structure is still relatively young, is battling for "post-90s" consumers. The Japanese, by contrast, rarely discuss how to do business with the "Heisei generation."

Japan's Cabinet Office's *White Paper on the Aging Society* predicts that by 2050 Japan's population will fall below 100 million, and people over 65 will make up 38.8% of the total — far above the 26.5% figure for developed countries as a whole.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170524211550QmCOu1A96adGIEp4.jpg-w600)

The "baby-boomer generation" that worked through the bubble era is retiring, and more and more companies are fixing their eyes on the cash-rich retirees of that generation; even Sony and Panasonic have crossed over into the "silver economy." Sadanobu Waku, president of Panasonic age free (Panasonic's subsidiary in the elder-care business), once said: "If you run the numbers, going into elder care from here on is the only choice."

During the high-growth and bubble periods, large numbers of rural people flooded into the cities. Today Japan has the "concentration-in-Tokyo" phenomenon, with the population piling into the capital; villages and small towns that have lost their young labor force don't have enough tax revenue to sustain public functions, and the problems faced by withering "marginal settlements" (communities where more than 50% of residents are over 65) are more severe than those of the "low-desire society."

Around the world, no country's population is aging as far along as Japan's — but in the longer run, China will face the same problem, and for a considerable stretch of the future China will also be in a phase of slowing economic growth.

The "Lost 20 Years" may well drag on, and the impact of the low-desire society extends far beyond consumption — it is far greater than we imagine.
