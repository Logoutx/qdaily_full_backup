Why Are Most of the Companies Showing Up at the Beijing Film Festival Online Film and Video Companies?

@@QD_BODY@@
Will a rapidly ballooning present give rise to a creative future?

Judging by the raw numbers, the 7th Beijing International Film Festival looked like a resounding success, with signed agreements totaling 17.458 billion yuan. But the biggest single deal among them — Dadi Cinema's 3.378 billion yuan acquisition of Orange Sky Golden Harvest's mainland China cinema business — had already been announced back in January. The same goes for Poly Film's 680 million yuan acquisition of Xingxing Culture. News like this makes you wonder whether the so-called signing ceremony really was just for signing, and whether the real deals had nothing to do with the Beijing film festival at all.

The Film Market is one component of the Beijing film festival, where companies set up booths in the hope of facilitating cooperation and deals between different film companies. How many deals get done is the official yardstick of whether the Film Market counts as a success.

Another figure is also worth chewing on: after the latest verification by the Film Bureau of the State Administration of Press, Publication, Radio, Film and Television, China's 2016 box office should stand at 49.2 billion yuan. At the start of the year, the Film Bureau had said 45.7 billion.

Everyone knows where that growth came from. At the end of January this year, the Film Bureau adjusted how box office was counted: on top of traditional ticket sales, e-commerce service fees were now included — the fees people pay to platforms like Maoyan, Yupiaoer, and Taopiaopiao when buying tickets through them. And so 2016's Chinese box office magically gained an extra 3.5 billion yuan out of thin air.

These days, the film and TV business — like many things — looks booming in the numbers, but walk around the venue once or twice and the things worth pondering are entirely different.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170427153519PVfG9bXaTH8zyDxZ.jpg-w600)

Although this year's Film Market is bigger in scale than the 2016 one, almost all the new exhibitors are unfamiliar faces. "Lots of these companies I've never even heard of," an industry insider visiting the market told QDaily, pointing at a company's booth in the Beijing International Hotel Conference Center where the Film Market was held. "The exhibitors this year are all so low."

Perhaps "low" would be more fairly replaced with "unfamiliar." Look into these companies' track records and you find that most were founded not long ago, and mainly shoot and produce online film and video content. Judging by current production standards, this may be the lowest-end category of product in the entire industry.

The booth rented by Yongyi Pictures is one of the largest at this year's film festival Film Market. The company was registered back in 2007, but the work that first gave it some renown was a 2012 web series, *The Past of the Northeast: Twenty Years of Gangland Turmoil* (《东北往事之黑道风云 20 年》). Recently they've been promoting their new project on Weibo: a web series and online film project called *Notes from a Haunted House* (《凶宅笔记》).

Lexiang Pictures, from Shenzhen, works on importing overseas films and distributing Chinese films overseas; its most notable previous case was taking *Crosscurrent* to Japan. On the content production side, its titles include the *Xie Wendong* (《谢文东》) series and online films like *My Gangster Sister* (《我的黑道妹妹》) and *The Best Youth* (《最好的青春》).

Laike Media from Guangzhou also claimed a small booth in the Film Market, with web variety shows *The Goddess Has a Cure* (《女神有药》) and *The Awesome Detective* (《名侦探猴塞雷》), and web series such as *Dreams and Reality* (《梦想与现实》) and *Genius J* (《天才 J》).

![*The Goddess Has a Cure*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170427152839BZRx5bsevSoKQNPX.jpg-w600)

Both the names of these companies and the film and TV programs they produce are probably quite unfamiliar to many people. Yet it is precisely companies like these — whose main product is online film and video content — that make up the mainstream of this year's film festival Film Market.

This can be confusing. It calls itself a film festival, so why are the exhibitors all web series and online films — things that seem to have little to do with film, from companies that are still very marginal in film and TV circles?

Part of the reason, of course, lies in the nature of the Beijing film festival itself.

Internationally, a film market segment like this usually appears at industry-facing festivals or film fairs. The European Film Market, the Marché du Film in Cannes, and the American Film Market are known as the world's three biggest film markets. Their main function is to provide a platform for rights trading. Producers from various countries announce their film plans there, and distributors from all over the world buy up the local distribution rights to films.

China has no such conditions. Neither the Beijing nor the Shanghai film festival has a film market capable of carrying the rights-trading function. For foreign producers, the censorship system means they can't pitch their films to the Chinese market. For Chinese producers, the quality film projects on the market have long been snapped up, so there's no need to come to a festival Film Market either. And so the rights-trading function has been stripped out of Chinese festival film markets.

Still, the Beijing film festival can bring together large numbers of industry insiders in a short time, which makes it a good platform for marketing and promotion. Ask the exhibitors QDaily interviewed at this year's festival why they came to the Film Market, and the words "marketing and promotion" were bound to come up.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170427153959ZnrqyD71ovis5Ypz.jpg-w600)

If the trading segment serves only a marketing function, then it's more or less dispensable for big companies that have already secured their position in the industry. What followed was traditional film companies pulling out of festival film markets. At the 2015 Shanghai film festival Film Market, you could still see Huayi Brothers and Wanda. By 2016, only mid-sized companies like Chenming Media and Yinrun Media remained. And by the 2017 Beijing film festival, traditional film companies had virtually disappeared altogether.

What took their place were the online film companies that have only risen over the past two years and desperately need the market's attention. With the big companies gone, they naturally occupied this year's Beijing film festival Film Market.

Of course, they didn't stop at the Film Market. The Motion Picture Association of America held a forum at the Beijing film festival titled "Online Films and the New Forms of China's Film Industry Chain," inviting industry insiders to discuss the development of online films. Asked why they held the forum, they said, in a matter-of-fact tone: "Because online films are films too — they're the hot thing this year."

And hot they are. Over the past year, China's box office stalled, and the number of companies shooting theatrical films fell from 553 in the first half of the year to 398 in the second half.

As one side ebbed, the other flowed: in today's Chinese film and TV industry, it's the companies making online films that stand out and perform most actively. According to EntGroup's *China Online Film Industry Research Report*, released in October 2016, only 64 companies were shooting online films in the first half of 2014; by the first half of 2016, that had exploded to 843.

And the data revealed by Chang Bin, general manager of the film investment business division at Tencent Penguin Pictures, at the MPA's online film forum was even more startling: more than 2,000 companies across the web are involved in online films. That means the number of companies making online films is four to five times the number of traditional film and TV companies making theatrical films.

All of this seems to hint that online films are becoming a new trend for the future.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426214711e81CXGycJmzURtBa.jpg-w600)

Judging from where this industry stands, that's not a happy conclusion — after all, even if you haven't seen one yourself, you've probably heard about the quality of online films. The two most obvious problems are vulgarity and knockoffs.

In 2015, among iQiyi's ten highest-grossing revenue-share titles, the *Taoist Priest Comes Down the Mountain* (《道士出山》) series accounted for four, with total revenue-share income reaching 25 million yuan. Yet the series is widely seen as riding the coattails of the film *Monk Comes Down the Mountain* that Chen Kaige released back in the day. And around the release of Feng Xiaogang's *I Am Not Madame Bovary*, a wave of online films with titles like *The Revenge of Madame Bovary* (《潘金莲复仇记》), *Who Killed Pan Jinlian* (《谁杀了潘金莲》), and *I Won't Be Madame Bovary* (《我不做潘金莲》) also appeared on video sites. The online film industry was consequently tagged with the label of "riding on IP."

Online films laced with porn and violence are also common on the various video sites. Posters and titles find all kinds of ways to push past the usual limits. In November 2016, more than 60 online films were ordered pulled from the shelves by the regulators for vulgarity.

![*Taoist Priest Comes Down the Mountain*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170426214812T3qHdF0m2t4JZ8nE.jpg-w600)

Beyond the questionable quality of online films themselves, the industry may not qualify as a healthy one either. What's driven the rapid growth of online films isn't entirely audience demand for content, but video sites' need to fill out their content libraries — just as the earliest to propose the concept of the "online film" was not someone who makes films, but iQiyi.

iQiyi proposed the concept three years ago. At a summit forum titled "Online Films Make Dreams Come True," iQiyi argued that it could itself become a platform for distributing films on the internet, and on that basis laid out the standards for an online film: a running time of over 60 minutes, a complete film structure and capacity, and the internet as its first-release platform.

At the time, iQiyi was still trailing in the video site race. By mobile monthly active users, iQiyi ranked third in 2014, behind Youku and Tencent Video. To compete, iQiyi urgently needed a large volume of film and TV works that could draw viewers to its platform — and the concept of the online film was proposed against exactly this backdrop.

Once again — as in many industries — online films became a cash-burning battlefield. To catch up with iQiyi and obtain more exclusive titles, Tencent Video, which entered the market relatively late, preferred to simply buy out rights. Youku, meanwhile, set up a dedicated online film department, aggressively acquiring quality online films through minimum-guarantee or buyout deals.

Overall, though, quality is not the only factor the video platforms consider. Just as Hollywood's Big Six in the early days had to acquire many independent labels to fill Walmart's shelves with DVDs of every genre, today's video platforms need as much content as possible to attract every kind of viewer and advertiser.

Online films and the online versions of traditional films share the same business model: membership fees and advertising are both revenue sources for the video platforms. But the two are obviously not on the same order of magnitude in quantity — and if you regard them all as ad slots, there's no difference in quality when it comes to ad exposure; the former simply has far more slots than the latter.

Given the questionable play-count statistics of video sites, the existence of online films and every kind of web series could be generating revenue for the platforms on both the advertising and traffic fronts.

If you factor in return on investment, traditional film production is even more disadvantaged. For one thing, the traditional film industry is notorious for uncertainty. Big-name directors, famous actors, and familiar stories — none of them can guarantee with 100 percent certainty that a film will succeed in the market. Wanda's *The Great Wall* and Huayi's Chinese New Year tentpole *The Wasted Times* are both famous recent cases. When facing audiences directly, no filmmaker can be fully confident.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201704271532282QLTHRW8Xe7chGbg.jpg-w600)

This risk becomes even more erratic in the presence of policy swings. If risk strikes everyone equally, lower-cost online films and web series become a refuge for many producers — no one wants something they spent years making to end up locked in a dark room.

For now, the ones paying for these online films are the capital side more than the audience. But if we're optimistic and believe that everything on the internet follows the principle of "from shoddy mass production to quality segmentation," we might hope that truly creative products can emerge from online films too.

Low cost can be a double-edged sword. Lower experimentation costs may make the web fertile ground for genre films — territory that, in the traditional Hollywood system, usually belongs to independent labels.

Market research firm EntGroup believes that thrillers, game adaptations, sports, and similar genres have relatively niche audiences compared with other theatrical genres, so the theatrical model doesn't work for them. "But with the user-preference profiles and precision marketing of online films, such niche genres can find their target audiences, greatly increasing the profitability of niche-genre films while also enriching the range of online film genres."

Film critic Zhou Liming, who once made online films, believes this kind of product is not in direct competition with theatrical releases: "I think the content best suited to the internet is a certain kind of comedy — very down-to-earth, very reflective of ordinary people's joys and sorrows right now, and possibly short and punchy, connecting to some degree with short video."

Capital may also attract higher-caliber creators — especially amid tight policy control and the sky-high cost of a failed film. "This year alone, at the online film level, I've already seen so many really creative ideas. I think we're in an era of surplus creativity — the ideas are there, but the conversion is poor. So capital can squeeze these things out quickly and everyone can see them — I think that's great," director Wu Bai, who directed the web series *Evil Minds* (《心理罪》), said at one of the Beijing film festival's forums.

Of course, judging just from the companies exhibiting at this Beijing film festival, innovation seems hard to come by. Even the so-called big companies can't claim they've found their direction here. Huayi Brothers' subsidiary Huayi Chuangxing took the lead on *Constable: Blade of Blood* (《捕快血之刃》); Huace Film & TV partnered with Qishu Youyu on the online film *Adult Fairy Tale* (《成人童话》); and iQiyi brought in Roger Corman, known as the "King of B Movies."

![Roger Corman](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20170427153722hL6DEI4VrsgpkXcU.jpg-w600)

Even these companies haven't produced any online films that reached breakout-hit level. This industry is even more fragmented than the traditional film industry. Behind the top ten online films on iQiyi's revenue-share list the year before last were multiple companies — Seven Entertainment, Taomeng, Lanlanlanlan, iQiyi, and more — and if you count other platforms, even more than that.

In the online film industry, traditional film companies and today's small companies stand at the same starting line. For an industry only three years old, data remains the sole KPI by which performance is measured.
