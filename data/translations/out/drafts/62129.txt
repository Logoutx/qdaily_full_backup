When a word like "history" is placed before us, it is usually overshadowed by grand narratives. We hope to do what we can to sort things out through more micro-level interviews.

Ninety-nine-year-old Wang Shuzhen is moving. Asked what she thinks about it. Wang Shuzhen has not moved in 78 years. She says she has many thoughts. But once she opens her mouth, she can't say more than a few sentences.

The sun came out rarely, and soon shifted away again. Wang Shuzhen sat at the entrance to the lane, wearing a knitted beret she had sewn herself three years earlier, with eight panels and a short point at the top. Her head was small, her hair gray and soft, pressed against her scalp, with a little fringe showing beneath the brim. Compared with the photographs of her youth, her face was thinner and flatter. In the photos the fold of her eyelid had been a thin line; in old age it became countless fine wrinkles. Her right eye was blind, a gray haze.

Among Zhang Yuan's 1,122 households, many elderly people still live, and Wang Shuzhen is the oldest. Passersby asking where she is moving have to lean in and shout into her ear. "Moving to Pudong, to my eldest son's place," she shouts back. The lanes grow emptier by the day. Tricycles collecting old furniture pedal in one after another, leaving only when fully loaded. Someone walks past carrying a spittoon -- even now some households have no flush toilet and empty it every morning.

More than a month ago, the land-use rights to a 9,375-square-meter plot on the east side of Zhang Yuan were sold for 5.802 billion yuan to a group of developers led by China Resources. The Jing'an District government, where Zhang Yuan is located, wants to turn the area into a second Xintiandi: a commercial district that preserves old-style lanes. Along West Nanjing Road, where Zhang Yuan sits, they have already redeveloped Fengsheng Li in succession and built the commercial district HKRI Taikoo Hui on the former site of the demolished Dazhong Li; the Starbucks Reserve Roastery occupies one 2,700-square-meter, classical-style circular building inside it.

Zhang Yuan, on Taixing Road south of Wujiang Road and west of Shimen First Road, will be next. "The houses won't be demolished," but residents are required to clear out by March 8 this year.

![Zhang Yuan in late February](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317184901JpPZYRIHdOnz2gMN.jpg-w600)

Wang Shuzhen lives in a room on the ground floor, east side, about thirty square meters, facing Zhang Yuan's main lane. Push open the courtyard door and enter, and first there is a modest skywell; stones and bricks piled on either side mark the boundaries, weeds and wildflowers grow in open spittoons, and only a passage about a meter wide is left. Wang Shuzhen's home is on the left side of the passage. In front of the skywell there was originally a main reception hall, but after several renovations it is hard to recognize. Walk farther in on the small patterned tiles laid years earlier (now fashionable again), and you reach the stairs to the second floor, rickety and trembling, on their last legs. At the end is the shared kitchen, known in Shanghainese as a "zaopi" room.

Because of fluid in her knees, Wang Shuzhen can hardly walk. She often lies down; her bed is placed beneath the north loft. Looking up, she can see the half-loft added in 1948, plus the original half: new wood joined to old wood, clearly distinct -- the old wood had turned white and the planks had cracked. If she gets up, she sits at the square table by the door, on a rattan chair with a cushion sewn from cotton or denim fabric.

![Beneath the north loft](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317195336GDePCA5zjy87T3lV.jpg-w600)

![The mirror opposite the shared kitchen](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317195336V72ykxAIwHso698C.png-w600)

In front of the square table is a large glass window. In some of Zhang Yuan's houses the windows were once fitted with colored patterned glass. Wang Shuzhen's home has none, only ordinary transparent glass. In the east-facing room, sunlight only comes in for a stretch in the morning. No curtains hang; only a half-length translucent white cloth is draped as a screen, like the booth partition in an old Western restaurant.

The glass window rises four or five meters; seen from the skywell it is enclosed by carved eaves. Originally the glass also ran straight down into the ground, but in 1976, when the youngest son got married, the lower half was removed: they worried the glass could not bear the weight, so wooden supports were added below. A lot happened in 1976, and it was roughly that year that the appearance of this room was fixed and did not change much again, until 2008, when the government paid to build a toilet with a flush toilet. The toilet has a modern white facade, like a shipping container jammed into the lanes by mistake; sooner or later it would be moved away.

### 1

These lane houses in Zhang Yuan were built in the 1920s. Before that, the site was Shanghai's largest private garden, "Zhang's Weichun Garden," also known as Zhang Family Garden. The merchant Zhang Shuhe bought the garden from a British trading house and, in the last years of the nineteenth century, kept renovating it; besides the garden, he added restaurants and an amusement park and opened it to the public. The highest building in Shanghai at the time, Arcadia Hall, was completed inside the garden. Even now, some historic-building preservationists have proposed restoring the facade of a certain building in Zhang Yuan to recreate an Arcadia Hall.

But the prosperity brought by Arcadia Hall did not last. In 1919 Zhang Family Garden changed hands; the original buildings were all demolished and replaced by row upon row of lane houses. Still, people habitually called the place "Zhang Family Garden."

![The old Zhang Yuan gate, from [Shanghai Archives Information Network](http://www.archives.sh.cn/shjy/scbq/201203/t20120313_5786.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317201650YZwX0obyCvezt3nH.png-w600)

![The exterior of Arcadia Hall, from [Shanghai Archives Information Network](http://www.archives.sh.cn/shjy/scbq/201203/t20120313_5786.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317201639T6PE2KWqjGM5Q0eI.png-w600)

The lanes and Wang Shuzhen have lived about as long. Wang Shuzhen was born in 1920 and adopted by a family that ran a traditional Chinese medicine pharmacy in Shanghai's Nanshi District. She rarely went out, occasionally accompanying her family to see southern opera, or to the Confucius Temple or the Great World. The Great World was then the most popular amusement venue; a few years earlier it had eclipsed Zhang Family Garden and driven it to close and sell. At home Wang Shuzhen often did needlework; she had studied for five or six years but preferred women's handiwork. If she went out for a wedding banquet, her father would call a tailor to the house to make her a new qipao. When the tailor came, he was invited to eat three meals, which saved quite a bit on labor costs.

She had several close girlfriends. At twelve they went to a photo studio; one wealthy young lady wore a Western-style dress and before long had "gone off to join the revolution."

In 1939, at nineteen, Wang Shuzhen married a husband three years her senior and planned to move with him to Hong Kong. Her husband, Ling Zhenhua, knew a little English; his uncle by marriage, Zhang Ruibo, was a senior manager at the Bank of Communications in Hong Kong and had found him a job at the same bank. The couple first held a wedding banquet in Shanghai at the Dafugui Restaurant at Laoximen, then a favorite of wealthy families. Her husband wanted Wang Shuzhen to take his surname, and for a long time afterward letters arriving at the house were addressed to "Ling Wang Shuzhen."

![Wang Shuzhen looking at old photographs](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317201230Z0TzkJeESKA5x2j4.jpg-w600)

In Hong Kong Ling Zhenhua's shirts were sent to shops to be washed and pressed. Life looked refined, but mostly because the climate was damp and hard to dry things. The Ling couple lived on Queen's Road West, in a large house divided into three rooms and sublet to three households. The ceilings were low and the partitions did not reach the top. One room housed more than five people. Wang Shuzhen did not like traveling far -- for example, taking the ferry to Macau -- but she occasionally went to amusement parks. She planned to stay in Hong Kong for good.

The plan was broken on the morning of December 8, 1941. Japan air-raided Kai Tak Airport and the Sham Shui Po Barracks. The Governor of Hong Kong announced surrender on February 25. The Bank of Communications, where Ling Zhenhua worked, was relocated to Chongqing. Wang Shuzhen was already pregnant; she returned to Shanghai alone.

Her husband Ling Zhenhua's family home in Shanghai was in Ruihua Fang on Shunchang Road; besides Wang Shuzhen, the household included two other women -- Ling Zhenhua's mother and "the old lady," Ling Zhenhua's grandmother. In 1939, not long after her grandson left for Hong Kong, the old lady had led a move; using gold bars she took over the lease on an east-side wing room from a household in Zhang Family Garden. At that time Zhang Family Garden still housed many wealthy families, but some had begun renting out ground-floor rooms. The old lady moved in one cabinet and one square table, both custom-made years earlier in Suzhou, along with a set of furniture made for her grandson Ling Zhenhua's wedding, including a redwood dressing table, all arranged in this one long room.

![The long room; the loft is above the wall with the clock, and the cabinet was custom-made in Suzhou](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317195552lLBWkYjJFgHr0ZQD.jpg-w600)

When Wang Shuzhen came back and saw it, she thought Zhang Family Garden was "nothing special." The new-style lane Jing'an Villa had already been completed in 1929, and the original Ruihua Fang had flush toilets shared by multiple households. But Zhang Family Garden had none. Men bathed in the skywell; women had to hide inside a large wooden tub to wash.

There were some fashionable places outside, such as Nanjing Barber, the largest barbershop in Shanghai, but Wang Shuzhen still did not go out much. She sat at the square table doing needlework; when she tired of sitting she went to the balcony to see what the people opposite were doing. There was little that required her hands. The family had hired a "Nanjing amah" to cook and wash clothes; she would hang the washed clothes on a short, low wooden pole in the skywell. Her mother-in-law was responsible for emptying the chamber pot; she considered the posture too undignified for a young woman.

The eldest son was born in 1941. Ling Zhenhua could not get back from Chongqing; he did not officially move back to Shanghai until 1947, when the eldest son was six. But if you ask Wang Shuzhen when she was happiest during all her years in Zhang Yuan, she will say without hesitation, "Raising a son -- happy, of course." She points to a black-and-white photograph in which the eldest son is two months old, and the shoes on his feet were made by her. At other times, looking at her old photographs, she uses the word "funny." "Isn't it funny? A person has actually turned into what I am now."

In the year the eldest son was born, the house flooded; heavy rain raised the water as high as the palm-fiber bed. The ground-floor houses in Zhang Yuan were low-lying and had no drainage; without some temporary residence to free up space to raise the ground level, similar flooding would continue until after 2000. Wang Shuzhen's family had not raised their floor. When the water was at its highest, the chamber pot had to be placed inside a foot-washing bucket so it would not tip over. For a day or two every year the elderly and children would hide in the loft, and Wang Shuzhen, wearing her husband's imported rubber boots, would send food up to the loft in a bamboo basket.

![The imported rubber boots placed in the skywell](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317195718xCKjIPkt0elu1Jwm.jpg-w600)

### 2

The year after Ling Zhenhua returned to Shanghai, the family would soon grow to eight people. Besides the two elderly women, the Nanjing amah, the couple, and the "precious eldest son," the eldest daughter had already been born, and the second son was due in early 1949. But they could not afford a larger house.

The loft was originally a storage space only half a story high; a person could not stand up straight up there. Ling Zhenhua planned to build out double the area. He was not the only one expanding his loft. Carpenters would habitually ask, "Want to add some flourish, make it fancy?" Wang Shuzhen said no, it was just for sleeping. The old lady managed the money, but Wang Shuzhen also knew the family was not that well off. The loft was eventually expanded to thirteen square meters; no bed was added -- they slept directly on wooden planks, as many as five people.

![The loft before the move; a movable ladder leads up to it](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201903172000005BV6foU7Z4Lc0Qul.jpg-w600)

As soon as the second son was born, Wang Shuzhen hung a ham outside the front door. On the night of May 26, crackling gunfire startled them through the night. At dawn the Nanjing amah came back from outside and said, "At the entrance to the lane are all PLA soldiers, sitting opposite No. 77 at the lane entrance." No. 77 was a single-family courtyard, home to a Nationalist officer's family. As soon as Shanghai was liberated they left in a hurry, leaving a carload of flour and clothes for anyone to take. The Nanjing amah took home a few cotton undergarments.

A subletter's family had originally lived on the second floor, four brothers. They were a refined household; in summer heat they would put up an awning in the skywell. But a few years later they moved away entirely. Because the government had decided on the "socialist transformation of private housing," houses would be "uniformly leased by the state." Zhang Yuan took in more less well-off residents, all squeezed into tiny single rooms. Even so, it could not ease Shanghai's severe housing problem at the time. Both old and new tenants began paying rent to the state. The tea table and benches originally in the ground-floor reception hall, used by the Ling family to receive guests, now became the place to pay rent and water fees.

Life was worse than when they had merely been short of money; it was even strained. After the government introduced the new "ranking and salary evaluation" wage standard, ordinary bank clerk Ling Zhenhua's salary and benefits were no longer enough for household expenses. In the past, the Bank of Communications had issued rice, cooking oil, soap, and whole cases of kerosene for kerosene stoves each month, and had covered employees' children's tuition through university. But by the 1950s only a few employees still received a "retained salary" as consolation after ranking and salary evaluation; Ling Zhenhua was not among them.

The Ling family could no longer afford the Nanjing amah. Ling Zhenhua left the Bank of Communications; he first went to Minhang to pour cement, coming home once a week. Then he went to the vegetable market. Later he worked as a cashier in a shop, sitting and collecting money. Wang Shuzhen wanted to go out to work to help with household expenses; "even working as a gatekeeper at an elementary school would be fine," but her mother-in-law was unwilling. In 1957 she bought a Wudi-brand sewing machine and planned to do sewing work. Her mother-in-law died that year; people said it was because she had been too grief-stricken by the death of Ling Zhenhua's sister the year before.

1958 is usually considered the beginning of the Great Leap Forward. The unrealistic steel-making campaign altered the facades of many houses. The iron frames on Wang Shuzhen's windows were completely removed, exposing the wooden structure. Shanghai was to "be built into the nation's largest industrial city," and the reception rooms in lane courtyards were occupied by production teams. Wang Shuzhen worked in a sewing production team in a reception room on Qinghai Road; she moved her newly bought sewing machine there. The Ling family's reception hall was turned into a hairdressing production team, leaving only a narrow passage in and out of the room.

![The skywell; on the left, the former reception hall of Ling Zhenhua's family](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317201031DxKvefz6IBpiTmQ9.jpg-w600)

These production teams had slogans like "Seven jiao eight fen, with the whole world in our hearts," which rolled off the tongue in Shanghainese. Wang Shuzhen's wages were three jiao a day. She made swimsuits from cloth, with fine handiwork, but once the elastic loosened the suits lost their stretch; "as soon as you came ashore the swimsuit would fall off." The sewing production team also made ready-to-wear garments for Hongxiang, a company famous in the 1930s and 1940s for making coats and satin capes for Soong Ching-ling and Princess Elizabeth of Britain.

The youngest daughter was born that year, but the cramped, strained atmosphere made her realize from the start that she was "not precious at all." The greatest favor she received was never having to sleep in the loft. The number of people living in the house was decreasing; the eldest son left Shanghai the next year to attend university in Beijing. Before leaving, the family took a group photo in front of the main reception hall of Zhang Yuan.

Everything inside and outside the house left Wang Shuzhen no time for her newborn daughter. The old lady was bedridden with illness before her death in 1964; she had Alzheimer's and would suddenly wake at night shouting "I'm starving." Wang Shuzhen went to the production team during the day, asked for leave at two in the afternoon to go home and get her some water, then returned to the production team until 4:30, rushing home to change the old lady's diapers while cooking. The hairdressing production team in the reception room had not yet finished work, so moving from the room to the stove area was inconvenient.

Yang Hongxing, who lived upstairs next door, would sometimes say, "Mrs. Ling, you have it hard." He would not have imagined that a few years later people would say the same of him.

![The main reception hall of Zhang Yuan seen from the second-floor kitchen](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317200207TIdpL03DEPr5txZ8.png-w600)

### 3

One day in 1967, Wang Shuzhen looked up and saw Yang Hongxing stick one foot out the window. During the Cultural Revolution he had jumped from the second floor and broken a leg. But at least he survived.

Yang Hongxing's father was a contractor who had bought several courtyards in Zhang Family Garden years earlier and left them to his three sons. Yang Hongxing was the second son. In the past he had driven a small car, parked at the lane entrance under a car shed. But starting in 1966, groups of teenage Red Guards loudly and aggressively ransacked homes, trucking private belongings away by the load. Local gazetteers record that 14 percent of households in Jing'an District were "ransacked."

Zhang Family Garden was quite unsettled in those years. Every day people were paraded through the lanes, beating gongs and shouting for an hour or two: "I am Xie Baiqing (approximate), guang~guang~, a counterrevolutionary, guang~guang~, gold, silver, treasures, murder weapons." "Then hand them over," people would say, and he would reply, "I really don't have any."

The Ling Zhenhua family did not run into major trouble during the Cultural Revolution. The eldest daughter worked as an accountant in Minhang for two years, then in 1968 went to a farm in Anhui; only four people remained at home. Thirty-some square meters for four people was not too spacious, but the household next door still criticized their housing as too large.

In 1976 the Cultural Revolution ended. Ling Zhenhua wrote a letter to the Jing'an District Weihai Road Housing Management Bureau, hoping to have the additional loft built in 1948 counted as a "private loft" rather than a "public loft," so that he could pay a few jiao less in rent each month. Ling Zhenhua found two witnesses; one was Yang Hongxing. After the fall, Yang Hongxing's family still lived on the second floor of the next courtyard. His wife lived in Zhang Family Garden until she was one hundred, dying in 2008.

Because the youngest son was getting married, the Ling family spent quite a bit of money in 1976. This was probably one reason Ling Zhenhua wrote the certification letter. The youngest son called in the housing management bureau and partitioned off an independent room on the south side as a wedding room, and added a door. The whole house was repainted. The windows were changed into short windows half wood and half glass. Wang Shuzhen had a set of wedding furniture refurbished at considerable expense and placed it in the youngest son's wedding room.

![The window of the south room](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317201135uwN8slJvQy657LFn.jpg-w600)

![The door of the independent south room](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317203042ChbwymUT3BGrgil1.jpg-w600)

The youngest son was the person who most wanted to move out of Zhang Yuan; though he was also the most invested in renovating it, he could never be satisfied. Two years later he even had a falling-out with Ling Zhenhua over it. Ling Zhenhua thought there was nothing wrong with the place; he was quite used to it. In 1985 the youngest son finally got a work-unit housing allocation and moved to Nanshi District as he wished. It was no impressive new house, and not large either.

The house suddenly felt a little empty; there was even room for a sofa in the reception hall. Only the unmarried eldest daughter lived with them. Ling Zhenhua and Wang Shuzhen moved their bed into the partitioned room, placed facing south. Since marrying, they had never had a separate bedroom. In the 1990s they also bought a television for the bedroom.

The youngest daughter had been assigned to Xinghuo Farm in Fengxian in 1976; she did not return until 1989, when she married and had a child. When she came back she found the reception hall had been converted into a telephone booth; people would come in from time to time to take calls, addressing Wang Shuzhen as "Old Mrs. Ling of Zhang Family Garden." The large "four-sided corridor building" at No. 84 Lane was gone; a component factory operating there in 1977 had caught fire, and by 1989 the site had become the Harbor Hotel.

She also discovered that from the year she left, the appearance of this room had been fixed. No one wanted to renovate it anymore, and no one had the means. "It never changed. It stayed like this for years."

When the youngest daughter married a Shanghainese she had met at the farm, their son Ye Yi was born in 1989. At the wedding Wang Shuzhen instructed her: "At your in-laws', do everything yourself; don't ask the man to do everything. Don't let people think your elders have no upbringing."

### 4

Shanghai's old-district redevelopment began in the 1990s. The government came up with the land-leasing method, bringing in foreign investment to help with urban renewal while itself helping to speed up relocation. In those years there were twenty major projects on West Nanjing Road. The most famous example is Plaza 66: when construction started in 1993, 146 households and 27 companies moved out in eight months. In the same year Cihou Beili on West Nanjing Road was also leased; the municipal government said it would complete the shantytown renovation there before 2000.

Perhaps Ling Zhenhua had thought about leaving Zhang Yuan then, and had even eagerly planned for it. Zhang Yuan is south of West Nanjing Road. In 1996 Ling Zhenhua saw the whole area ahead being relocated and told his youngest daughter and grandson Ye Yi to move their household registrations back to Zhang Yuan. "Your family is small, and you have a son; both of you returned from the farm, and you don't have the ability to buy a large apartment." At the time, relocation compensation area was calculated by household population. The Zhang Yuan room suddenly had five people registered.

That year the residents of the curved Central Building at the Wujiang Road intersection were also relocated, while the building itself was preserved; Metro Line 2 began construction, with one stop at West Nanjing Road Station. But there was still no news of relocation for Zhang Yuan.

![The curved Central Building at the Wujiang Road intersection, opposite the sign that reads "Zhang Yuan." Facing the picture, Line 2 Exits 1 and 4 are on the left, and Line 2 Exits 2 and 3 are on the right.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317200429ZjStLmkPuOlE1rRM.jpg-w600)

Because of the household registration change, Ye Yi was assigned to Minli Middle School, a junior high near Zhang Yuan, in 2001. For convenience he moved into this room and slept with his aunt beneath the loft. The conditions were terrible; at night rats leaped across the quilt with rustling sounds, and Ye Yi slept with his head covered. In the morning he sometimes went to empty the spittoon, but had to wait until the family had finished using it. Xiao Yang Shengjian had just opened its first shop on Wujiang Road; shengjian were one yuan a tael, and were Ye Yi's breakfast.

Ye Yi moved out of Zhang Yuan after graduating from junior high in 2004; many of his friends at Minli Middle School moved away in large numbers during the relocation of Dazhong Li in 2006.

Ling Zhenhua died in 2005, without seeing the demolition of Dazhong Li and the enormous uproar it caused, and without seeing the newly installed washroom in 2008, equipped with a flush toilet and shower. The washroom was placed in the partitioned south room, taking up about half the room, as if this were the most important thing in the house.

![The toilet with a flush toilet built by the government in 2008, with its white exterior](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317200550uGLPv26hkFMXyOi9.jpg-w600)

After Ling Zhenhua died, only Wang Shuzhen and the eldest daughter, who had never married, remained in the Zhang Yuan room. They sold the wooden bed and placed two small iron-frame beds beneath the loft. Only a little light came through the window at the head of the beds.

In 2010 Wang Shuzhen and her youngest daughter traveled to Hong Kong. She wanted to see where Ling Zhenhua had worked. Passing Victoria Park, she recognized that this place had not been a park in the past but a river.

Wang Shuzhen has an impressive memory. At the end of February, packing things with her son and daughter, she could almost recount the origin of every item, which made her even more reluctant to discard them. She put away the two qipaos from her wedding; only someone with as slender a figure as hers could still wear them.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317202016TKgStob4qycmZieG.jpg-w600)

![A cushion sewn from denim fabric](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190317202014J7vbKPtVgDc1UN5W.jpg-w600)

Perhaps she too had thought about leaving Zhang Yuan, but Wang Shuzhen rarely spoke of it, and besides, the moment came too late. Moving is suffering for an old person. The resettlement housing offered by the government was on Gaojing Road in Baoshan District, the Pujiang Base in Minhang District, north of Sheshan in Songjiang District, Sanlin in Pudong New Area, and north of Xujing in Qingpu District. Wang Shuzhen chose cash compensation; based on construction area, the government offered about 160,000 to 180,000 yuan per square meter. That amount was enough to buy a place of the same size downtown, but if she wanted an elevator for easy daily access, things became tricky.

Wang Shuzhen has tentatively decided to move to her eldest son's home in Zhoupu. The eldest daughter will go with her as before, taking care of her daily needs. The old cabinet and square table will also be moved there. The eldest son is seventy-eight this year; he was born in Zhang Yuan, but from the age of seventeen he was gone from this house.

Her grandson Ye Yi is a fund manager. After university he worked at an investment bank in Hong Kong and returned to Shanghai a few years ago. Standing beside Wang Shuzhen, he announced a determined plan: after Zhang Yuan's renovation is complete, he intends to rent this room and open a cafe or whatever.
