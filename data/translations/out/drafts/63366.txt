Why Did Sweden Become a Hotbed for Premium Video Games?
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
Sweden has built a game-development ecosystem across the country. How individual creative forces accumulate and ignite within it may offer some lessons for places starved of creativity.
@@QD_BODY@@
Sweden has built a game-development ecosystem across the country. How individual creative forces accumulate and ignite within it may offer some lessons for places starved of creativity.

According to a report by the Swedish Games Industry, [at least one in ten people worldwide has played a game made in Sweden](https://dataspelsbranschen.se/media/152276/gamedeveloperindex%202015.pdf).

This Nordic country, with a population of just 10 million and tucked away on the Scandinavian Peninsula, ranks in the global top 10 by game-industry output. It produces acclaimed games of all scales and styles, from the casual *Candy Crush* and all-ages *Minecraft* to AAA titles *Battlefield* and *Tom Clancy's The Division*, and on to indie games *Goat Simulator*, *Hotline Miami*, and the mobile indie *Year Walk*—a diversity and quality that holds its own against the United States, whose market is 20 times larger. EA DICE, Microsoft Mojang, Ubisoft Massive, Paradox, THQ Nordic, and Bethesda's MachineGames—studios familiar to players—are dotted across the country, grabbing headlines with new releases every so often. Oh, and the country even produced the biggest gaming streamer, PewDiePie.

![[Minecraft (Mojang studio) PC sales have surpassed 154 million worldwide, with another 150 million downloads in China](https://www.gamesindustry.biz/articles/2019-04-03-minecraft-on-pc-surpasses-30m-sales)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2019042822510304bQUpRlq5XKfuvM.jpeg-w600)

From the standpoint of individual works, people usually don't overemphasize a game's national identity. Yet Sweden's game industry keeps turning out hits, its developer numbers keep soaring, and new studios keep springing up—none of which happens without regional reasons. How individual creative forces accumulate and ignite within this system may offer some lessons for places starved of creativity.

QDaily spoke with game-industry professionals based in Stockholm, Skövde, and Malmö. We will try to explain, across three articles, how this "small" country in a land of ice and snow gave rise to a gaming boom that has spread around the world.

### 1. Long Nights, Demoscene, and the Safety Net

**“Long, dark nights.”**

When asked what unique advantages Sweden gives them, almost every developer mentions the sudden shortening of daylight in winter. Near the Arctic Circle, Sweden's December daylight shrinks to around six hours—fitting the etymology of Scandinavia, "skadino" ("darkness"). They can't help but smile as they tell you that these long nights are perfect for staying indoors to play games and make games.

Bring up the long nights and the conversation naturally shifts to ancestors who survived the bitter cold, their experiences somewhat like a survival-horror game. Per Micael Nyberg, who works for The Game Incubator in central Sweden's Skövde, explains that this land lay buried under glaciers for a long time; even after human tribes appeared, the climate remained harsh and inhospitable. With resources scarce, people had to maximize resource utilization as much as possible, and creativity was spurred by the desire to survive, forcing people to become better problem-solvers. The extra indoor thinking time that long nights brought also refined design after design and solved more problems. This habit of thinking things through may be etched into survivors' genes and is one reason Swedish design industries are so developed.

A more personal explanation is that, although Swedes were Christianized in the 11th century and Protestantism later replaced Catholicism after the Reformation in the 16th century, Swedes have never been all that close to Christianity—they originally worshipped the Norse gods and revered nature, and modern Swedes are more inclined to treat "nature" as their religion. In a country where forests cover 64% of the land, people are exposed to the wild in all sorts of ways, and creativity is to some extent shaped by nature. If you look at the folklore and distinctly Nordic natural settings in Swedish games, you'll understand what "nature shaping creativity" means.

![The game based on Swedish folklore, [*Unforgiving*](http://unforgivinggame.com/), is full of Nordic elements](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190428224748K73jONEycvqMeCAU.png-w600)

Of course, these somewhat mystical reasons probably can't explain the reputation and revenue that Swedish games have rapidly accumulated more recently. The [*2018 Swedish Game Developer Report*](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5a61edb7a803bb7a65252b2d/t/5bc097ec104c7bc0b225a67d/1539348506035/GDI_2018_ENG.pdf) presents a string of rising figures: compared with 2012, game revenue in 2017 had more than tripled, reaching 1.53 billion euros, total profits exceeded 445 million euros, and player reach surpassed 1 billion. Over ten years, the number of companies and employees had tripled and quadrupled respectively, far outpacing the global average.

In terms of output, EA DICE's *Battlefield V* and *Star Wars Battlefront II*, Ubisoft Massive's *Tom Clancy's The Division 2*, MachineGames' *Wolfenstein II: The New Colossus*, Hazelight Studios' *A Way Out*, Tarsier Studios' *Little Nightmares*, ColdWood Interactive's *Unravel*, Coffee Stain Studios' *Satisfactory*, and Avalanche Studios' *Rage 2*—this string of titles over the past couple of years has more or less made it onto media lists of most-anticipated games, and each has had its moment in the spotlight, whether from a big company of several hundred people or a tiny indie team of a dozen or so.

Almost every Swedish studio is expanding, hiring, and agonizing over competing with other companies for design talent. The new positions added in 2017 alone (1,047) were double the 2016 figure (582), and full-time employees at game companies exceeded 5,300, not counting independent developers scattered here and there. Game development in Sweden is gradually becoming a very popular career choice.

Sweden's hardware provision looks almost ideal.

Looking back at the 1990s, the Swedish government's foresight paved the way for a generation of geeks. Today, every Swedish citizen under 40 has more or less grown up with a personal computer. Starting in the 1990s, recognizing the importance of computers, [the government introduced a tax break favoring companies that provided home computers to all employees](https://www.theatlantic.com/business/archive/2017/09/sweden-startups/541413/)—"all" meaning everyone from management down to toilet cleaners; every employee was allocated a computer. The government also ran subsidized computer-purchase programs, so any family with children could get a subsidy to take a computer home from a store, and schools were required to teach computer courses. Around the same time, they began investing in faster internet services, so by the 1990s Sweden had effectively put everyone online. By 2018, household and corporate 4G broadband penetration had reached 99.99%, with an average speed of 22.5 MB/s, exceeding the US average of 18 MB/s.

These Swedish youngsters who grew up with PCs and even the internet from childhood, once interested in design or programming, would inevitably join the local Demoscene before long. This was a spontaneous gathering born out of the European geek subculture of the 1970s and 1980s, organized around competition and exchange: individuals or teams made demos to compete in programming ability, visual-design ability, even soundtrack ability. These demos couldn't really be called "games"; they were more like small programs that generate multimedia interactive content, ultimately realizing some function or creating an interesting electronic artwork. The contest values innovation rather than "copycatting," and encourages exchange and the hard work of solving problems rather than asking others for help.

![[Assembly 2002 in Finland](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Assembly_demo_party_2002_panorama.jpg)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190428223735e0sgOZuIF6ajY8Cl.jpg-w600)

Large-scale Demoscene events are called demoparties, usually week-long offline gatherings with many participants. There is plenty of time each day for socializing, and at night people gather to show their work. In the early 1990s, the most famous and best-attended parties included Denmark's The Party, Finland's Assembly, Norway's The Gathering, and Sweden's DreamHack—the Nordic countries' computer benefits and "long nights" clearly fostered a more active Demoscene culture. Although these parties later attracted more and more "tourists" sniffed at by originators—people who came not to enter demo competitions but to play multiplayer online games—many of today's game developers first became interested in demo-making after attending one of these parties.

Nyberg, who walked me through this history, is a veteran game developer himself before he took charge of business development at the incubator, and before that a geek teenager who regularly took part in the Demoscene.

Out of the Demoscene also came one of the most important companies in Swedish game history. Four university students from Växjö met there and formed a demo group called The Silent. Their starting point was making demos for the Amiga, which they then refined into a real game, *Pinball Dreams*. In 1992, the members of The Silent founded Digital Illusion CE, whose name DICE is now known worldwide. Acquired by EA in 2006, the company—which appears in game-industry reports almost every year, is the developer of the *Battlefield* series and the maker of the Frostbite engine—posted revenue of 135 million euros last year, second only to Microsoft Mojang and King, and its 722 employees made it the largest in the Swedish games industry.

![Ranking of Sweden's largest game companies by revenue and number of employees](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190428223651ywJ4uLecI89q1TQH.png-w600)

It was precisely these social gatherings for honing skills and the hacker culture of that era that laid the groundwork for the tech startup wave of the 1990s; many teams born from it later became famous and grew into big companies that contribute the bulk of tax revenue.

That startup wave was also a product of social reform. After the housing-and-stock-market bubble burst at the end of the 1980s and start of the 1990s, triggering a financial crisis, the government launched "product market reforms" to revive the economy. It deregulated industries such as taxis, railways, electricity, and telecom, and the Competition Act enacted in 1993 also restricted large corporate mergers, aiming to eliminate monopolies and encourage new companies to enter the market. At the same time, the corporate tax rate was slashed from 52% to 30% (today it's 22%). Before the reforms, the government favored established companies over entrepreneurs, who had to pay both personal and corporate income taxes, while the former had many ways to reduce taxes. The post-reform environment could not be said to favor entrepreneurs more, but it at least made the market more dynamic and made obtaining licenses much easier for new companies.

The 1980s rules protecting domestic companies from foreign acquisition were also relaxed. Sweden opened up to overseas markets, and more and more foreign capital acquired mature local startups. DICE, Massive, and Mojang were successively bought at high prices by EA, Vivendi/Activision/Ubisoft, and Microsoft, gaining increasingly solid support; expanding companies added new jobs every year.

That reform also cut the top marginal income tax rate from 85% to 57%. Although this is still a high figure for the middle class, Swedes still enjoy free health care and free education and other generous welfare benefits, and they don't see much to complain about.

This social safety net built from the welfare system is also considered a necessary condition for Swedes' bold innovation. One survey shows that among Swedes aged 18–64, 65% feel confident starting a company locally; the corresponding figure in the US is 47%.

Swedes say such a system "allows mistakes." Young people can choose any career path they like without fear of ending up on the street. Even if a startup fails, it's not the end of the world—just switch jobs. Even the minimum standard of living is relatively high compared with other countries; "you'd have to try very hard to fall out of this safety net; if someone ends up on the street, it's more like a self-determined choice born of stubborn will." In short, it's a system with a high tolerance for failure.

With hardware and social welfare as foundations, Swedes are born into more and freer career options. And an interlocking game-industry system that gradually took shape through trial and improvement naturally draws in citizens who want to work in games. When they discover that every step they take comes with guidance, advice, even hardware and funding help, and that they can smoothly integrate into local game communities, it's easy to understand the convenience and joy they feel in this industry.

### 2. Malmö: A System That Is Fluid Yet Stable

**“As a Swedish resident, you can have everything here.”**

![[Malmö](https://www.goodfreephotos.com/sweden/malmoe/city-of-Malm%C3%B6-under-the-clouds.jpg.php), a city in southern Sweden](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190428230442bH2XKym5CsBAS3eL.jpg-w600)

On March 29, Eliana Oikawa, chief operating officer of DevHub, saw off the last group of Avalanche employees from the building in downtown Malmö.

Avalanche made its name with the *Just Cause* series and in recent years worked with Warner Bros. on the *Mad Max* adaptation; in May the wasteland game *Rage 2* would be released. Founded in 2003, the company now has more than 300 employees, with headquarters in Stockholm and branches in New York and Malmö. Last year it rented a large office on the second floor of DevHub, and now, because of studio expansion, it has had to move elsewhere.

![A poster wall for Avalanche downstairs at DevHub, photographed by the reporter](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904282256347Ix5tkjAzdUuiry6.jpg-w600)

DevHub is a co-working space and event venue under [Game Habitat](http://www.gamehabitat.se/), a nonprofit founded five years ago by Martin Walfisz, who served as CEO of Massive for 12 years. Its purpose is to sustain the game-developer community in southern Sweden, share information, keep communication open, make sure everyone is informed about industry news, and give people opportunities to connect with one another.

In the years that followed, it filled the gap left by the absence of a union in the games industry, taking on an organizational role: it began hosting all kinds of free offline meetups, inviting industry professionals to give lectures, and allowing anyone interested in game design to sit in. Oikawa notes that Malmö's relatively mild climate, status as a transport hub, developed education, and inclusive environment have led more and more game companies to settle here, and developers from various countries have gradually converged on the city. Cultural differences, however, can leave these new arrivals temporarily without a sense of belonging, and Game Habitat is precisely the place that tries to keep them. It gives game immigrants opportunities to build long-term relationships with peers outside of work and integrate into the local community; it provides not only information but also a mutually supportive way of life.

Teaching, exchange, meetups, business discussions—all kinds of social activities are hosted there, making it deservedly a community hub. University students, newcomers eager to enter the industry, veteran company employees, and people between jobs can all find what they need here. It is both fluid and quite stable—imagine an indie developer attending an event here: they can easily connect with a company to discuss collaboration, or find trusted development partners and hit it off to start their own company. All sorts of changing factors surge through it, yet talent rarely leaks elsewhere.

Most of Game Habitat's event funding comes from the government; starting in its second year it began receiving government subsidies. In fact, Malmö has always been friendly to the games industry: driven by Massive, the city's largest company, games generate a sizable chunk of revenue, and the expansion of game companies creates more jobs every year. In recent years the municipal government has been keen to make games part of the city's calling card, which also makes it look more appealing to young voters.

![The DevHub front desk, photographed by the reporter](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190428225808VyUYe6Tdrax4o38w.jpg-w600)

This intention is quite serious. Malmö's tourism bureau even has a dedicated esports coordinator. Paul Peterson-Rebelo holds this one-of-a-kind job in the world: on one hand he connects Malmö with major game companies and tries to bring in heavyweight esports events, and on the other he stays in touch with esports communities and lobbies city councilors to support event hosting and allocate city funds. Unlike China, where esports sponsors can be secured fairly easily, Malmö has high requirements for sponsors: their company values must align with the values the city identifies with. So for the most part, the staging of these major tournaments and the corresponding community activities still rely on government grants.

Back to Game Habitat. From the start it put forward a plan to expand community membership as much as possible and establish a permanent venue as soon as possible. A healthy community undoubtedly makes the local industry run more smoothly, talent less likely to leave, and revenue growth increasingly positive—the municipal government can certainly see that potential. From then until now, Game Habitat has indeed achieved its goal of membership exceeding 1,000 and, since last October, has had its own building in the city center. The building is now a weekend gathering place and a co-working space for game developers; DevHub's rents are still competitive in Malmö's startup scene. The Nordic Game conference each May is organized by [Nordic Game Community](https://nordicgame.com/), which is also based on the second floor, keeping it closest to the developers.

Another part of Game Habitat's funding comes from membership fees. Local game companies voluntarily become members to support these nonprofit activities, choosing from different annual-fee tiers. The four tiers are aimed at startups that haven't released a game, rookie companies, mature companies, and highly successful companies. King, Massive, Tarsier, Nordisk Film, Coffee Stain, and Frictional Games, which we interviewed last year, are all well-known members.

![A display wall of game artwork in the DevHub lobby](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190428225526E8xGHODvi7X0bQtT.jpg-w600)

One name on this member list that differs from the others is [The Game Assembly (TGA)](https://www.thegameassembly.com/faq-vanliga-fragor-om-tga/). It is a free 2–3-year game-design education program launched by Malmö University; in a 2017 evaluation it stood out from 166 schools in 38 countries and was considered the world's second-best game-development program. It has since expanded to Stockholm and the northern city of Umeå. It teaches programming, 3D production, and level design; because instruction is entirely in Swedish, it also offers free language classes. Students are trained exactly the way real studios develop real games, so that after graduation they can immediately fit into local Malmö game companies.

That's right—it's a bit like a football club's youth academy, and also a talent reserve base for Malmö game companies. Senior developers or even founders from any game company may walk into the classroom at any time to teach on-site—you'd be hard pressed to get that experience anywhere else; in any other city, the people you could reach would be limited to company PR. Because it is practical enough, 99% of students have already received offers before the course ends, snapped up by Malmö companies competing for them.

Imagine you are a newcomer to game development in Malmö. You know nothing about making games right now, but a clear path still lies before you: apply to study at TGA, get a company offer or start your own startup (the city also has the well-known tech startup incubator Minc), work in DevHub's co-working space or rent a desk or office at relatively low rent, take part in Game Habitat's series of free activities to accumulate knowledge and connections, and look for publishers at the Nordic Game conference and expand your company (recruiting more community members at gatherings).

It's hard to fall outside the community with no support. "As a Swedish resident, you can have everything here."

This system is unique to Malmö in Sweden, but different regions have the systems best suited to them, which has produced thriving clusters of game talent across the country: Umeå in the north; Stockholm and nearby Uppsala in central-southern Sweden; Skövde to the south; Gothenburg to the west. Even if a developer keeps job-hopping and relocating, he or she can still fall into one well-fitting local system after another, like changing shoes, without any jarring sense of dislocation.

![From the [*2018 Swedish Game Developer Report*](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5a61edb7a803bb7a65252b2d/t/5bc097ec104c7bc0b225a67d/1539348506035/GDI_2018_ENG.pdf)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190428230303rOEmAG9CjgZobRhP.png-w600)

Of course, the mechanics of their communities differ slightly, and they may even exchange gentle complaints—people think Stockholm is too big, its companies tend to be large, its developer community isn't as close-knit as Malmö's, and it's hard to meet founders in person; Skövde has a university town and a free incubator, but "there are few people and the place is small"; Malmö has a community to be proud of, yet Game Habitat "actually charges developers." But these complaints are usually followed by "they're doing a pretty good job; it's worth talking to them."

Although the communities operate differently, their emphasis on and serious attitude toward game education remains constant. This is vividly illustrated in Skövde, which covers 20 square kilometers (about the size of Shanghai's Xuhui District) and has a population of about 50,000. The local university and science park take pride in its "mini" size—small as it really is, it is a main training base for Swedish game talent and the host of the annual Sweden Game Conference in October. How this miniature city became a game-industry hub because of "education," and how the viral hit *Goat Simulator* was born here, will be covered in the next article.
