IKEA is finally going to start doing e-commerce in China.

The news drew the most attention at IKEA's fiscal 2017 press conference on August 16. For many years, IKEA had been the staunchest supporter of the offline retail model among large retailers. "You can do business on the web, but it reduces the number of people who come into the store, and that way you lose some additional business." With that argument, IKEA founder Ingvar Kamprad rejected a proposal to run e-commerce back in 2008.

In early 2014, Peter Agnefjall, IKEA's then-new CEO, set a target of "growing online sales to 5 billion euros by 2020," but [he remained hesitant about the e-commerce business](http://www.wsj.com/articles/SB10001424052702303277704579347942836197338): "By then, the vast majority of IKEA's sales should still be completed by our offline stores."

This caution about online business stood in sharp contrast to the relentless expansion of physical stores offline. The question "why doesn't IKEA do e-commerce" was raised more and more frequently afterward, and IKEA's explanations were varied, but boiled down to roughly this: with sales growing the way they were, e-commerce was dispensable.

A year later, Peter Agnefjall completely relented. [In an interview with The New York Times](http://www.nytimes.com/2015/01/29/business/as-profit-slows-ikea-notes-need-to-move-online.html?_r=1), he said: "We're not the earliest adopters [of the internet], but we've thought this through pretty well. We understand this is not a trend, but a huge shift."

Ireland, the Czech Republic, Australia... more and more countries joined the e-commerce pilots, and the China region began following headquarters' direction too. Despite being the fastest-growing market, it is in fact part of this grand plan.

Over the past few years, whenever Zhu Changlai, IKEA China's retail president, was asked "when will online shopping launch domestically," her answers were commitments along the lines of "planning for online services is synchronized with IKEA globally," along with the hope of "the sooner the better."

At the press conference, her introduction to this pilot was simple and vague. All the information known is limited to this: online shopping will be trialed at the end of August, with operations and delivery limited to the Shanghai area; after the pilot data is analyzed, it is expected to expand to more Chinese cities.

You can still see IKEA's caution on this matter.

And at IKEA's annual internal employee meeting after the press conference, "Transformation" became a topic mentioned repeatedly, with e-commerce regarded as one of its components.

Combined with the contents of this press conference, we pulled together the relevant information and raised 10 questions, trying to answer: what kind of e-commerce will IKEA's e-commerce actually be?

### Why is IKEA only now doing e-commerce in China?

In the discussion above, we already answered the part of this question closely tied to timing: as part of a multinational group, IKEA China's business is highly dependent on the unified direction of the Swedish headquarters; on advancing e-commerce, they likely maintained a preparation pace similar to other markets, but when it comes to actually going live, they had no priority whatsoever.

Moreover, if IKEA still holds the idea that "offline sales are growing fast enough, so there's little need to develop e-commerce," then the Chinese market, which looks far from saturated, indeed doesn't much need e-commerce to help boost performance.

In the fiscal year ending this August, more than 80 million customer visits came to IKEA's 20 stores across China, buying away over 11.7 billion yuan worth of goods, with sales up 19.4% year on year; in the previous fiscal year, that growth rate was 18%.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818230009tZyN3qRerlQoMpET.jpg-w600)

But rising sales and foot traffic don't mean IKEA has no worries in China. A simple calculation reveals that IKEA is not making more money from each customer: in the two most recent fiscal years, IKEA China's average transaction value held at around 140 yuan.

For a company that sells furniture, home products and all kinds of food at the same time, this number is clearly on the low side. And given Chinese consumers' love for IKEA restaurants, that number could shrink further.

### What will the experience of buying IKEA online be like?

In an interview with QDaily, IKEA China gave a slightly more detailed explanation of this matter:

When the time comes, if you want to buy IKEA products online, you'll still select items on the official website, and logistics and delivery will still rely on existing suppliers; the goods sold will be picked from the warehouse of the physical store nearest to you, with online and offline prices identical; and the launch of e-commerce will not mean expanding the range of product categories sold online or speeding up the pace of new product launches.

The same website, the same products, the same prices and the same delivery system — this should satisfy the kind of "consistency" IKEA emphasizes for the consumer experience.

Previously, on the subject of buying IKEA online, Zhu Changlai said: "Everyone is still figuring out how to achieve consistency between online and offline, consistency of customer experience, including consistency between stores, consistency of product and service experience... Chinese consumers' expectations of it will be higher than those in other countries."

### Has IKEA finally realized just how enthusiastic Chinese consumers are about buying furniture online?

In [an observation of the Chinese furniture market](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/18586.html) by QDaily at the end of 2015, we found that this industry had no intention of standing still and waiting for IKEA —

Those "basic" furniture items that emphasize value for money were already selling well on e-commerce platforms like Taobao; and the brands that pursue design and quality were all wondering how, building on the consumer education IKEA had already completed, they could use the internet to do higher value-added business.

On Taobao, China's largest e-commerce site, home furnishing products have been the best-selling category on the platform after clothing since 2014. At present, this category can already solve the most critical problems for furniture e-commerce — delivery and installation — and even has a relatively independent sales platform, "Jiyoujia," offering nearly 700,000 products.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818230033ZNFzHYwDdrq0Cve1.jpg-w600)

But official statistics put the entire building and home-decoration industry at a market size of over 4 trillion yuan, of which furniture accounts for more than 700 billion yuan, while internet penetration is only 3%. People willing to place furniture orders directly online are not necessarily just solving for value for money — they want somewhat better product quality and a better shopping experience.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818230045tCbLzD6UTM0Wls9K.jpg-w600)

This reflects, to some extent, IKEA's hesitation. When IKEA China confirmed to QDaily in April this year that it would launch online shopping in the second half of the year, it said its Swedish headquarters had finally realized "how many internet users China has, and how much demand consumers have."

But judging from the data, the categories these enthusiastic internet users currently favor most are still not furniture.

### IKEA has many stores in the Yangtze River Delta, so why is the pilot only in Shanghai?

Looking at the distribution of IKEA stores in China, this is also a puzzling question:

In the Yangtze River Delta region, IKEA has 8 stores in total, 3 of them in Shanghai. IKEA China said Shanghai was chosen as the pilot city because of the maturity of this market — "the base is relatively large, and resources are relatively plentiful": beyond its 3 large-format stores, the city also has 2 IKEA logistics centers, 1 distribution center, over 10,000 employees, and IKEA China's headquarters.

But around Shanghai, IKEA also covers cities like Wuxi, Suzhou and Ningbo quite well; and this year in Wenzhou, IKEA opened the country's first "order and collection point," hoping to handle local consumers' growing shopping demand with a "showroom + order center" model.

Unlike traditional IKEA stores, which require buying land and building from scratch, order and collection points lease space from existing stores, sit in mature commercial districts in city centers, increase contact with consumers, and greatly reduce the cost and time of opening a store, while serving both purchasing and pickup functions.

IKEA hopes this new retail channel will further improve IKEA's money-making efficiency — although compared with traditional furniture marketplaces like Red Star Macalline, IKEA, dominated by 30,000–50,000-square-meter large-format stores, already fully qualifies as "small profits, quick turnover."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818230106LXWbRfPYI0xrqlk7.jpg-w600)

Seen this way, IKEA piloting e-commerce in Shanghai first very likely mainly reflects this Swedish company's characteristic steadiness and conservatism.

Uncertainty certainly exists as well. IKEA China said the team currently responsible for e-commerce is still very small, and that it may need to gradually work through difficulties underestimated at the outset through a period of piloting in the Shanghai market. These difficulties could include the number of orders the logistics team can handle, after-sales service capability, and the shopping experience on the official website.

### What will IKEA sell online?

IKEA is world-famous for flat-pack furniture that is cost-effective and easy to transport, and its logistics system is mainly built for these large furniture items.

And the goods sold in this e-commerce pilot will cover the vast majority of categories already in offline stores (those explicitly excluded from online sales for now include green plants, IKEA food, and made-to-measure items like curtains and rugs that require measurements).

But judging from the various "IKEA daigou" (buying-agent) shops on Taobao, once the e-commerce business opens, it may still be those small items that have the most market —

In the Taobao daigou shop with the highest sales volume, this month's three best-selling items are a children's high chair at 108 yuan, a small square table at 39 yuan, and a drinking glass at 2.9 yuan each.

After studying all the product listings on IKEA's official website, QDaily also found that for large furniture like beds, tables and chairs, cabinets or washbasins, the total number of individual items still falls short of small things like mirrors and hooks.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160818230129KJtlPOWGDr4iaCTL.jpg-w600)

### Will IKEA later open a flagship store on Tmall or JD.com?

In an interview with QDaily, IKEA China said it is "very interested" in e-commerce platforms like Tmall, "but for now there are no plans to cooperate with any other third-party platforms."

In other markets around the world where e-commerce has already launched, IKEA has never cooperated with any other e-commerce website either. They simply added a "shopping cart" icon to the official website — where all products and prices can already be seen — plus the payment and logistics/delivery half of the functionality.

So the feeling of buying IKEA products online will be more like buying a phone on Apple's official website (except that when IKEA launches new products, it presumably won't draw the massive traffic of a new iPhone release).

After Steve Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, he revamped the company's official website and launched e-commerce functionality. By the end of 2011, that website had become the second-largest online retailer in the US after Amazon, contributing over 10% of Apple's sales.

In the Chinese market, IKEA has also made some more localized attempts — such as a WeChat official account. In fiscal 2015, this account, less than two years old, attracted more than 3 million subscribers; the specially discounted item of the week could also only be discovered through the WeChat official account's push notifications.

Imagine: before e-commerce existed, consumers could only see a push notification and then go to an offline IKEA store to buy; afterward, that process will become somewhat smoother.

### If you just buy small items, will IKEA offer free shipping? Same-day delivery?

As we said earlier, IKEA's e-commerce business is not as much like "e-commerce" as you might imagine. So experiences like free shipping or same-day delivery are unlikely to materialize in this pilot.

Currently in offline stores, IKEA offers two services: in-store pickup and home delivery. Pickup costs a low, flat 60 yuan per order; home delivery is priced according to the quantity of goods and the distance of transport, with a minimum of 59 yuan. When buying a large quantity of bulky items, this pricing can seem like a good deal, and IKEA can also use it to sell consumers more goods. But as mentioned earlier, IKEA's best-selling products are currently still small home items. This will become a contradiction between IKEA's vision and consumer demand.

As for logistics, IKEA China said that on one hand it will rely on existing suppliers (that is, logistics companies skilled at transporting large items), and on the other hand it will actively cooperate with third parties to solve the "last mile" problem. So this may mean delivery of small items will be somewhat faster.

In addition, IKEA seems to have forgotten its enormous membership system here. Amazon, which originally had only the concept of "users," used free shipping and faster delivery to turn Prime membership into a profitable program, but IKEA has not done this.

### Detailed photos, buyer shows, comment sections... will these standard e-commerce features exist?

Similar to the previous question, judging from IKEA's e-commerce trials in other countries, there will be no clearer images, open comment sections, or buyer photos showing real setups — because that involves more complex renovation of the company website.

If anything could roughly serve as a supplement to buyer shows, it would probably still be IKEA's offline showrooms and the product catalog that can be browsed online. They remain keen on the approach of "letting consumers experience it in IKEA's offline stores first, then go online."

### As a standalone e-commerce business, how big can IKEA's operation get?

Over the past year, more than 67 million visits were made in China to this website that has no shopping function. It's hard to say to what degree consumers' spending enthusiasm will be ignited once online shopping fully opens up in the Chinese market.

One reference point is Germany, IKEA's largest overseas market. In fiscal 2013, IKEA Germany's online sales reached 92 million euros — only 2% of total sales, but with year-on-year growth of 27%, meaning it attracted more consumers' attention.

For IKEA as a whole, e-commerce's contribution to sales is not yet that obvious: according to group CEO Peter Agnefjaell speaking at the end of 2015, online sales contributed over 1 billion euros in turnover to the company in fiscal 2015, but that was still only 3% of total sales. He hopes that proportion can rise to 10% — 5 billion euros — by 2020.

### With e-commerce, will IKEA stop opening so many stores, or stop going to smaller cities?

IKEA has emphasized that it will not slow the pace of physical store expansion in China or in global markets. In the coming fiscal 2017, three new stores will still open in China.

And after the order and collection point in Wenzhou opens, IKEA's brand presence in China may become more diverse. More and more smaller, more agile stores of this kind will appear around areas where IKEA already has large stores.

Compared with the landing and further rollout of e-commerce, whether IKEA China can open 34 stores in China as planned by 2020 may be the more urgent matter.
