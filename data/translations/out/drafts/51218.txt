Farewell!

The writer Li Ao died of a brain tumor at 10:59 a.m. on March 18 at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. He was 83.

The news stunned every circle. But Li Ao had long since given everyone advance notice of his death.

In February 2017 Li Ao learned that he had a brain tumor and less than three years to live. That June, through his agent Zheng Naijia, he released a public farewell letter, which read: "I now have to take six steroid pills every day, so my body has become like a battlefield. I've recently been hospitalized twice with acute pneumonia. I am in great pain; it feels like hell is not far from me. In my life I have cursed many people and hurt many people; I have countless enemies and few friends. The doctor told me: you have at most three years left — whatever you want to do or get done, get on with it!"

This was not the first time Li Ao had come close to the line between life and death. In 2003 he was diagnosed with prostate cancer. Facing the illness, he met it with equanimity; on the eve of the operation, Li Ao was still reciting poetry in a merry, chatty mood.

On the day he made such a high-profile public farewell letter, Li Ao also laid out his plans: he said he would start a new show on the mainland, *Goodbye, Li Ao* (《再见李敖》), and intended to finish editing *The Complete Works of Li Ao* (《李敖大全集》). Before bidding farewell to the world, he wanted to gather all his enemies and all his friends in one place, have everyone speak plainly, and settle the tumult of a lifetime.

Speaking of Li Ao's life, everything about it was indeed accompanied by controversy. Every conversation, every thought, every act of his was magnified and scrutinized by the media — even his private life.

A reporter once asked Li Ao whether he knew he had cursed more than 3,000 people. He said it pained him, and quoted a line from the British writer Oscar Wilde: "Be careful in choosing your enemies." He added: "Enemies are to be chosen, because some enemies are rotten — they don't deserve to be your enemies! The most painful thing about coming to Taiwan is that the enemies I ran into were all rotten ones! I couldn't choose! So I swept them all up in one net and cut them down, every last one."

Arrogant, unruly — no adjective fits Li Ao better.

Among his few close friends, Chen Wenxi was the best known. In December 2017, Chen Wenxi posted on Weibo: "Everything is counting down." In a later interview she said, "Li Ao transcended this era, so only when Li Ao is truly dead will Taiwan truly want to commemorate him." Today, when she learned of Li Ao's death, she would not say much, replying with just two words: "Grief. Sigh."

For many younger viewers, Li Ao was that man in the red jacket and black-rimmed sunglasses, holding forth nonstop in front of the camera about the affairs of the day, every so often throwing in a "careful, or I'll sue you." Although Li Ao's lawsuits usually ended in defeat, he believed that "the point of a lawsuit is not the outcome but the process."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803181740079gUt3n8Ssrq4clPp.png-w600)

Some critics described him with the term "cultural enfant terrible," holding that Li Ao found fault with everything under the sun — but the point was that he dared to voice his opinions, even when they ran completely counter to the prevailing mood, without the slightest fear.

In today's society, few people dare call themselves a master, but Li Ao was not one of them. He not only called himself a master but stressed that everyone who admired him did so for a very simple reason: "Because I, Li Ao, am brilliant."

Li Ao did too many things in his life; he published more than a hundred books alone. Almost no one could fully summarize and review his achievements and controversies. Even so, one thread stands out most clearly: the courage to say no to an era.

### The Courage to Say No to an Era

Li Ao was born in Northeast China in April 1935. In 1949, the 14-year-old Li Ao moved to Taiwan with the Nationalist government. For a very long time he lived in Taiwan, passing through the martial-law era and the social changes that followed its lifting, and he kept speaking his mind throughout.

But as with many writers who came to Taiwan with the Nationalist government, feelings toward mainland China were a shadow that followed Li Ao all his life, evident in both his literary work and his political positions.

In the year Li Ao came to Taiwan, the Kuomintang government had just promulgated martial law in Taiwan, beginning 38 years of martial law. For many intellectuals this was a period of deep anguish: any thought or action at odds with the authorities was judged to be that of a dissident or rebel, and landed you in prison. During this period, Li Ao was imprisoned twice.

On March 19, 1971, the 36-year-old Li Ao was arrested for the first time. On February 28 of the following year he was imprisoned on charges of "rebellion" and sentenced to ten years, finally being released in November 1976. Li Ao was arrested because he had helped Peng Ming-min — known as the godfather of Taiwan independence — slip out of the country and flee abroad. Chiang Kai-shek was then in power and cracked down hard on Taiwan-independence activity, and so Li Ao was put behind bars.

The second imprisonment came in August 1981, arising from the cases of "Li Ao's appropriation of Hsiao Meng-neng's family property" and "Hsiao Meng-neng's false accusation against Li Ao." The Taipei District Court initially found Li Ao not guilty; on appeal, the Taiwan High Court changed the ruling and sentenced Li Ao to six months in prison, a verdict that became final.

Looking back over the course of Li Ao's two imprisonments, many commentators believe that he indeed carried a fair share of trumped-up charges in that era — and, at the same time, his story illustrates the common fate of cultural figures in those years.

None of this, however, stopped Li Ao from continuing to voice dissent to society; he became a well-known tangwai ("outside-the-party") figure of the martial-law years.

### "The Top Three Writers of Vernacular Chinese Are Li Ao, Li Ao, Li Ao."

Recalling Li Ao, Chen Fangming, chair professor at the Graduate Institute of Taiwanese Literature at National Chengchi University, spoke of the magazine *Wenxing* (《文星》). The magazine was one of Taiwan's most important cultural journals during the martial-law era.

In 1961, the 26-year-old Li Ao submitted a piece to *Wenxing*, in which he expressed pro-Westernization views, championed vernacular Chinese, and came to Hu Shih's defense. His article at the time set off a debate in literary circles between Chinese and Western culture, and with his sharp pen Li Ao began to make his name. In 1963 he took over as *Wenxing*'s editor-in-chief.

Chen Fangming once commented of Li Ao: he "carved out a road for that era."

In the final issue of *Wenxing* before it was banned, Li Ao brought people of many different positions together in the same magazine and wrote essays criticizing the Kuomintang. Chen Fangming believes Li Ao possessed uncommon courage, and that his achievements included introducing young people of the day to modern literature and liberalism.

In his life Li Ao indeed cursed countless people; Hu Shih alone was the man he revered.

In 1958, when Hu Shih came to Taipei to take up the post of president of Academia Sinica, someone introduced Li Ao — sitting in the audience at one of the lectures — to Hu Shih. That was their first meeting, and the two went on to talk for several hours.

After that, Li Ao wrote "Hu Shih the Sower" (《播种者胡适》) for *Wenxing* magazine: "We need only affirm Hu Shih's contribution to the literary revolution, his contribution to the New Culture Movement, his contribution to constitutional democracy, and his contribution to academic independence and the long-term development of science — with that, we can conclude his contribution to our nation's march toward modernization. Everything beyond that is beside the point!"

After Hu Shih's death, Li Ao kept praising Hu Shih's contributions on countless occasions. In February 2009, on the program *Li Ao's Aphorisms to the World* (《李敖妙语天下》), he spoke of his admiration for Hu Shih. At the time he directly criticized Ma Ying-jeou's ignorance of the May Fourth Movement and the New Culture Movement, then carefully traced the history of the movement, beginning with Hu Shih's return to Taiwan from the United States. He said he himself had joined the crowd welcoming him at the airport, and saw that Hu Shih not only shook hands with everyone but could even tell each person some news about their own recent circumstances. Li Ao stressed Hu Shih's gift for minute observation of people and his extraordinary memory.

Li Ao's reverence for Hu Shih also led him to publish several books about him — such as *Studies on Hu Shih* (《胡适研究》) and *A Critical Biography of Hu Shih* (《胡适评传》) — and to compile *Selected Works of Hu Shih* (《胡适选集》).

Li Ao once said that in his life he had written more than 21 million characters — three times Lu Xun's 7 million — and boasted of his contribution to the literary world: "For the past 50 years and the next 500 years, the top three writers of vernacular Chinese are Li Ao, Li Ao, Li Ao."

Whether it was a major event in cultural circles or a political upheaval, Li Ao's critical spirit was that of a fighter: no matter how the media environment changed, he always found a channel through which to make his voice heard and an outlet for his words.

In fact, Li Ao once hoped to turn his critical spirit into politics and directly influence society; he ran twice each for president and for Taipei mayor. But in the end his vote share never exceeded 1%.

In a sense, this also seems to show that Li Ao was fated to be a critic outside the system. The writer Yang Chao once wrote in a commentary that although Li Ao's conception of writing was rough and crude, in his actual use of language he forged a uniquely infectious style that almost no one could imitate.

### A Look at His Works

**Opening**

In the Song dynasty there was a custom called "the eighty children." When a child was born, in the hope that he would live a long life, people wrote two big red characters — "eighty" — on his forehead for good luck. Now I have really lived to eighty; if anything were to be written on my forehead, it would have to be "eight hundred" to be satisfying. It looks like I won't make it to "eight hundred," but writing several hundred random memoirs of a floating life is no problem, so I spent forty days writing several hundred of them. Since these are random memoirs of a floating life, I make no claim to completeness — writing whatever comes, entirely at ease, capturing all the charm. The book's title was originally going to be *Li Ao at Eighty: A Racy Chronicle* (《李敖八十风流录》), but eighty seemed too old-fashioned, so it became *A Racy Autobiography of Li Ao* (《李敖风流自传》 — the simplified-character edition is titled *Li Ao's Autobiography* (《李敖自传》) — editor's note). Given how unorthodox the writing is, it could also be called *Li Ao's Kaleidoscopic Memoirs of a Floating Life*. It would seem that even the rule-breaking André Malraux's *Antimémoires* and Günter Grass's *Mein Jahrhundert* were written too properly. When Tang poets wrote that "the grace and panache of letters live on still," they were bragging; in fact, their "grace and panache" were only truly realized in Li Ao. "Those who come after will look upon today as we look upon the past." The one who "looks upon the past"? That is me. The one who "will look upon today"? Is there such a one? There is not. Walking alone among a thousand mountains, one man for all ages — the melody of Guanglingsan ends here!

**Author's Notes**

1. All the books I have written in my life were too well-behaved; this book must subvert that: the pen goes where it will, with no concern for structure, genre, balance, or tidy beginnings and ends — as long as it can wander at ease in nature, unafraid of messiness and unafraid of incoherence.

2. Sometimes I use the gourd-vine method. The gourd vine is a runaway horse. It runs for a while and then comes back — or never comes back — all as fancy dictates.

3. Sometimes a long story is told short, sometimes a short story is told long — a bit of rambling. Nor do I mind small repetitions. Repeated it is, repeated it is — I am reinforcing the facts, adding weight to the tone. And telling a long story short — am I giving a briefing? No, I just don't want to spend more time. If by some chance it comes out long, I must be truly furious.

4. What is written here finds wildfire in darkness and barbarism in civilization, gaining its wild charm; and finds elegance within vulgarity and vulgarity shed from elegance, gaining its refined charm as well as its vulgar charm.

5. Write plenty of digressions. The ancients had "the broad outline of traces" and "the small sample of feelings" — the ancients bragged, but I actually possess them.

6. Sometimes I use the leaping method. The leaping method is the most natural. It looks headless and tailless, but in fact each part stands alone. Nor is the leaping method a leap from which one never returns — it "circles back and swirls around." Only, by the time it leaps back, you can no longer connect. What a remarkable creature the flea is: at three millimeters in size, it can jump to a height seventy times its own and a distance one hundred and ten times its own. Leaping is wonderful — from level ground it gives itself height and distance. Please accept my leaping method.

**Two Old Ladies**

My style adopts the "casual-natter form." Though I can no longer endure heavy, elaborate prose, the autobiography is an exception: where it should ramble, I will not let it off lightly — I will ramble with special vigor. Enough to make oxen sweat, enough to fill houses to the rafters, enough to go online, enough to download — autobiographies ancient and modern are many, but the best come from two old ladies. One is Yang Buwei, the wife of Yuen Ren Chao; the other is Jiang Dongxiu, the wife of Hu Shih. The great virtue of the old-lady autobiography lies in its casual nattering. Precisely because it is casual, it seldom falsifies; precisely because it nags, no pearl is left behind. Li Ao, for all his grand master strokes, here writes in the manner of an old woman with grand master strokes; only one who can understand an old woman is fit to read an autobiography. Who knows me? Surely only the old ladies. Among my collection is a chapter of typewritten manuscript with corrections in old Mrs. Hu's own hand — a vivid scolding of her godson-in-law and goddaughter, Chien Shih-liang and Chien Shih-liang's wife, for their ingratitude. In life, Hu Shih gathered friends and company, and most of those he gathered were riffraff; after his death, they one by one showed their true colors and drifted away. No wonder old Mrs. Hu's pages brim with grievance.

**Time — 1935**

1935: that was the era of China's greatest misfortune, Abyssinia's greatest misfortune, the Jews' greatest misfortune. The Jews were locked in as the source of all evil, and only after six million of them were killed did they get a moment's respite. That slaughter was gradual, beginning with making them wear placards of self-blame; as early as 1935, the placards were hung on them. As for hanging placards, the foreigners got there long before!

**Place — I Live in China**

I deny the existence of the "Republic of China." Someone asked: if you don't recognize the "Republic of China," where do you live? I replied serenely: "I live in China." The so-called "Republic of China" is, in fact, essentially equivalent to one province of China — yet on the basis of that one province' status — a status dragged out for as long as sixty-six years — it presumptuously demands to be "a province the equal of a country," to "stand up as an equal." This is a shameless, fact-erasing attitude. If the People's Republic of China had not appeared on the mainland from 1949 onward, perhaps the four characters "Republic of China" could still have passed muster; but the People's Republic of China was founded long ago and has long been recognized by the Chinese people and by the countries of the world — the "Republic of China" has, in truth, long been a dead nation! I love excavating Kuomintang documents, and I pointed out long ago: on March 13, 1950, Chiang Kai-shek — the "president" of the defunct "Republic of China" — secretly lectured on "the mission and purpose of my resumption of office" at Yangmingshan Villa, admitting: "From my retirement last January to the end of the year, in less than a year's time, all the mainland provinces had fallen. Today we have truly reached the state of a perished nation! Yet the people who have come to Taiwan today, civil and military cadres alike, seem to feel no pain of national extinction... I especially remind everyone today: our Republic of China perished along with the fall of the mainland by the end of last year! We have all become people of a dead nation without realizing it — is this not agony?" Black characters on white paper, as plain as this: anyone who says the "Republic of China" has not perished naturally does not conform to the "Director-General's teachings" or the "President's instructions." And the lackeys' "Director-General" and "President" privately never failed to admit the fact that the "Republic of China" was dead. Of course, what is said privately is one thing; what is said brazenly is another. Brazenly, Chiang Kai-shek still shamelessly proclaimed that the "Republic of China" not only had not perished but encompassed the entire mainland. And, on top of that, Outer Mongolia. Outer Mongolia had long been sold off by him; Outer Mongolia had long entered the United Nations — and Chiang Kai-shek still said it was his. Never mind the nation-selling Chiang Kai-shek: in my heart, Outer Mongolia has always been China's.

**The Man — "Such a One in Flesh and Blood Walked Upon This Earth"**

Summing up eighty years of achievement, it seems to come down to "example" and "sowing." Li Ao walked alone all his life and existed independently, unshaken by poverty or low station, unbent by force or power — this is the "example." And year after year, breaking through siege after siege, flying from the island of Taiwan to the motherland, championing for the world freedom, wisdom, reverence for China, and opposition to America — this is the "sowing." Only the world and China are too vast, and resistance too great; how many seeds could actually be sown is hard to assess. What can be grasped in the end is still mostly the "example." In truth, of individuals through the ages who "as common men became teachers of a hundred generations, whose one word became law for all under heaven," there are but a handful. Assessed against the long flow of history, one merely comes as a flash of spirit and goes as a wandering shade. Look at Gandhi: he never saw, in his lifetime, the fruits of a lifetime of painstaking effort and ascetic practice; and after his violent death, the evolution of his own country diverged from the direction of his life's striving. To this day, when Indians remember Gandhi, it is only the "example" of "gazing up at the high mountain, following the great road." Beyond the mountain there are mountains, and that mountain is not this mountain; the people follow your road, sir, for half a day — and the people are still the people. "My way is not lonely" — that exists, but call the roll and there are few names. A few bronze statues for you is fine — bronze statues are just a tourist attraction, no? (A digression on the side: in my life, the women who went to bed with me saw most clearly this flesh-and-blood body of mine, tasted the "true and not false" mood of the *Diamond Sutra* — but did they know how great I am?)

**Ghosts — The Japanese Devils**

The year I was born, 1935, was precisely the year the Japanese devils were humiliating China with wildfire intensity. Chiang Kai-shek's Kuomintang government was all along servile toward Japan. The various treaties it signed need not be mentioned; but one story about freedom of speech is especially worth telling. Du Zhongyuan published the essay "Idle Talk About the Emperor" (《闲话皇帝》) in *New Life Weekly* (《新生周刊》); the Japanese consul-general in Shanghai, on the pretext of "insulting the Emperor," demanded that the Japan-friendly Kuomintang government prosecute him. The Kuomintang court sentenced Du Zhongyuan to one year and two months in prison and, at the same time, shut down the magazine *New Life Weekly*. Hard to believe, isn't it? Seventy-six years later, in 2011, the likes of Wang Wei-chung used the media program *Quanmin Zuida Dang* (《全民最大党》) to apologize to the Japanese emperor — how shameful! Back then the charge the Japanese gave Du Zhongyuan and the press was "insulting the Emperor, harming friendly relations." The Japanese were hateful, but at least they capped you on the basis of existing relations. What about today? The Japanese long ago kicked you aside — Taiwan has no "diplomatic relations" with them — yet Taiwan, while 210,000 of its own poor households live in straitened circumstances, took up a collection led by Ma Ying-jeou and offered up 5.8 billion for Japan's disaster relief. Now, doubling down on top of that with an apology, they have debased the people of Taiwan like this. And both Ma Ying-jeou and Wang Wei-chung are waishengren — mainlanders in Taiwan! From the 1935 *New Life Weekly* incident to the 2011 *Quanmin Zuida Dang* incident — I lived to see them all.

**So I Am "God"**

Fundamentally, human beings are played for fools by God's chemical game. God made man and woman, then toyed with them chemically, creating physiological needs and psychological needs — hence intercourse and love. Dancing to God's baton on these needs, in a hundred strange forms, making a spectacle of oneself; God sits on high, chuckling in private amusement. One cannot free oneself from God's spell, but there is still a chance to choreograph one's own steps and stand in one's own pose. In other words, to transform the physiological and psychological needs under God's direction into something as ever-shifting and gorgeously colorful as a kaleidoscope, until God gasps in wonder and loses control: God is but heavenly craft, but human craft can outdo it. God remains vulgar at the animal level — including the single missionary position — but I have raised myself above that level. I have transcended vulgarity; I turned the tables on God.

God made the Adams and Eves so crude; through progress and refinement, how different they have become from Adam and Eve, how different from the animals in Noah's ark, how different from their primitive selves. I came from the crowd; I was contaminated; I was exposed to far, far too much vulgarity and worldliness — yet I walked out of it, and proved that one can triumph over heaven, triumph over the masses, and triumph over oneself. In relations between the sexes, from God down to the crowd, such relations were made bestial, monotonous, and painful. Now I have walked out; I have defeated the limits God gave me.

In bed, God vanishes — it turns out I am "God."

**God Handles the Two Ends; I Handle the Middle**

Even so, I still exhort myself with these words: "When it becomes nothing, and you are one with it, you too become nothing. You need do nothing to a meteor; if you do not shatter with the meteor, then keep to your own work of world-ness, universality, eternality, and vitality." This is the plan of my life and the direction of my remaining years. My lifelong plan is to organize all of humanity's ideas and behavior and draw conclusions. Humanity's ideas and behavior are too many kinds, too complex; I want to enumerate the items one by one, then sort out, study, explain, and conclude each item, tracing the whole course from beginning to end. This is not a great task one person can do or do well — yet I will complete it alone. This is the greatest gift I leave to humankind in my life, because not a single human being has yet been able to devote a lifetime of concentrated effort to organizing every question of all human ideas and behavior. Perhaps someone will say: "What you're doing sounds like the Last Judgment?" In fact it is different. The Last Judgment comes when human folly is already fully accomplished and beyond recall, with God merely handing down the final verdict. What I am doing is a mid-term settling of accounts. After the accounts are settled, humanity becomes clear and clear-headed, and can adjust its future conduct and direction. So what I do is not the same as what God does; we merely divide the work and cooperate. God handles only the two ends — from the opening act of creating humanity to the closing curtain of the Last Judgment — while I handle the middle: to clear the field a little and review everything of the first half. Therefore God may judge me in the end — but until the end arrives, I will judge Him first.

**Sight — Where My Eyes Rest**

I most love to close my eyes and bury them in the thighs of my seventeen-year-old lover. Smooth, soft, warm, fragrant — and springy... With my eyes buried there, I would gladly never open them again; that is the place of eternal rest I forever long for.

Year upon year of toil, day upon day of vexation — in an instant all turn to nothingness. What appears before me is just like the cloud-dream maiden of "The Goddess" (《神女赋》) — now near, now far, now bowing, now gazing up — freeing you and freed for you, letting you float in the heavens yet dance in the human world. Such comfort, such tranquility. How I wish it could be a final resting place — eyes never to open again, sleeping forever there.

**Sound — Madame Butterfly**

A friend of mine, Xue Qiwen, is an audiophile, utterly bewitched by new sound systems day and night. The moment anything new hits the market he goes crazy, pulling every string to trade in the old for the new, hauling things in and out — hardly a day of peace. I asked him: is the gap between old and new really that big? He gave a wry smile and shook his head. Ah, life — N is already good enough; why struggle after N+1? For those who keep listening to soul-stealing music on ever-newer sound systems, why not subtract one — N is enough. Xue Qiwen most loves listening to opera on CD, and is especially addicted to *Madame Butterfly*. I find that the hardest thing to stomach about *Madame Butterfly* is the long silences between passages — you think the opera is over, that Madame Butterfly has died, and unexpectedly she comes back to life. Xue Qiwen the opera buff told me: every time he listens to *Madame Butterfly* he cries. His wife forbade him to use the big sound system anymore, so he bought a Walkman and secretly plays it in the bathroom — and every time he finishes on the toilet and opens the door, he comes out in tears. I roared with laughter when I heard: Madame Butterfly has finally died a tragic death.

**Desire — Japanese Women**

My wife Xiaotun once said a marvelous thing to me: "When you start liking Japanese women, it proves you're getting old." What a shame — in recent years I've already begun to show a tendency to like Japanese women. Japanese women are far more delicate than foreign women. For photo books, Riona Hazuki's are the best. On a TV program I displayed photographs of Riona Hazuki, as counter-evidence that in this respect we lag behind Japan in openness and freedom. For this display, Taiwan's Government Information Office sent the TV company a written warning — but it did not dare warn me; this time it seems Li Ao could not attract them. The most beautiful sound in the human world is not the opera *Madame Butterfly* — it is Madame Butterfly's cries in bed.

**The "Bei" of the Study**

In the study, sometimes I no longer search for a book; instead I am happily surprised by it — it appears, as if I have bumped into it. Books seem to pop up on their own initiative. "The wind blows, the grass bends low, and the cattle and sheep appear" — the dagger shows when the map is unrolled — the active party is over there, not here. Let my eyes be passive: passive pleasure is like being massaged. What a fine way this is to interpret the word "bei"! (Though all of this is the romance of ten years ago.) I was infatuated with that seventeen-year-old. Seventeen is not a static number; seventeen is a number of three hundred and sixty-five days slipping away. When the last one slips away, seventeen is about to be no more. "No more" is not time no more, nor the passing of this one year of youth — it is the grace, joy, voice, and smile of seventeen no more. What is left to you is a complete grid of memories, waiting for you to fill in the fine details, waiting for you to trace over in red. Memory is an extension of the lived moment — finer and more complete and lifelike than the moment itself, if you can remember rather than mourn. Mourning the past is not memory; mourning the past is a wrong attitude toward life — it dusts the happy memories over.

From that seventeen-year-old, I hold a conclusion in my heart. I write it about myself in a line of pidgin English: I have taken more out of seventeen than seventeen has taken out of me. That is my conclusion. The literary work *Virtual Seventeen* (《虚拟的十七岁》) is my other-category harvest "out of seventeen." Without the real body of seventeen, I could hardly have written scenes so magnificent. Writing, like painting, needs a model. That real body was my model.

**Wish — I Wish I Were an Animal Trainer**

Of all professions, the one I admire most is the animal trainer. The animal trainer's trade is built on a strange enemy-and-self relationship, on the great premise of "because you are both unafraid of me and afraid of me." When the trainer works with a male lion, the moment the lion charges, the trainer is finished; yet the trainer cracks a long whip and shouts the lion into submission, crouching onto the pedestal. The lion is not convinced: it refuses again and again, holds out for a while, and in the end mounts the pedestal against its will — then extends a paw in protest, roaring at the trainer again and again. What the animal trainer faces is constant unpredictability. The trainer is not like the bullfighter: the bullfighter's colossal adversary is predictable. It is simply the enemy; it simply doesn't like the look of you; it simply charges straight at you — and you have the skill to dodge. The space is large; by the time the bull turns a corner and comes back, you are set again. But the trainer is different: the lion turns better than the bull, the space is the same big cage, and there is more than one lion stirring with intent — every one of them a murderer. Only, the lions labor under an illusion: they think you are stronger than them; they fear you — both fear and no fear — and in the end they decide on fear. Under protest — merely fearing you while protesting. There is another kind of trainer, so skilled and so bold that for money he will risk his life: he can actually make the male lion open its huge jaws and put the trainer's own head into the lion's mouth — go on, bite! Let's see you dare! Of course, nine times out of ten — ninety-nine percent of the time — the lion does not dare bite. But the one time in ten, the one percent, can happen — and then you can't say. We have heard the four characters "spared from the tiger's jaws," but we have never heard of what was spared from the lion's jaws. So the trainer's trade is too splendid, too risky, too thrilling, too challenging. And, I hear, like the executioner's, it runs in families. If the father is an executioner, the son carries on the axe and is an executioner too. If the father is an animal trainer, the son is an animal trainer too. A joke goes: one day, the son couldn't find his father. He ran into the lion cage and made the lions open their mouths wide, checking them one by one. Someone asked him where he was going. He said: I'm looking for my dad.

**Longevity — Eighty Reflections Before One Hundred**

Because I resolved to live to one hundred, writing this book at eighty seems a bit too early. But eighty is always a pass — a moment to fix one's character and position — and a settling of the matter is still a settlement. Everything done after eighty will merely settle on this established pattern, adding flowers to the brocade; so for the twenty years before one hundred, it will be nothing but brocade dazzling to the eye — the brocade itself was set long before eighty.

And so, after much hesitation, I still decided to write this book — to make a forward look and a summation of my life. A forward look to one hundred, a summation of eighty — unless before one hundred I go campaigning against traitors and take a concubine, living myself into tedium; my life' character and position, at the occasion of my eightieth birthday, stops right here. I want to use my eighty to fix my character and position and to make it manifest to people, to make them feel that posterity will find it hard to imagine that "such a one in flesh and blood once walked upon this earth." — These are my eighty reflections before one hundred. In my essays and talks I have a habit — you may call it a vice — of inserting self-praise at any moment. After the insertion I return to the main topic, speaking like any ordinary person. The person in the whole world most accustomed to this habit of mine is Lai Yuezhong; jokingly, he is my "personal photographer." He lets me have my way: noticing that I have gone off on an insertion, he takes a sip of coffee, unsurprised, a smile on his face, quietly waiting for the crooked wind to blow past. Chen Wenxi says that people who can view Li Ao's self-praise with appreciation are "persons of the Way." She is sometimes a person of the Way, and sometimes not.

**When Mother Lion Is Out**

Mother lion has gone out to hunt. Three lion cubs slip out of the den, happily nipping at one another. Suddenly an eagle descends from the clouds and carries one off. For the hungry eagle, it is the law of nature. For the two terrified survivors who have just lost their playmate, it is also the law of nature. In the eyes of animals there is no compassion; in the hearts of animals there is no sigh. Compassion and sighing are things we add on top. Sometimes we know full well that this is the law of nature and can be explained — yet it is hard to let go. We should adjust ourselves, widen our field of vision: first animalize, then humanize. Animalization is a certain degree of the "self-forgetting" in appreciating animals; humanization is keeping one's eyes on the great questions of humanity and refusing to "forget." A certain return to origins — the animalized primitive — is not without its wisdom.

**Free China and I**

When I was a middle school student, I already did deep research on the Chinese thinker Hu Shih. I wrote an essay, "On Hu Shih's *Collected Writings*" (《关于〈胡适文存〉》). Chen Shixi was then running *This Generation* (《这一代》) at National Taiwan University's College of Law; he took a fancy to the essay and offered to trade for it Hu Shih's inscription for his magazine — "Those who act will succeed; those who keep going will arrive." I agreed. Unexpectedly, NTU's student affairs authorities judged there was a problem with it during review and would not allow publication, so Chen Shixi returned it to me. Two years later, I was already a university student; Lu Xiaozhao ran *University Magazine* (《大学杂志》). He didn't care about such things and so grabbed it for publication. Nearly a year after it appeared, "Luo" suddenly suggested: "Why not send it to Free China? They are sure to publish it!" I had always considered Free China beyond my reach and had never even thought of it, but on her suggestion I cut out a portion; she made a clean copy, and it was mailed to Free China.

In the March 1, 1957 issue of Free China, Volume 16, No. 5, the essay appeared, retitled "Speaking of Reading *Hu Shih's Collected Writings*." Lei Chen immediately wrote to Hu Shih in America, heartily recommending this expert who had original research on Hu Shih — Hu Shih later told me this himself. This was the beginning of my bond with Free China.

**A Decade of Free China**

Looking back on the distinguishing feature of Free China's galloping ten years, it lay precisely in its simply telling the truth. Its axis was the promotion of freedom and democracy; its thousands upon thousands of words "vied with the court" — which was Su Dongpo's old "flaw" — and that was remarkable. But I often wonder: after Free China was banned and the applause that moved an era died down, setting all feelings aside, whom did the magazine actually influence? Apart from me, Li Ao, who could carry on that bone and that method of "vying with the court" — whom else did it influence? I searched high and low and truly could not find a single resounding name. While the magazine lived, its deeds were righteous; when the magazine died, its righteousness ceased — is that not so? Look at my Taichung First Senior High classmate Shih Chi-yang: back then, like me, he was a university student writing for Free China. The only difference was that I used my real name, "Li Ao," while he used the pen name "Yang Zhengmin." In Lei Chen's diaries, published after his death, we even find details of Shih Chi-yang's secret dealings with Lei Chen. Yet the moment Free China was gone and Lei Chen was in prison, the Shih Chi-yang we saw was a literatus who had sold himself to the Kuomintang. The freedom, democracy, enlightened rule of law, and all the "vying with the court" that Free China promoted were forgotten clean by its own contributor Shih Chi-yang. Whom did the magazine actually influence? I finally saw the perfect case of "doing everything in reverse" — with the "reverse" spelling Shih. Ma Junwu's poem says: "When the written word completes its work, the revolutionary tide rolls through a thousand ages." But in the magazine Free China, I could not see it. After Free China, I too ran magazines — but whom did they influence? Looking and looking, it turns out the answer is myself. I deeply feel the limits of the written word's "completion of work": the forces of "counter-revolution" (not in the narrow KMT-CCP sense here) were too great, and in the end the most prominent thing was merely the spirit of "vying with the court" — a victory of the spirit! The other winning hands were, after all, slow and pitiful. Hu Shih said in an address at a Free China tea gathering: we must not belittle ourselves; we too are powerful — only our power is not the worldly kind of military force or wealth. His view refreshes the heart and mind. But after all, the times have become ever less hospitable to "a common man becoming a teacher of a hundred generations, one word becoming law for the world." Tsiang Ting-fu predicted that his generation of intellectuals was China's last generation of influential intellectuals. Seen from many angles, Tsiang Ting-fu was right: intellectuals have indeed grown ever smaller, ever more specialized. This world has rarely seen "great Confucians" — the "great Confucians" who are the romantic geniuses of all ages, washed away by the waves.

1. "If I were not Li Ao, I would wish to be Li Ao II."

2. "When I want someone to admire, I go look in the mirror."

3. Ah, life — N is already good enough; why chase N+1?

4. Mourning the past is not memory; mourning the past is a wrong attitude toward life — it dusts the happy memories over.

5. "In a dark room, I make my own light."

6. "As one whose mind roams at midnight over the woes of humankind, as one whose thought and talent stride alone across a thousand ages, I was truly born at the wrong time and in the wrong place."

7. "I should have been born fifty years from now, for my sensibility is at least fifty years ahead of this island. With many of my enemies and friends, it is not 'regretting we met too late' but 'regretting we met too early.'"

8. "My profession is a bit like a butcher's: every day I slaughter many pigs. Only he uses a knife; I use a pen."

9. "I utterly fear neither loneliness nor solitude; the long night is endless, the burden heavy and the road long. I can hardly find a single person who shares my way — I walk on alone, step by step, toward what lies ahead."

10. "My method of cursing people is this: everyone else calls people bastards, but I have a special skill — I can prove you are a bastard."

11. "The English say that England has no permanent friends and no permanent enemies, only permanent interests. For me, Li Ao, I have no permanent friends and no permanent enemies, only permanent justice."

12. Be weak, and you mostly fail to live well; be strong, and you mostly fail to die well.

13. Explanation is sometimes unnecessary — enemies will not believe your explanation, and friends need no explanation.

14. The terrible thing about fools is not their foolishness but their belief that they are clever. Fools cannot do the stupidest things; the stupidest things are done by clever people.

15. I cannot wait until the Last Judgment to deal with all the petty men and enemies; along the way, I will casually butcher a few of them.

16. Someone once challenged me: "Unleash your horse and come at me." I did not answer; I simply galloped away — then felled him with a cannon shot from behind the horse.

17. The thinker and writer of the new era has arrived: Li Ao, who created *Virtual Seventeen*.

18. I do not envy other people's youth; I only envy last year's me, or the me of the first half of this year.

19. The ancients with great ambition spoke of "toppling all the heroes of the age," but I think they were talking big. The one who truly embodied that spirit is none other than Li Ao himself — Li Ao, who walks alone among a thousand mountains, Li Ao, one man for all ages.

20. I have never had many friends in my life, and I never spent time gathering followers, so I "magnify myself" — relying entirely on blowing my own trumpet. Those who cannot stomach my self-promotion should be ashamed: you were supposed to blow my horn for me, but you dodged the duty, so I had to come myself. I boast because you are silent.
