Huayi Brothers, So Distracted — Are You Making Films, or Becoming Some Other Kind of Company?
@@QD_EXCERPT@@
Regular article
@@QD_BODY@@
## None of these options is inherently better, but Huayi needs to pick one.

Regular article

*Mr. Six* was one of the most noteworthy Chinese-language films of 2015. In the final scene, Lord Six, played by Feng Xiaogang, props himself up on a military knife in the middle of a frozen lake; shot from below, the moment reads as deeply tragic.

![Lord Six in Mr. Six](/uploads/image/201602/8406c8c150bd.jpg)

The "left-behind" Lord Six looks like a portrait of Huayi Brothers itself, the studio behind *Mr. Six* — Huayi had fallen behind in China's explosively growing box office. After losing the number-one spot among private film companies that it had held for years in 2014, [Huayi Brothers' market share slid further to around 4.5% in 2015](http://www.entgroup.cn/report/f/). The golden age that belonged to Huayi Brothers appeared to be over.

Huayi itself may not see it that way. [Its financial statements](http://data.eastmoney.com/notice/20151023/2Wvl2XuCLVH1K6.html) — no less important for a company — tell a completely different story. In the first three quarters of 2015, Huayi Brothers' revenue doubled. Excluding income from its various investments, net profit of 128 million yuan was up 148% from the same period in 2014.

The growth came mainly from Yinhan Technology, the gaming company Huayi Brothers acquired; Yinhan's results were folded into Huayi's books in May 2014. [Huayi's subsequent half-year report showed that in just over a month, Yinhan Technology contributed nearly 75 million yuan in revenue](http://www.yicai.com/news/2014/09/4014902.html).

In Huayi's 2015 interim report, the gross profit of its internet entertainment segment, home to the gaming business, [exceeded 400 million yuan](http://data.eastmoney.com/notice/20150826/2Wvl2XRJpvCGFU.html) — surpassing the 330 million yuan contributed by film and TV entertainment to become Huayi Brothers' single most important source of profit.

![Space Hunter is Yinhan Technology's best-known game](/uploads/image/201602/c96c999320fe.jpg)

In fact, the biggest profit line in Huayi Brothers' books was neither film nor games, but so-called investment income. Selling shares of Ourpalm — a previous investment — along with Jiangsu Sparkle Roll Cinemas, plus the Hong Kong listing of Chaofan Network, in which it had invested, brought Huayi [close to 500 million yuan in gains](http://data.eastmoney.com/notice/20150826/2Wvl2XRJpvCGFU.html).

In theory, it is no longer quite right to call Huayi Brothers a film production company.

A strategy that began in 2009 looks like it paid off. That was the year Huayi Brothers first proposed its "de-filmification" strategy.

"If in the future you only do box office, I think the company has gone off track. The first thing I said to our executives when we went public in 2009 was: from today, Huayi Brothers is de-filmifying. I hadn't fully thought that through — actually I thought it through and I hadn't — but I had a vague feeling that Huayi absolutely could not make only films," [Wang Zhongjun said in an exclusive interview with Phoenix Finance](http://finance.ifeng.com/a/20140611/12519851_1.shtml).

![](/uploads/image/201602/3e6bcb825e95.gif)

Even as Wang Zhongjun kept saying he hadn't thought it through, he understood perfectly well. The film business is too risky — in China especially, it's a business that lives and dies by the box office, and whether people feel like going to the movies, whether they feel like seeing Huayi's movies, is impossible to predict.

By then, the Chinese film market had already exploded, growing more than 30% a year, but the model Huayi had long relied on internally — using star talent to drive its entire film production — was being eroded.

Feng Xiaogang is the best example. From *Big Shot's Funeral* in 2002 to *If You Are the One* in 2008, Huayi and Feng Xiaogang together took the box-office crown for several years running.

But in the past couple of years Feng Xiaogang had aged, and his creative powers were fading. In February 2012, Feng posted on Weibo that his love for film was growing thinner and thinner, hinting that he might quit the film business. That same day, Huayi's stock price began to fall, ultimately forcing [Feng to apologize on Weibo](http://weibo.com/1774978073/y4IgIgZ0K?from=page_1035051774978073_profile&wvr=6&mod=weibotime&type=comment): "I will honor my contract with Huayi and fulfill it to the best of my ability, at a high standard of quality." Even so, Feng Xiaogang had lost his absolute dominance in the market.

![Feng Xiaogang](/uploads/image/201602/f71893ee67b1.jpg)

Huayi's proud talent-management business was also shrinking. Wang Jinghua walked out; then Zhou Xun, Huang Xiaoming, Li Bingbing, Fan Bingbing and others left the Huayi system to set up their own studios.

Meanwhile, other competitors in the film industry were rising. Enlight Media moved into production on the strength of its powerful ground-level distribution; Bona Film Group leaned on Hong Kong filmmakers who had gone north to chase the mainland gold rush; Wanda had deep pockets; and internet companies lurked on the sidelines, eager to use the internet to disrupt the traditional film industry. All of this made the big-budget Feng Xiaogang-style film look cumbersome and unpredictably risky.

The de-filmification strategy was proposed against exactly this backdrop. For Huayi, the intent was to help it find sources of profit beyond film. [In Wang Zhongjun's words](http://finance.ifeng.com/a/20140611/12519851_1.shtml): "I don't think a film company can become a great company... I might end up becoming the owner of a Playboy — it puts out a magazine that's all show and beauty, but it has no ability to sustain profits, or to make big profits."

Although the Wang brothers stated many times that "de-filmification" did not mean Huayi would stop making films — only that Huayi would not make only films — when all of Huayi's resources and energy went into its other businesses, the marginalization of its film business was inevitable.

And so we saw another Huayi emerge over the following five years.

In 2010, Huayi Brothers acquired a 22% stake in mobile game maker Ourpalm for 148 million yuan. After Ourpalm went public, [Huayi Brothers sold down its stake, cashing out nearly 1 billion yuan in total](https://www.baidu.com/link?url=nCxuccXvLYJvHwCJuY4E2nrZ4reZa3UFk2BvTjCe9GV0zgcK7WX62dr09EWtHxd6CR9TvWCfCYiKO6XlDsp604GYFLxGcvnhQCX8YG-OvUm&wd=&eqid=fa7bdcec00002fea0000000456cbfa2f). Huayi Brothers' remaining Ourpalm shares are still worth more than 1.7 billion yuan today.

In 2013, Huayi Brothers acquired Yinhan Technology, whose numbers were folded into Huayi's financial reports a year later. In 2015, Huayi Brothers invested in game publisher Xianyu Games as well as Hero Entertainment, developer of *Crisis Action*.

![Crisis Action](/uploads/image/201602/d63de19db705.jpg)

Huayi's entertainment-real-estate projects (what Huayi calls its brand-licensing and location-based entertainment business) may also be lucrative. Since the opening of 1942 Street at the Feng Xiaogang Film Commune in Hainan, [Huayi Brothers had signed contracts for 14 similar projects as of the third quarter of 2015](http://news.163.com/15/1224/15/BBK2AVNO00014SEH.html).

But compared with Huayi's former top-dog status in the film industry, in gaming and entertainment real estate it is still a minor player. [In 2015, China's game industry generated more than 140 billion yuan in revenue](http://games.ifeng.com/a/20151215/41523804_0.shtml), of which Tencent took more than 40 billion, NetEase more than 15 billion — while Huayi made less than 1 billion.

In entertainment real estate, [the Feng Xiaogang Film Commune officially announced more than 1 million visitors a year](http://www.1905.com/news/20150607/898616.shtml?fr=wwwstar_news_xgxw_4_20141017). Even at the 1-million scale, it is far from its benchmark, Disney. The Shanghai municipal government's official forecast was that Shanghai Disneyland would draw more than 7 million visitors in its first year.

In fact, 1942 Street is just a pedestrian street, done up in Republican-era and old Hong Kong style, with some shops and snack stands inside — essentially no different from the ancient towns and old streets already flooding every tourist spot in China.

Its only selling point is the connection to Feng Xiaogang's films. As the name suggests, 1942 Street was built from scenes in Feng Xiaogang's *Back to 1942*. But compared with its competitors — Disneyland, Universal Studios — Huayi has no way to offer richer entertainment experiences, and therefore has no real competitiveness.

![The Feng Xiaogang Film Commune (image from Trip.com Group)](/uploads/image/201602/2ca2974bbbee.jpg)

What's more, in most of its entertainment real-estate projects, what Huayi puts in is its own brand, [and what it takes back is licensing fees, dry shares, and 10% of ticket revenue](http://www.yicai.com/news/2015/02/4570615.html). In Huayi's financial reports, this business has a gross margin of 100%; in Wang Zhongjun's words: "A brand is a money-printing machine."

Of these, the biggest chunk is still the licensing fee. That one-time income is handsome but unsustainable. Once the entertainment-real-estate market saturates, the revenue these projects bring Huayi will drop sharply.

So Huayi Brothers' entertainment-real-estate business rests on the fame generated by its past films and on the entertainment-real-estate bubble — "living off its past glory" is the apt description.

In fact, when running its entertainment-real-estate and gaming businesses, Huayi Brothers had real ambition: [its benchmark was Disney](http://money.163.com/13/0620/02/91PF2VQN00253B0H.html). It wanted to learn from Disney — take a film like *Iron Man* and turn it into a TV show, make it into toys, develop it into games, and finally plant it in a theme park as an attraction for people to visit.

But Huayi never pulled it off. In 2014, Huayi announced it would adapt a Yinhan Technology game, *God and Demon*, into a film, and invited Wong Jing to direct. Yet *God and Demon* is nowhere to be seen in the future plans Wong Jing himself has described.

The Feng Xiaogang Film Commune looks more like an ordinary place to stroll and shop; compared with the 700 million yuan box office of Feng Xiaogang's *Personal Tailor*, this piece of entertainment real estate clearly has far less draw.

From this perspective, one can only say that Huayi's gaming and entertainment-real-estate businesses do make money, but neither can form sufficiently deep interplay with Huayi Brothers' core film and TV business. With these businesses, Huayi Brothers can grow bigger and find new revenue beyond the hugely risky film industry — but that is not enough to make Huayi Brothers China's Disney.

The gap between Huayi Brothers and Disney lies not in which businesses Huayi lacks, but in the fact that Huayi cannot make good films. Every time Disney summarizes its own business, what it talks about most are still its four brands: Disney, Marvel, Pixar, and Star Wars. In other words, what drives Disney's entire business is still those films and TV works.

*Star Wars* is the best example. In 1977, the *Star Wars* film hit screens and stunned the world — and only then came the later films, the bestselling toys, the theme-park attractions, the mobile games, and the whole chain of derivatives.

Look back at Huayi's moves in film and TV over the past year. Apart from *Mr. Six*, Huayi did not produce enough work to convince audiences.

![](/uploads/image/201602/c29a1a6b12b9.jpg)

A new strategic adjustment may already have begun.

In the second half of 2015, Huayi acquired two companies — one founded by its own artists Li Chen, Chen He, Feng Shaofeng, Zheng Kai and others, the other founded by Feng Xiaogang — for 756 million yuan and 1.05 billion yuan respectively.

This looks like a disguised way of handing bonuses to the artists, and Wang Zhonglei confirmed as much to QDaily: "What Huayi Brothers values most is the value of the artists themselves."

The bonuses took the form of acquisitions, and Huayi also signed performance-bet agreements with the artists. But in essence, this is no different from Huayi's old strategy of holding on to Feng Xiaogang, of holding on to Zhou Xun, Huang Xiaoming, Li Bingbing, and Fan Bingbing — all of it banking on star artists as a guarantee of box office or ratings.

Of course, Wang Zhongjun may now want more than that; the fan economy spun off from celebrities has become something Huayi wants to grab. In 2014 and 2015, Huayi partnered with Tencent and BesTV respectively to launch two fan-economy products, Xingying Alliance and Yulejia, for mobile and TV.

Huayi also set up a dedicated fan-economy company, Huayi Chuangxing, led by Hu Ming, formerly secretary to the chairman of Huayi Brothers. Not long ago, the news about Huayi Chuangxing was that Hu Ming had resigned as chairman and general manager of the company, and Wang Zhonglei personally took over.

You can see the fan economy has become a Huayi priority. Artists themselves will become IPs — when they are part of the fan economy, they might barely count as an IP — and Huayi will present all of this to audiences in one breath.

But whether fans will buy it, whether the artists Huayi signed can become idols, whether they carry enough weight to activate the fan economy — these are all questions Huayi needs to answer.

Besides refocusing on star artists, Huayi already sees itself as an investment company too.

During last June's Shanghai International Film Festival, Huayi also proposed an "entertainment-plus" strategy modeled on the "internet-plus" concept. It set up an investment fund, Fuhui Capital, to invest in companies that could create synergies with Huayi Brothers' entertainment business — among them Sutu Entertainment, which runs official WeChat accounts, and Zhihui Mofang, which does online travel.

![](/uploads/image/201602/5dd0f44c5406.jpg)

So with Huayi, what we see is a contradictory picture. On one hand, [Wang Zhongjun wants to turn Huayi Brothers into a Chinese Disney](http://www.yicai.com/news/2015/02/4570615.html): "Huayi keeps making films, keeps accumulating influential IPs; the IPs feed into games, the games keep being developed, and the games keep generating IPs — and then these two IPs will support the third pillar, brand licensing."

But on the other hand, film — the most central link in the IP value chain — has been weakened under the banner of "de-filmification." Huayi no longer looks like a film company, nor like an entertainment company. It looks more like a conglomerate that became bloated before it ever grew up, with a little bit of every business.

From this perspective, the criticism of Huayi's distraction — unrelenting over the past year — has a point. It reminds Huayi to confront a question: what kind of company is Huayi, exactly?

If Huayi's answer is "an investment company," then Huayi can let loose and go on a buying spree in the market the way it did with Ourpalm. If Huayi's answer is "a film company," then it should put more energy into making good films, rather than spending it on entertainment real estate, games, and the like.

Neither of these options is inherently better, but the problem is, Huayi needs to pick one.

It is not an easy choice. Like Lord Six in *Mr. Six*, whether he sticks to his old Beijing ways or changes himself to fit society, either choice carries a certain tragic weight.
