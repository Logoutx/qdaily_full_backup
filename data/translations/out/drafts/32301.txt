These stories are the embryo of the business landscape of today's film and TV entertainment companies. Over the dozen-plus years that the internet has held sway, the story of the videocassette recorder has simply been remade and re-released, over and over.

If, a little over a month ago, Japan's Funai Electric had not announced that it had produced the world's last batch of videocassette recorders (VCRs), there would have been no reason at all to revisit the VCR and the videotape.

Never mind videotapes: streaming has grown surprisingly well, and even the DVD—which just a few years ago still felt threatened—has become an old topic. It is already two or three years since the famous video rental company Blockbuster closed all its stores**.** No one doubts anymore whether Netflix, which got its start renting movie discs, can pull off becoming a video site; what people care about now is when Netflix's own productions like *House of Cards* and *Narcos* will release their next seasons.

With streaming video sites and TV set-top boxes, people have also coined new words to describe this "pleasure"—"binge watching," for instance, meaning "wolfing down shows one episode after another, one season after another."

But think about it, and there is nothing new here at all. The pleasure people get from video sites today was obtained, 40 years ago, through videotapes.

In the golden age of the videotape, people could find some idle time, carry home a dozen-plus cassettes in one go from the rental shop, or use a home VCR to tape a stretch of TV programming themselves; with a little beer to go with it, they could while away an entire day in the living room—the home entertainment stronghold of its time.

The stories surrounding the videotape don't stop there; the subject is complex enough to fill a business textbook. In them, you can learn how a new technology can win the mass market, how Hollywood, shaken by the videotape, began to consider becoming an entertainment company, and whether it is technology or content that holds the high-margin value-added.

All told, these stories are the embryo of the business landscape of today's film and TV entertainment companies. Over the dozen-plus years that the internet has held sway, the story of the videocassette recorder has simply been remade and re-released, over and over.

### How to Win the Mass Market

The videotape can be traced back to 1956, when [the American company Ampex introduced "the first practical videotape recorder"](http://www.ampex.com/news/history.html?start=30). Priced as high as US$50,000, the machine was something only TV networks and large private stations could afford. Even so, it sold 100 units in its first week on the market—almost a full year's revenue for Ampex.

In the past, images on screen followed the linear time of daily life, gone in an instant. For the first time, people discovered that time could be turned back and replayed. Ampex's orders flowed in unceasingly.

![From: [The New York Times](http://www.nytimes.com/2016/07/22/technology/the-long-final-goodbye-of-the-vcr.html?_r=0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160918142856F5NG9ch3MLZOe1Wz.jpg-w600)

By the 1970s and 1980s, the home VCR had become quite widespread among ordinary consumers. People could use it to record TV programs, watch television, and also watch movies. [Analysts estimated at the time that 15 million American households owned a VCR](http://www.nytimes.com/1984/06/30/business/the-boom-in-video-cassettes.html). By [1990](https://www.amazon.com/New-Pot-Gold-Hollywood-Electronic/dp/0520232666), two-thirds of American households had one.

Without question, this was the golden age of the videotape, the best of times for the VCR business.

To fight for the mass market, two Japanese companies—Sony and JVC, a subsidiary of Panasonic—launched two different videotape technologies in 1975 and 1976: Betamax and VHS, each hoping to own the market outright. The battle is regarded as a classic case study in how companies understand consumer demand and control distribution channels. It is also, roughly, the most famous business story ever connected with the VCR.

Sony was always confident that Betamax recorded better picture quality. But in the business world, victory never goes simply to the best, most perfect technology—the one that best meets the need is what wins. Compared with Sony's Betamax, JVC's VHS recorded three times longer. And VHS deliberately welcomed blank tapes and was cheaper to boot. All of this fit consumers' needs of the day far better: what they needed was recording time and the act of recording itself; what they needed was value for money, not the picture quality Sony preened itself on.

What's more, JVC licensed other companies to produce tapes and owned many rental chains, and Hollywood adopted the format, so there were more movies on VHS—ultimately producing a network effect (also known as the fax machine effect). That is, the more people use the VHS format (or, say, the fax machine), the more utility each person gets: you can obtain more resources, more conveniently. This also satisfied consumers' appetite for more film and TV content.

In 1984, 40 companies were making VHS machines, versus only 12 using Betamax. By 1987, Sony formally conceded defeat, and the following year began producing VHS recorders itself.

### Movie Studios? Why Not Become an Entertainment Company

There are always people unhappy with a new technology. Hollywood was one of them.

The movie studios watched with alarm: in 1971, only 15.8 million Americans went to the cinema, whereas in 1929, when movies had only just caught on, the figure was still 95 million. This was partly because people had moved to the suburbs and television had risen, and partly because people could now use videotapes to watch TV programming without restriction.

Hollywood would not sit idly by. In 1976, two of the era's major movie studios, Universal and Disney, sued Sony. The two Hollywood giants argued that consumers using Sony's VCR to record their copyrighted movies without permission constituted copyright infringement; they demanded compensation and demanded that Sony be barred from producing and selling the machine.

True, it was only a year since Sony had introduced its recorder—but the machine's influence already could not be ignored.

The outcome of the lawsuit is easy to guess: seeing how the VCR flourished over the next 20 years, you know Sony more or less won the case. The case worked its way up through the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals, and the U.S. Supreme Court; in 1984, the Supreme Court ruled for Sony, allowing home users to record programs freely.

The significance of that lawsuit for Hollywood continues to this day. They were forced to experiment with distribution channels and profit models beyond the cinema. Why not actively join this new market? The structure of the entertainment business was transformed.

Australian film scholar Richard Maltby (Richard Maltby) wrote in [*Hollywood Cinema: The History of the American Film Industry* (《好莱坞电影：美国电影工业发展史》)](https://book.douban.com/subject/7160867/): "By 1986, the major studios were taking in more revenue from the home market—that is, from videocassettes—than from cinemas. Over the same period, pay-cable subscribers grew from 9 million to 42 million. In 1990, revenue from the videotape rental market reached US$840 million, nearly double the box office."

Because of the videotape, Hollywood began to notice the possibility of "ancillary markets." It was slowly transforming into an entertainment company, not just a movie studio. They could hardly know who, in the future, would capture the most audience attention. The best approach, as industry analyst Harold Vogel (Harold Vogel) put it, was that "the major studios simply wanted to establish an ancillary mechanism—an integrated entertainment complex"—controlling all of the exits.

With Wall Street's encouragement, film companies and TV companies launched a merger wave. In 1985, Murdoch's News Corporation acquired the 20th Century Fox studio and its media and television stations, becoming America's largest independent TV network. In 1990, Time Inc. and Warner Communications merged to form Time Warner. In 1994, Paramount was acquired by Viacom. In 1995, Disney bought the ABC television network. In 1996, Time Warner merged with Turner Broadcasting, and so on.

On the other hand, whether a film could earn well in ancillary distribution channels like videocassette became a key factor in deciding whether it would be made at all. The pre-sold sequel thus became a category Hollywood eagerly embraced. Compared with new films, sequels had a much larger existing user base; the odds and base numbers for renting or buying their tapes were higher; and people usually wanted to buy the prequels and follow-ups as well.

Even today, sequels have increasingly become Hollywood's box-office guarantee. Of Hollywood's top ten box-office champions between 2006 and 2015, eight were sequels.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160918141740KSjYJui6o9plBZkh.jpg-w600)

The arrival of the VCR exposed Hollywood's conservatism, and prompted some appropriate corrections. At first, it was months after a film's theatrical release before Hollywood grudgingly allowed the videotape to come out; afterwards, the "window" steadily shrank. Now people can see newly released movies much sooner on video sites, or buy or rent the discs.

Still, none of that feels as liberating as an entire season of a Netflix original dropped all at once.

### New Technologies Keep Arriving, but the Way to Capture the Market Is Much the Same

The arrival of the DVD player in 1997 foreshadowed the death of the VCR ahead of time. Every new medium is superior to the previous one in size, storage capacity, picture clarity and the like, and offers a richer experience. [Five years later](http://www.nytimes.com/2002/08/26/technology/26DVD.html?pagewanted=all), DVD sales had already surpassed videotape sales. The same year, Sony discontinued the Betamax recorder.

After that, DVD manufacturers went through their own format war, between HD DVD and Blu-ray (Blu-ray Disc)—and the key to victory again lay in licensing the format to more manufacturers.

At first, Hollywood studios such as Paramount, Universal, Warner Bros., New Line and HBO all announced support for HD DVD in Toshiba's camp, while Sony's Blu-ray camp, beyond the MGM and Columbia studios Sony itself owned, won backing only from Disney and Fox.

But then NBC, Universal, Microsoft, Intel, and Viacom's Paramount and DreamWorks announced support for HD DVD. The competition intensified, and things turned in 2008. On January 4, Warner Bros. finally announced it would no longer support HD DVD—directly forcing the HD DVD Promotion Group to cancel its press conference at the International Consumer Electronics Show (CES), and setting off a chain reaction that tipped the whole industry toward Blu-ray.

On February 15, Walmart agreed to sell Blu-ray players exclusively. Four days later, Toshiba announced it was abandoning the HD DVD business. André Haas, Sony's chief marketing officer, said: "We can now let the ghost of Betamax rest in peace."

![Akio Morita, from: [innopedia](http://www.innopedia.jp/detail/%E7%9B%9B%E7%94%B0%20%E6%98%AD%E5%A4%AB/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160918144913tmPqNQl9FcCIoSMn.jpg-w600)

The licensing and rental model that videotapes inaugurated has persisted, without major change. From DVD to online DVD rental and sales, to online digital delivery, and on to Netflix and Amazon—it all comes from this model. Netflix, born in 1997, as you know, spanned film-and-TV subscription services across DVD, Blu-ray and streaming over the following two decades. Streaming technology also appeared at the end of the twentieth century, and Netflix grabbed onto it fast.

But people now generally believe that Netflix's transformation and rise reveal that high-margin, value-added products are shifting toward content. Just as, back then, the film library was of immense value to the major studios—this was their real competitive edge, the part that was hard to surpass overnight. According to Richard Maltby, in 2000 the major studios' film libraries were valued at US$8 billion—equivalent to 20 times their 1980 worth.

### Make the Technology, or Make the Content?

Make the technology, or make the content? This question applies equally to Sony, the VCR maker of those early years.

Founded in 1946 by Akio Morita and Masaru Ibuka, Sony carried a strong engineering gene, devoted to innovating and manufacturing consumer electronics. In 1946, Ibuka wrote in Sony's founding prospectus: "We want to establish a stable company where engineers can devote themselves wholeheartedly to their work, knowing that they are enjoying the pleasure of technology while fulfilling their obligations to society." From Sony came world-class products like Trinitron picture-tube technology, the Walkman and the PlayStation.

But on September 25, 1989, led by founder Akio Morita, [Sony announced the US$3.4 billion acquisition of Columbia Pictures](https://book.douban.com/subject/19912117/), taking on US$1.6 billion of the company's attached debt. At the same time, Columbia's film library became Sony's property from then on. Owning the IP, Sony could draw a steady stream of revenue from releasing videotapes, DVDs and streaming—and from licensing adaptations.

At the time, this was the largest acquisition of an American company by a Japanese company, and Americans voiced worry and displeasure. *Newsweek*'s cover showed the Statue of Liberty—emblem of the American spirit—dressed in a Japanese kimono, under the headline "Japan Invades Hollywood."

![From: [wxrw123](http://www.wxrw123.com/hq/20160813/2286486.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160918143458E0T94ac2lnFmR3pS.jpg-w600)

"You Americans will never understand! Look at the videotape format war. Sony's Betamax format was technically superior, but Hollywood chose VHS. By acquiring a Hollywood movie company, we can make sure this never happens again!" [said Akio Morita in response to Brian Roberts, CEO of Comcast, America's largest cable company](https://book.douban.com/subject/26309514/).

The clashes over videotapes with American entertainment companies taught this Japanese company what it takes to hold the discursive power in the entertainment industry, and Sony as a whole shifted from technology toward content. In 1999, Sony welcomed its first CEO not from an engineering background, Nobuyuki Idei. He decided to turn Sony into a "media and technology company." In 2004, Sony acquired MGM again.

In 2005, Howard Stringer, an American, succeeded Idei. This former journalist turned film producer wasn't thinking about Sony's engineering culture either. "Without content, most devices are no different from garbage," Stringer said. "Like the orbits of a new world circling an ancient sun, the planet of broadband entertainment will revolve around content. Sony is first and foremost an entertainment company."

Then Kazuo Hirai took over, and in 2012 Sony sold its chemicals business; in 2013, it sold its headquarters building; in 2014, it sold its personal computer business (VAIO) and spun off its TV business into a separate company; in 2016, it sold its battery business.

A quarterly earnings report Sony recently released shows that the traditional consumer electronics business has been stuck in long-term losses, while the audio-visual, games and entertainment divisions keep turning profits. In the first quarter of fiscal 2016, the games and network services segment booked US$3.208 billion in sales revenue, up 14.5% year on year.

No company ever became a giant by making videocassette recorders alone. Funai Electric, still making the last batch of VCRs? You've probably never even heard of it.
