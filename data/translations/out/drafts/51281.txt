"Creativity is in our DNA" — nonsense! How could it be? Creativity only exists in education and culture.

In 2006, 40-year-old Marcus Fairs was fired from *Icon* magazine — the design magazine he had helped found, which had just entered its fourth year. He hesitated about starting over with another print publication. Marcus turned to the older architecture magazine *Blueprint*, asking its founder how one could build a magazine as great as *Blueprint* from scratch. The answer: a manager with ideas, a group of capable writers, and at least £500,000.

Marcus never found the £500,000. He spent £10 on a domain name and another £10 (yes) to have a friend build a test version. The blog was named Dezeen.

Dezeen's earliest content consisted of project materials from architecture firms and designers' drawings. At the time, Marcus's designer friends hadn't even heard of a "blog" and sent over their press kits half-believing, half-doubting. Thomas Heatherwick, who would go on to design the "dandelion"-like UK pavilion at the Shanghai Expo, told Marcus point-blank: "You must be mad. Who would ever want to look at architectural drawings on a website?!"

Dezeen is a blend of the English words "design" and "magazine," but to this day, some people still open their emails with "Dear Mr. Dezeen." "Many people assume our boss is a man named Dezeen," Marcus said on March 17, looking back on the 12-year-old online magazine at the [Shanghai "Design Republic · Design Celebration" event](http://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/jTmzf6wtMjkDjE1KSBi_gQ). Today it has become the world's best-known architecture and design website.

![Marcus Fairs (Image / Dezeen.com)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320131912LhQjqYEKUXi1o4km.jpg-w600)

### The "Golden Age" of Blogging

Aside from the earlier Designboom (founded in Italy in 1999), Dezeen was one of the first online magazines, and it encouraged a wave of design- and architecture-focused online platforms that followed, including Archdaily. In an exclusive interview with QDaily, Marcus said that in the beginning they were like the flustered "pioneers" of the American West.

"People from print were used to a whole rhythm — slow and rigorous: find the story, write the story, hand it to an editor for revision, then layout, proofreading, printing, and finally the newsstand. Suddenly, all of that was compressed, as fast as a single click of the mouse."

Beyond coping with the occasional traffic overload, Marcus and his colleagues also had to deal with the website suddenly "disappearing." The company where the domain was originally registered never reminded Marcus that it needed renewing; one day they found the site gone, with an email demanding a payment of £500 to "redeem" it.

"In a situation like that, you don't have time to argue," Marcus said, explaining his obsession with "speed" — every second counted. To this day, Dezeen still maintains a rapid pace of news updates.

"Pioneering" also included a city's infrastructure. Once, Marcus brought his computer on holiday to a city in Italy, but there was no internet there; he had to drive an hour to a nearby town that had it, finish editing his piece, and drive back.

"Now everyone has a phone, and Wi-Fi is standard in cafés. But a decade or so ago, you had to drive somewhere just to get 'close' to the internet. Looking back, that's pretty bizarre, isn't it?"

Two months after Dezeen launched, the Design Museum in London — then preparing a solo exhibition for Zaha Hadid — came to them, hoping they could design a blog specifically for the show. No one expected that tickets sold through that blog would exceed the combined total of every other sales channel.

"After that, people no longer wondered whether I was crazy, and companies soon came knocking, wanting to place ads on the site. But at the time, we didn't even know how to insert an ad into a website."

At that year's Miami design festival, Marcus and his wife were invited to São Paulo, Brazil, to shoot a documentary about the Campana brothers. They bought camera equipment and plane tickets on the spur of the moment, but had no clue about documentary filmmaking — the sound quality was, by his own account, "terrible." When the editing was finally done, the rough documentary became the only exhibit at that year's design week to use digital media. Nobody had time to discuss its level of finish; Marcus called it the "golden age" of blogs and new technology.

### "We Care About Brands, and Also About the Seemingly Glamorous Practitioners"

In its first years, Dezeen quickly gained recognition, but people also questioned its credibility.

Marcus's early description of it sounded more like an advertising agency: "We're good at telling stories; we can help designers and brands unearth their own stories and then publish them through our platform." It was more like an empty shell pieced together from a mass of up-to-the-minute information, without an "attitude" of its own.

"Many people knew me from the critical essays I used to publish in print. They knew me as a 'troublemaker,' so the strange thing was why he would settle for serving up a mere platter of news."

Marcus said this was a deliberate choice at the start: focus on news, or on interviews, presenting the information itself without too much commentary. "I knew that one day we would do real journalism, but critical pieces take time, and we couldn't afford to wait then — honestly, we didn't even have the budget to pay our writers."

The turning point came at Milan Design Week 2011, when Marcus and a handful of journalists began discussing on Twitter the plight of young designers.

![Logo for #milanuncut](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320133815vEWTg5CBpFIaPN4b.jpg-w600)

The stars of the show floor were the well-known furniture brands, which used young designers' work to fill out their booths, draw eyeballs, or burnish the brand's reputation — and the media also praised these brands for encouraging innovation. But in reality, the vast majority of the pieces shown would never actually go into production.

The Twitter discussion was tagged #milanuncut, and journalists and young designers joined the fray, accusing Milan Design Week of becoming a monopolistic platform. Young designers had to bring their work to Milan, sign unequal contracts with brands, stand beside the booths and pitch to show-goers. The lucky ones got their pieces into production, and the royalties a designer could earn amounted to just 5% of the wholesale price at which the pieces were sold to the brands. If nobody bit, the designer didn't see a penny from the "samples."

The Guardian interviewed a designer of some reputation whose five pieces had all been picked up for production by manufacturers, yet in the previous year his royalty income was only £600 — barely enough to cover half a month's rent.

The questions Marcus and his colleagues brought into the discussion included: Are the contracts designers sign with brands fair? Is the royalty share appropriate? Who pockets the bulk of the money? Is there a better way to help young designers start their own businesses?

The media itself also became an object of their reflection: for a long time, the media had been an accomplice in this "exploitative" relationship within the design world. "You notice that everyone trots out the same line when talking to young designers: think about the exposure, the contacts you can make at the event…"

![Milan Design Week (Image / [Dezeen.com](https://www.dezeen.com/2018/02/20/tom-dixon-milan-design-week-news/))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320135731AMYTqe7zG9EORmhP.jpg-w600)

It was traditional media such as The Guardian that pushed the Twitter discussion to a deeper level, critiquing the design royalty system established in the 1950s. Back then, Italian home design targeted the middle class and was mass-produced, so designers could earn a decent income from it. But by 2011, with the arrival of Chinese furniture makers and mid-to-low-priced brands like IKEA, Italian furniture repositioned itself as a luxury product and cut production drastically.

In 2013, Dezeen covered crowdfunding platforms where designers could launch their own design projects. Marcus described this as an experiment in "decentralization" — Milan Design Week was no longer the most important, or even the only, showcase platform.

That report appeared in a new Dezeen section. Unlike posts announcing new design products or the completion of new buildings, this section, called "Opinion," began tackling serious issues.

"Judging by the numbers, it doesn't bring the site more traffic, but it gives us intellectual weight in the industry."

### "I'm a Journalist, Not a Designer"

The new section invited a large roster of contributors. Recent topics they have debated include: opposition to the large-scale demolition of postwar buildings in the UK; whether the Sony Tower on Manhattan's Madison Avenue (the former AT&T Building) deserves to be preserved; how, in the name of creating new urban places, architects have become accomplices to "social cleansing"; and how, in discussions of Los Angeles's future city planning, those with a seat at the table are almost all white architects — which hardly represents the city's diversity or people's real needs.

The discussion Marcus and his team recently initiated themselves concerned gender inequality in the architecture profession. They invited many well-known design firms to take part in a survey tallying the male-to-female ratio of designers at different levels. "Of course there are women designers in architecture, but you find that women are proportionally more numerous at the lower levels, and the higher you go, the fewer women there are."

"In the UK, the way traditional media are used to protesting is to launch a campaign — 'we demand this law be amended,' that sort of thing. But I don't like calling them campaigns; it sounds a bit old-fashioned, a bit self-important. We just call them agendas."

At last year's Dutch Design Week, Marcus organized a series of talks, "Good Design for a Bad World," with five agendas covering climate change, the refugee crisis, terrorism, environmental pollution, and politics.

Some suspected this strayed from a design week's proper remit; Marcus replied, "I'm a journalist, not a designer."

"Take the [refugee question](https://www.dezeen.com/2017/12/18/dont-design-shelter-refugees-kilian-kleinschmidt-rene-boer-good-design-bad-world/#disqus_thread): many designers believe design can solve the refugee crisis. But the solutions they propose are life jackets made from old materials, or an app that helps you locate relief supplies — honestly, I've had enough of it. However good their intentions, none of this helps in the slightest!"

Marcus invited the manager of a refugee camp to join the discussion. Refugees are not a "special group," he argued, and don't need "design for refugees" — like everyone else, most of them are educated and tech-savvy. "The problem right now is that the vast majority of policies assume these refugees will eventually be sent back to where they came from, so most design can only be 'temporary.'"

Marcus called it the most exhausting project he has ever done. "Its purpose is not to propose solutions or draw up design plans, but to reflect: where are the boundaries of what design can reach? Can design make a difference? The answer may be yes, or it may be no."

"I understand that some new media no longer value agenda setting, but for us, it has always been what we want to do."

**A = Marcus Fairs**

**Q: Your observations about Milan Design Week are interesting — you discuss exhibitors, and beyond the traditional brands, the participation of new brands like Google and Instagram. Why do these matter?**

A: Wherever I go in the world, people press me for my views on Milan. Let me ask you a question instead: do you really care about Milan? Why?

In 2013 I once wrote a critical piece about Milan. Beyond the fair itself, the services the city could offer were woefully inadequate: hotel rates that spiked every holiday, terrible public transport — I couldn't even make sense of the subway map. With the sudden influx of tourists, I had to wait by the roadside in the rain for taxis that never came. I used pretty harsh language at the time, something like "Visiting Milan during design week, you feel the opposite of design everywhere you go."

I was sick at the time, and I'll admit it wasn't neutral journalism — it was a personal essay. After it was published, the brands that had been friendly to me were pretty disappointed; for a long time I had been getting VIP passes, dinner invitations, and gifts from them.

A month or so later, Claudio Luti, then chairman of Milan Design Week, asked me in for a chat. I was nervous going in, not knowing what to expect, and it turned out he completely agreed with me. He took some measures — working with hotels to try to curb price increases, talking to people in the city about cooperation. He hit setbacks, left for a while, and has now come back.

**Q: That seems hard to imagine in China today, where many cities eagerly host all kinds of creative events.**

A: Looking back now, it's unfair to be too hard on a city like that. It's an industrial city in northeastern Italy — not the most beautiful, and the climate isn't exactly great either. It's just that because an important fair is held there every year, people from all over the world are drawn in, and the city's chaos is caused by an excessive influx of outsiders — a challenge for any city. It was never designed to cater to the needs of these outsiders in the first place.

The Milan government took a long time to transform, and the city has become more "civilized" now. New York is the same — it used to be hard to imagine getting around New York by bike; it was a city occupied by cars, and now it's becoming more and more livable.

Neither the criticism then nor the praise now is really information the fairgrounds themselves can provide. I'm just an observer, coming back at the same time each year, slowly discovering how it changes.

**Q: Is this related to how much a city values its creative industries?**

A: Of course. Milan is really just a furniture trade fair — to put it bluntly, selling chairs, tables, and lamps. But once it reached a certain scale and drew people from the design world across the globe, it started to get interesting. The media and the design elite are all there. All kinds of brands can try to figure out what's going on — not just the ones that make furniture, but new brands too: Google, Apple, Uber, Instagram, Facebook. Their people are on the ground as well; at this year's Milan Design Week, Google and Instagram were exhibitors. What they do is not display new products and say, "Look, here's our new phone" — they try to join the conversation.

China is placing more and more emphasis on creative industries — I don't know much about it firsthand, but judging by the density of events being held, that seems to be the case. In the UK, by contrast, the government is actually paying less and less attention to the creative industries, cutting funding for related programs at colleges and universities and discouraging students from applying. It seems like the same old Western arrogance — assuming everything China makes is a knockoff or a fake, while we've created so many legendary successful brands, "creativity is in our DNA." Nonsense! How could it be? Creativity only exists in education and culture.
