From Mass-Market to High-End and Back Again: What Happened Between a 50-Year-Old Creative Director and a 50-Year-Old Fashion Brand?

@@QD_BODY@@
In under two years, an experiment in brand transformation was declared a failure — but Raf Simons and the Calvin Klein Group at least put two rarely-asked questions to the test.

On December 21 local time, Calvin Klein announced it was parting ways with creative director Raf Simons, effective immediately. That was 8 months before the two sides' contract was due to expire, and less than two years since Raf Simons had taken the job amid enormous fanfare.

When the partnership began two years ago, the media called it "[the most high-profile brand-reinvention experiment in American fashion in the 21st century" and "a fairy tale"](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/12/21/fashion/raf-simons-out-at-calvin-klein.html). Calvin Klein declared its ambition at the time to lift annual revenue from $8 billion to $10 billion, and [the founder said enthusiastically](http://wwd.com/media-news/media-features/calvin-klein-raf-simons-mark-wahlberg-bill-clinton-10448452/): "They've finally made some of the design changes I've long been waiting for — truly replacing me by finding someone with extraordinary vision who can also take command of the creative side."

Two years on, the partnership broke down. There will be no Calvin Klein show at New York Fashion Week next February, and it may be a while before we learn when — or whether — a successor will be named.

Both parties declined to explain the split. [The official statement put it this way](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/news-analysis/raf-simons-exits-calvin-klein): "The Calvin Klein Group has decided to change the direction of the brand, and this diverged from Raf Simons's creative vision. The two sides subsequently agreed to part ways amicably."

"The only thing that's clear to onlookers is that this is no longer a fashion fairy tale. The story has turned cautious," [Vanessa Friedman, fashion director of The New York Times, commented](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/12/21/fashion/raf-simons-out-at-calvin-klein.html).

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181224063910aJKYgCi6wZqbSjzl.jpg-w600)

In fact, the rift had been quite public since the third-quarter earnings investor call the previous month. Calvin Klein's operating earnings before interest and taxes for the quarter fell from $142 million a year earlier to $121 million. Overall brand revenue slid 2% to $963 million. The stock is down 35% this year.

Emanuel Chirico, CEO of parent company PVH, unexpectedly pinned the third-quarter decline on "a $1 million increase in creative and marketing spend" — in other words, creative director Raf Simons was to blame.

Chirico also called two of Raf Simons's major product-line moves "a fashion miss": "While most product categories performed well, we saw little return on our investment in Calvin Klein 205W39NYC, the 'halo business.' We also believe the relaunched Calvin Klein Jeans line was positioned too high-end and did not sell as well as planned."

It's rare for a fashion group's business managers to publicly blame a sitting creative director. The last comparably seismic split dates to 2015, when Alexander Wang left Balenciaga after less than three years on the job — though both sides likewise described that as an "amicable parting" on every occasion.

To call Raf Simons's arrival at Calvin Klein "a fairy tale" is hardly an exaggeration. It put at least two rarely-asked questions to the test: Can a star creative director help a mass-market brand transform quickly into a high-end one? And if a brand wants commercial success, should it grant its creative director greater control, so that his aesthetic vision can be carried through?

Whether for a 50-year-old designer or a 50-year-old brand, you probably only get one chance to answer these two questions.

Fifty years after its founding, the American brand Calvin Klein has had real commercial success but has never been known for "leading fashion." It began with women's coats, and by the 1970s had added sportswear, classic jackets, and underwear to its womenswear. After being acquired by PVH in 2002, Calvin Klein developed three main product lines: high fashion (Calvin Klein Collection), designer ready-to-wear (CK Calvin Klein), and the denim line Calvin Klein Jeans. It also makes fragrance, eyewear, socks, underwear, sleepwear, swimwear, and home furnishings.

Yet the widespread perception of Calvin Klein remains that of a "mass brand" built mainly on selling minimalist underwear and jeans. These two product lines have long been its most profitable businesses. It is minimal and sexy, but has little to do with high fashion. And until now, few got excited about a Calvin Klein runway show. According to former CEO Tom Murry, the runway collection was originally booked as "marketing spend" on the brand's balance sheet — it didn't even count as product-development investment.

The change came from Calvin Klein's commercial ambition to push annual revenue past $10 billion in short order. It followed the playbook of Gucci and Alessandro Michele, Yves Saint Laurent and Hedi Slimane, and Coach and Stuart Vevers — making a star creative director the core of the brand's success story, the "halo" in CEO Emanuel Chirico's words. They hoped not only that the creative director's arrival would lend a "halo effect" to the lower-priced products, but that the high-end ready-to-wear business would be more than a brand-marketing exercise and become a genuine driver of results.

Raf Simons was the bearer of these hopes. Low-key, reserved, and sensitive, but not without nerve; a devotee of minimalist design, but also obsessed with contemporary art and youth culture.

The Belgian founded his eponymous label as early as 1995, but what made his name were two "firefighting" stints: first carrying forward Jil Sander's minimalist aesthetic after its founder departed, then, after John Galliano was fired from Dior over his antisemitic-remarks scandal, overturning Galliano's theatrical staging and returning Dior to elegance. After three years at Dior, however, frustrated that his creative work was confined to the women's line, he left.

Calvin Klein granted Raf Simons unprecedented control. He earned a multimillion-dollar annual salary, received the title of "chief creative officer," and was put in charge of design and marketing across the entire business — women's, men's, denim, underwear, and more. Previously, that work had been divided among three designers: Francisco Costa, Italo Zucchelli, and Kevin Corrigan.

This bold partnership faced extremely high risk and pressure. Raf Simons had always served high-end brands and had little mass-marketing experience, yet the mass market was Calvin Klein's main source of profit. Nor was he as unknown as Alessandro Michele had been when he arrived at Gucci, able to face the doubters with the bravado of someone with nothing to lose.

It was also hard to say whether PVH, a listed company with little experience running a high-end brand, could truly steel itself — as it claimed — for the enormous investment the transformation required and for the time investors and consumers would need to accept the brand's reinvention, especially faced with a consumer and investment market growing ever less patient.

The changes Raf Simons brought were plain to see. Beyond the new logo, the advertising that once foregrounded sexy young bodies was replaced by cooler, more art-driven visual concepts. This caused some controversy. The high-end ready-to-wear line was renamed 205W39NYC, taken from the address of Calvin Klein's New York headquarters at 205 West 39th Street, and appeared on the runway with a brand-new identity. Some complained the name was too much of a mouthful, too unrelatable.

Raf Simons was unafraid to use fashion to talk politics. At the February 2018 show — a year after Donald Trump was sworn in as U.S. president — he blanketed the floor of the American Stock Exchange with 50,000 gallons of white popcorn, opening and closing the show to David Bowie's song *This Is Not America*. "My understanding of Calvin Klein is also my understanding of American society," he said. Critics raved about the show, concluding that Calvin Klein had finally given New York Fashion Week — where brands had increasingly been decamping and which had grown dull from excessive commercialization — something worth watching.

Over the past two years, the American fashion industry handed him three of its top awards: the 2017 and 2018 CFDA Womenswear Designer of the Year awards, and the 2017 CFDA Menswear Designer of the Year award.

But Raf Simons also spent a great deal of money. He had artist Sterling Ruby remake the Madison Avenue flagship store, launched Calvin Klein by Appointment — the brand's first made-to-measure line — and signed a multi-year partnership with the Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts.

[PVH CEO Emanuel Chirico later told analysts](https://wwd.com/fashion-news/designer-luxury/raf-simons-and-calvin-klein-part-ways-1202940935/) that the group had invested $60–70 million in the 205W39NYC line over the past three years.

The high-end ready-to-wear line drew decent initial feedback. At least in the first season, the number of stores carrying the line grew from 30 to 300. Raf Simons did think about how to balance high-end sensibility with "relatability." He visited executives at Macy's and brought in the Kardashian family as models for Calvin Klein underwear and denim campaigns. More and more young celebrities turned up front row: Hollywood actor Jake Gyllenhaal, Motofumi Kogi of Japanese streetwear label United Arrows, and rapper A$AP Rocky.

As recently as this March, PVH still seemed to trust Raf Simons completely, praising publicly on every occasion the endorsement the 205W39NYC line lent the other product lines.

But over the past 8 months, its patience ran out quickly. The reason was simple: Raf Simons's designs failed to translate into steadily growing numbers on the earnings report — just as the unwieldy name "205W39NYC" failed to stick in most people's minds.

In February this year, PVH brought in former L'Oréal executive Marie Gulin-Merle as chief marketing officer, but she reported not to Raf Simons — she reported to brand CEO Steve Shiffman. The CMO subsequently pulled marketing funds away from Raf Simons's highly conceptual creative advertising and redirected them toward influencers and social media. Every promised new-concept store quietly went nowhere. In September, a runway show originally scheduled for the ground floor of the headquarters building was cancelled over budget constraints.

[According to BoF](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/news-analysis/raf-simons-exits-calvin-klein), multiple sources also said PVH worried that Raf Simons's partnership with the Andy Warhol Foundation was "too artsy, too high-end" for the mass market to accept — even though PVH itself was the one that had initiated the pivot to high-end.

PVH CEO Emanuel Chirico's remarks after the November earnings release amounted to a final verdict on all these changes: "From a product perspective, we went too far and too fast in terms of fashion and price." [He added](https://www.businessoffashion.com/articles/news-analysis/raf-simons-exits-calvin-klein) that the group would cut investment in the 205W39NYC line and move in a more commercial direction. Marketing focus would also shift from high fashion toward products aimed at a mainstream audience and priced more accessibly. It sounded like the Calvin Klein of two years ago.

This costly attempt at transformation, undertaken at enormous risk and under heavy pressure, was declared a failure.

Summing up Calvin Klein and Raf Simons's two-year attempt as "critically acclaimed but a commercial flop" would be too facile.

["A company of Calvin Klein's size needs a creative person, a certain amount of time, and a lot of money to transform. Calvin Klein wasn't built in a day, or in 18 months. This is a subtle and complex challenge. It seems neither side anticipated how heavy the task would be," luxury-industry consultant Robert Burke of Robert Burke Associates told The New York Times.](https://www.nytimes.com/2018/12/21/fashion/raf-simons-out-at-calvin-klein.html) His view was representative of much of the commentary: more than blaming either party, people felt regret over the failure of the experiment.

Or one could say Raf Simons's departure lets people re-examine what "failure" even means in the fashion industry. The year Raf Simons took the job, Anja Aronowsky Cronberg, editor-in-chief of the fashion academic journal *Vestoj*, gathered 14 practitioners to discuss *[“What’s Wrong with the Fashion Industry”](http://vestoj.com/whats-wrong-with-the-fashion-industry/)*. Two years on, some of what they said still applies.

> In fashion, failure means not selling.
>
> Ralph Toledano, president of the French women's ready-to-wear and couture federations, and chairman of the designer brand Victoria Beckham

> Success in fashion today can be measured by how many $5,000 bags you can sell. That determines whether a designer gets to stay in the creative director's chair. How many bags you sell matters far more than what fashion critics like Vanessa Friedman, Suzy Menkes, or me say.
>
> Nicole Phelps, director of Vogue Runway

> Most managers and CEOs today come from business school. They're smart, strategic, and good at making money, but they don't love fashion and don't know what's changing on the street. So they need young designers like Humberto Leon and Carol Lim of Opening Ceremony to run Kenzo, a brand under LVMH. They represent the younger generation. But all they do is generate buzz; the collections themselves quickly become boring and meaningless.
>
> Motofumi Kogi, director of Japanese streetwear label United Arrows

> In fashion, success or failure cannot measure whether you are a talented designer. The most talented designers in the world fail too. Countless breathtakingly talented designers have had to shut down their labels because they weren't commercially successful. Critical acclaim doesn't last. If you don't know how to turn a creative vision into commercially viable products, this industry will spit you out.
>
> Jean-Jacques Picart, fashion and luxury consultant
