On September 27, the Global Habitat Innovation Conference was held in Beijing. Architects from 15 countries, including the United States, France, Poland, Finland, Slovenia, Japan, Canada and the Netherlands, took part in the event, which belongs to the academic program of the 7th Beijing International Design Week.

At the Global Habitat Innovation Conference, organized by elab, City Window and E-House, these 42 architects brought their signature works and latest architectural ideas, exploring topics such as "green buildings and future trends in human habitat," "the integrated expression and design trends of new cultural and commercial ecosystems," "slow living in landscape," "the commercial transplantation of hybrid cultural buildings," and "the N possibilities of future human habitat" — through keynote speeches by guest speakers, thematic talks by designers, and salon discussions.

Although these 42 architects mainly used their own classic projects as presentation material, amid these grand topics they all, without prior agreement, kept coming back to a few questions — the relationship between people and nature, the relationship between buildings and nature, and the relationship between buildings and the city. And of course, in discussing these topics, they also talked about their own architectural practices and their projects in China.

Of course, these 42 designers did not come to Beijing Design Week purely for academic reasons — they also wanted to seize the opportunity to show their faces in the Chinese market.

"In the United States, I've attended many conferences like this for architects, and the organizers also invite real estate developers, but in general the developers are unwilling to come. The Global Habitat Innovation Conference is different — it's hosted by Chinese real estate developers, and they have come here to talk with us face to face," American architect Andrew Philip Heid told QDaily.

The Global Habitat Innovation Conference ran for two days, and the amount of information was enormous. We have summarized their remarks; the highlights are below.

### 1. What are they all concerned about?

**1) Joachim H. Faust, president of HPP and a German architect: we must observe certain basic principles in design.**

HPP is Germany's largest architectural planning and design firm; 20% of its business is in Germany and 80% abroad, in cities such as Shanghai and Istanbul.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609291424011FbouzyphrdGetHD.png-w600)

> "What we do is important in itself, and at the same time our audience is also important, so I think this positioning matters. When I was still a young designer, I came to understand that we design not just for ourselves — we design for developers, for owners, for users, for the people who use urban spaces and buildings. So the values of design are fundamental to us."

> "A very important factor in the value of design is its sense of identity, or identification, or distinctiveness. It may reflect a company's identity — what it produces, what line of business it is in — and it can even involve a city's identity, a city's landmarks."

**2) Stefano Boeri Architetti: urban expansion means the disappearance of some natural landscapes**

> "At first, cities accounted for only 10% [of the land], later they grew to 50%. In the future they may reach 70%, which means that by then some of nature's landscapes will be lost. Cities are expanding, which means animals are declining — they face the challenge of extinction."

**3) French architect Jean Pierre Heim: in China I also devote a lot of energy to designing nature reserves**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609291448074QXFIAZty1BUz5pm.jpg-w600)

> "In China I also devote a lot of energy to designing nature reserves. Recently we were in Chongqing and found that it is a very large city, with a population of 30 million. I want to create a piece of land and restore it to its original, unspoiled state. What we do is landscape planning and the protection of nature, and of course we also want to raise people's awareness of protecting nature. At the same time, in our projects we have to ensure that people can live here very comfortably, with a very good living environment."

**4) Finnish architect Pekka Salminen: the problem with China's theaters is that these buildings are expensive and underused**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929144948n4mqGINoEDlfa9wY.jpg-w600)

"Many theaters around the world actually make good money. **The problem with China's theaters is that these buildings are expensive and underused — most of the time they are closed, closed at night, and generally closed during the day too.** But theaters or performance spaces should be more profitable, or should pay attention to economic viability, as well as environmental protection and ecology, and be easy to use. That is the challenge facing China's cultural complexes."

"**Where are the profitable theaters and concert halls?** Think of Broadway in New York, or the West End in London. **In China, theaters must be built as hybrids.** Take the Fuzhou Strait Cultural Center I designed: of the five theaters under construction there, the core idea was to make it a complex that combines with many more functions, plus commercial buildings. In the end it genuinely has different functions, including theaters as well as exhibition halls and a film center — in essence a cultural and commercial center."

### 2. Some architectural concepts that kept coming up

**1) Stefano Boeri Architetti: the Vertical Forest**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929143226nOLzyGi1bKaYgtIE.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609291434199aIiPOnEFRLl5Z04.jpg-w600)

"The Vertical Forest project lets [a building] grow from the bottom all the way up, and can reduce urban traffic congestion and air pollution. This benefits both living space and urban space; for us, this system further optimizes the use of the city."

"After these two cities and prototypes, we began to think about how Chinese cities should do overall planning and how to draw on the Vertical Forest concept. **In this way, the forest can be concentrated vertically inside buildings, improving the quality of the city and its surroundings. At the same time, these facades absorb sunlight, provide insulation, and block out pollution and noise.** We have also begun to think that these ideas and concepts can be applied in different places. We can do Vertical Forest projects in different locations, but of course we have to choose different trees, different species, different plants — that depends on the local temperature."

**2) American architect Andrew Philip Heid: the heat index**

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929145927Sg1OhH2AKqfiI8jv.jpg-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929220011dBVSgibmU3RXCDQ4.jpg-w600)

"Globally, whether heating or cooling, together they account for 50% of greenhouse gases. The other half of energy is consumed inside buildings. This is not only a global problem but also a problem of cities and of architecture — we have cities full of high-rise buildings, including structures of reinforced concrete, as well as highways. We retain a great deal of heat in our cities: during the day you can see urban temperatures rise by 9 to 12 degrees, and for every degree of increase, electricity consumption rises by 4.5%."

"**We discovered a very simple concept, which is a heat index — in fact we linked humidity, solar energy and wind energy together in our research.**"

**3) Japanese architect Takanori Ban: hybrid concrete-and-timber structures**

"We emphasize combining concrete and wood to form a more sustainable and more resilient structure — designs handled with the concept of hybrid structures."

**4) The American architect: experience-driven design**

![Kimshasa Baldwin is a designer from Chicago, USA, who specializes in hotel design.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929144643XUACLewbBgcklm4O.png-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929214725elDq3U2nrZ4FgG5i.png-w600)

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160929214819ECdpT5L6SUgwXMWK.png-w600)

"We do structural geometric interior design; we are an experience-driven design studio. What does that mean? Say you go out at night — whether you spend a few hours dining in a restaurant or take a seven-day vacation — we think about how to make the experience perfect. Whether it's a dining experience or a resort experience, we consider your various needs from your point of view, and then start designing."

### 3. Some topics that sparked debate

**1) How to preserve a city's memory and heritage in urban renewal.**

Joachim H. Faust: "**The element of memory is very important; in a city it represents development and is passed down from generation to generation.** A city's memory can be a very important factor that makes you feel very comfortable about a place. Certain materials, for example — the surfaces of these old trees — create fond memories of an environment. At the same time, you also need some easily identifiable landmark buildings; they can be gardens, buildings, or streets and rivers. Every element has a strong power to summon memory, making people consider the identity of the place."

"**So in our designs, we consider that the surrounding environment should possess vitality.** Take the area around Shanghai's World Expo park: there are residents and hotel residences nearby, and it used to be a shipyard. We kept some tower cranes and warehouses as preservation of memory, reminding local people what this place used to be. When we consider the original life and activities and make improvements, we should also preserve a portion of that historical memory — and if it can be continued, all the better."

![Slovenian architect Dean Lah](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609291501560xiBDhZrUw1XajGF.png-w600)

Slovenian architect Dean Lah: "I come from Europe, where there are also many historic sites in urban areas; the preservation of memory is ongoing work. No city's transformation happens overnight — it must be a gradual process. **In the process of urban renovation and development, every step should carry some continuity from the step before it.** If we take it slowly, urban development stays anchored to the ground beneath it.

"Another question is whether residents' hopes and habits should be kept unchanged. **We speak of three kinds of space: personal space, public space and semi-public space.** If we keep these three properties of space in mind, we know what to do when we think about urban renovation."

**2) The practical problems of the "Vertical Forest"**

Zhou Yanmin, a professor at Tsinghua University's School of Architecture who studies senior-living real estate: "China's elderly population is 220 million; by 2050, people aged 60 and over will account for more than 30% of the national population. **If we build housing and develop residential compounds in the future, we cannot avoid considering the needs of this elderly population.** In fact, this kind of green building is something everyone yearns for — the elderly need this natural environment even more; they also need to breathe such fresh air."

"In practice we find that everyone yearns for houses like the Vertical Forest, but there are some contradictions. For example, such a green building can't work in the North — in winter the leaves all fall off. And in the North balconies must be enclosed; even if we achieve the greening, the outside is still glass. During the design process we also tried double-height voided balconies — two stories — but in the end we still had to make balconies. There are also problems with greenery on residential buildings: housing has become one household per unit, and an individual household can't maintain this part well — their patch of vegetation may end up looking bad."

**3) In urban planning and renewal, how to balance the economic and the cultural?**

Dong Fang, vice president of Shum Yip Group: "Forty years ago Shenzhen was a small city of just a few tens of thousands of people; now it has a population of 20 million. Shenzhen used to be a cultural desert, but we believe that establishing and shaping good culture greatly improves our economic returns. A project we are currently developing and selling in Shenzhen is about 500 years old, and next to the project there is an old ancestral hall. The whole village bears the surname Zhang, and the village's culture is fairly cohesive. During the renovation, we paid particular attention to how to preserve the distinctive culture of the village's Zhang clan, keeping some of its special symbols. At the same time we communicated a lot with the villagers, hoping they would support some of our development and construction ideas."

"**We also made some 'sacrifices,' giving up part of the public space** so that some of the villagers' special customs and activities could be held every year on important festivals. Having actually built it, we feel the effect is still very good, and it has won the recognition of the local villagers. We can say we have also achieved fairly good economic results. So we believe that if you respect the local culture while elevating it, it greatly helps our economic returns."

Feng Jinyi, vice chairman and president of Shenzhou Lvdu Real Estate Group: "A few years ago, the Peninsula Hotel — how it carries on from the old British Council building and the Rockefeller buildings behind it — is a classic. Beijing's 798 can still produce some boutique work. But the problem we run into is not the existence of a few boutiques — combining with culture can still yield something, and bringing in foreign architects can provide us with great help.

"China now faces an ever faster pace of urban renewal and iteration; in fact, houses from the 1980s are facing so-called old-city renovation or renewal. **But after so many years of such renovation, the complexity of urban renewal is unimaginable, because it involves the original residents, migrant populations and also agricultural populations — problems that foreign designers can hardly encounter.**"

**4) In building something fully ecological, how big is the gap between construction cost and the architecture you want to express?**

**French architect Jean Pierre Heim:** "Of course there will be some extra costs, but at the same time you save a lot of costs. For example, improving people's quality of life — from this angle you are saving costs, and it can also raise everyone's awareness of protecting the environment.

"For developers, their responsibility is to put their energy and work into raising people's awareness. For example, many hotels want to provide good food, a good spa, a good gym — in fact there may be a great deal of embodied integration."

### 5. How the developers see it

1) Lin Feng, executive director and CEO of CIFI Group: "**Under China's current land-transfer and auction system, the vast majority of buildings have become a kind of commodity. To treat a building as a work and an act of creation, you can only do that with a small fraction of buildings.**"

2) Chen Kai, founder of Shanyue Capital: "I think future commercial complexes will return to the suburbs, to major transportation hubs — these complexes can be big enough to satisfy a family's eating, drinking and entertainment once they arrive, including play areas for children. So for commercial complexes in the city center, I think a certain quantity will remain — we say community commerce will be very developed — but for something like a 200,000-square-meter commercial complex, I don't think there is much room to survive."

3) **Gao Yixuan, president of Jingtou Development Co., Ltd.: "Many department stores have already started bringing in the co-working concept.** The 5th and 6th floors have relatively low sales per square meter — rent might be 1 yuan. Now that they have cleared out two floors and turned them into co-working space, the rent paid is higher than what the businesses on the 6th and 7th floors pay. With office workers inside, there is consumption to be had in the building — at the very least they will eat and drink there — injecting a certain base flow of people into part of the commercial space. Combined with the outside, this represents the future direction. **There are also commercial properties built directly on top of rail transit, closely linked to it,** which in the future will both have a certain service radius and basically be rooted in the community, serving across boundaries.

"For people living in such communities, **70% choose a full Chinese meal, 28% choose hotpot, and only 2% choose other dining options.** So here we arrange the businesses very point-to-point, including experiential bookstores and experiential cinemas. These days when people go out for entertainment, movies come first — you definitely go see a movie. The core things that stop people from consuming are that the travel time is too long and parking."
