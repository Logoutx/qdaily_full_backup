Those businesses are beyond saving anyway.

LeEco no longer carries the Jia name.

After more than a month of trading halts, Leshi announced [in a filing yesterday afternoon](http://vip.stock.finance.sina.com.cn/corp/view/vCB_AllBulletinDetail.php?stockid=300104&id=3436207) that Jia Yueting had applied to resign as the company's general manager, while remaining chairman. Liang Jun, formerly president of LeShi Zhixin, will become general manager of Leshi.

[According to Leshi's corporate charter](http://www.cninfo.com.cn/finalpage/2011-09-02/59911535.PDF), the company operates under a general-manager responsibility system, with a single general manager accountable to the board of directors, running day-to-day operations and management, and organizing and implementing the board's resolutions.

Although Jia Yueting is still the largest shareholder, stepping down as general manager already means he has given up day-to-day control.

In a subsequent [letter to all employees](http://tech.qq.com/a/20170521/023095.htm), Jia Yueting announced that Leshi would have a full-time CEO for the first time, along with a broader executive reshuffle: Yang Lijie, Leshi's chief financial officer, would no longer hold any position, to be replaced by Zhang Wei, who had previously been in charge of finance for LeEco Holdings' China operations.

In the letter, Jia Yueting said the appointments of Liang Jun and Zhang Wei would become "an important milestone for Leshi since its 2010 IPO."

The news that Liang Jun would succeed Jia Yueting had already been revealed at the beginning of this month by Sun Hongbin, chairman of Sunac China, [at Sunac's earnings briefing](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/40502.html). Sunac bought into LeEco in January to put out the fire.

At the early-May earnings briefing, Sun Hongbin said of LeSports: "The Chinese Super League cost 1.35 billion yuan last year, and they collected a total of 50 million — a loss of 1.3 billion. That's insane." Last week came reports that LeSports was cutting 70% of staff and that three senior executives had left.

Liang Jun, the man now replacing Jia Yueting, previously ran the LeEco business Sun Hongbin values most: LeEco TV.

LeEco is being remade step by step according to Sun Hongbin's vision.

### 16.8 Billion Yuan Couldn't Save Leshi's Stock

LeEco's crisis began last November. After the company was exposed for defaulting on payments in its phone business, its market value evaporated by 10.8 billion yuan over 37 days, through December 9. See [this report QDaily published at the end of last year](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/35401.html).

LeEco then halted trading for forty days to find a way out, and Sunac arrived with a 15-billion-yuan investment. Together with two other, smaller investors, LeEco raised 16.8 billion yuan.

The money went to Jia Yueting and to the three LeEco businesses considered relatively promising: Leshi, LeShi Zhixin, and Le Vision Pictures.

With the cash in hand, LeEco soon resumed trading, but after a few brief days of improvement, the stock kept falling.

In April, LeEco announced that part of the financing had arrived, and the stock improved slightly. Then negative news kept pouring in: the founder of LeEco's car business departed; the founder of Yidao Yongche publicly accused LeEco of diverting 1.3 billion yuan in loans to build cars; an affiliate fell behind on loan payments... These events showed that the incoming money had not solved the underlying operational problems. Then came [a poorly received annual report](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/39996.html), and LeEco's stock plunged again.

After the crash, LeEco went into another prolonged trading halt beginning April 14.

![LeEco's stock price (2017.1.15–present)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705220500362uROilwGKUWS09rn.jpg-w600)

From receiving the investment in January to the most recent halt, Leshi's stock fell 13% and its market value shrank by 8.6 billion yuan. Nearly one-third of the trading days in that period were spent halted to stop the slide.

In 2015, when Leshi's stock was at its high of 100 yuan, Jia Yueting cashed out more than 6 billion yuan and lent it to Leshi interest-free, with a term of no less than five years. By the end of the first half of 2016, the loans from Jia Yueting and his sister Jia Yuefang to Leshi totaled [3.9 billion yuan](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/39996.html).

By the time the 2016 annual report came out, the money Jia Yueting had lent Leshi was down to just 2.6 million yuan. It was supposed to be a five-year loan; in reality it lasted one year. According to LeEco's announcements, even if Jia Yueting's and Jia Yuefang's money was no longer lent to the company, it would be used to buy more shares. But Leshi never disclosed a follow-up share-purchase announcement, and the stock kept falling. 6 billion yuan is no small sum — more than a third of Sunac's investment in LeEco. Jia Yueting's silent share-buying is hardly a signal to the outside world that he is confident in the company's stock.

### Unlike a Normal Company, LeEco Cannot Afford a Falling Stock

LeEco's businesses fall into three blocks: the listed businesses, the unlisted businesses, and cars.

The listed businesses refer to Leshi, which went public on the A-share market in 2010, plus its TV and cloud services.

The unlisted businesses include film, sports, phones, Yidao's ride-hailing service (70% owned), finance, and a string of newer ventures.

Jia Yueting is Leshi's largest shareholder, and during 2014–2015, when the stock performed well, he [pledged shares of the listed Leshi to third-party financial institutions 15 separate times](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/35401.html) in exchange for cash to invest in new businesses.

The financial institutions do this to earn interest. But to avoid losses, they agree on a stock price with Jia Yueting at the time of the loan.

Once Leshi's stock falls below that price, the institutions have the right to sell the shares and recover their money.

According to the first-quarter financial report, Jia Yueting, his brother Jia Yuemin, and partner Liu Hong together hold 30.94% of Leshi's shares.

Jia Yueting holds the most: 512 million shares, 97% of them pledged. Jia Yuemin holds 43.94 million shares, 98.4% pledged. Liu Hong holds 61.23 million shares, 48.5% pledged. In addition, LeEco Holdings, the group company, has over ten million more shares, all pledged.

If the slide can't be stopped, it means as much as 30% of Leshi could end up bought by others, and LeEco's executives would lose actual control of the company.

Something similar has happened on the A-share market. Last February, [the chairman of Co-top Electronics](http://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_1428413) lost control of his company for exactly this reason.

If it came to that, Sunac would also have the chance to put in more money and buy up the shares Jia Yueting pledged, thereby gaining actual control of the company. But that itself carries risks — and there could be other interested bidders.

### Sunac Prepared Early to Take Over

At [the press conference](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/36839.html) announcing Sunac's investment at the start of the year, the star of the show was not Jia Yueting.

At the two-hour event, Jia Yueting spoke for only 15 minutes. He rarely mentioned LeEco's signature "ecological chemical reaction," saying "synergy" instead. Sun Hongbin's speech ran almost three times as long as Jia's (followed by a Q&A).

Jia Yueting said the money could solve LeEco's current funding problems once and for all. From that moment on, his control over LeEco was already much diminished.

Sunac China placed restrictions on how Jia Yueting and LeEco Holdings obtained and used the money, and set out specific conditions written into the investment contract, such as:

- Leshi's board of directors would have five members, with Sunac China nominating two — i.e., 40% of the voting power. Jia Yueting promised to vote in favor of this proposal;
- Leshi's board would establish subordinate investment-decision and management committees, with Sunac nominating one member to each. Jia Yueting promised to vote in favor of this proposal;
- Sunac would nominate a finance manager to join Leshi;

- LeShi Zhixin's board of directors would have three members, with Sunac China nominating one;
- Sunac China would nominate a finance manager to join LeShi Zhixin;
- A CEO position would be added to improve corporate governance;

- Sunac would appoint one supervisor each to the core manufacturing and sales companies of LeEco's phone business — even though Sunac did not invest in phones. This can be read as Sunac China's worry that Jia Yueting would divert funds from the listed company and its subsidiaries to other LeEco ecosystem businesses.

Sunac also wanted Leshi, LeShi Zhixin, and Le Vision Pictures to become its associates. Sunac's stake in LeEco's unlisted hardware arm LeShi Zhixin is already more than one-third, while proposals require two-thirds of shareholders to pass. That means Sunac can block proposals at shareholder meetings, and LeEco matters are no longer Jia Yueting's call alone.

Next, these control conditions were fulfilled one by one.

In April, Liu Shuqing, a senior general manager at Sunac China's risk-control center, became a non-executive director of Leshi. Sun Hongbin stressed that Liu Shuqing was not a figurehead but would "actually manage Leshi's affairs."

Sunac also followed through on its earlier investment announcement by sending three finance managers to Leshi and its affiliates. A CFO has now parachuted into LeShi Zhixin, finance managers have been posted to Le Vision Pictures and Leshi, and a Sunac China supervisor sits in the phone business.

"Our finance people are all in place, and we have veto power on the listed company's board. The first step is definitely to fix the listed company's governance structure — this structure still has enormous room for improvement," Sun Hongbin said at Sunac's May earnings briefing.

Sun Hongbin also stressed: "LeEco must have a CEO. It didn't before; now someone has to run it, and Liang Jun is running it."

Of course, judging by the series of changes, the person actually running things is still Sun Hongbin.

"As for the car business, I believe Old Jia will devote a lot of energy to cars. And there's still me on the listed-company side — it will keep getting better," Sun Hongbin said at the briefing.

Liang Jun took charge of LeEco's TV business in 2012. TV is also the relatively healthiest of LeEco's many businesses today; at a launch event late last year, Liang announced [a plan to turn profitable this year and post substantial profits in the following two years](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/35836.html). Among LeEco's businesses, that is rare.

### What Is the Way Out for Sun Hongbin's LeEco?

Whether they believed in Jia Yueting's "ecological chemical reaction," or believed Jia Yueting could keep playing hot potato and cash out before one giant bubble burst, the people who bought the stock were, more than anything, buying Jia Yueting.

That is probably why Jia Yueting did not step down as general manager when Sunac's investment was announced, but only now — when it is clear that even 15 billion yuan cannot rescue the stock — handing over control of LeEco in a manner that looks relatively dignified.

At yesterday afternoon's media briefing, Jia Yueting and Sunac's representative Liu Shuqing repeatedly stressed there was no fight over control. Jia Yueting also said the "official decision period" for the CEO choice at the listed company began last August or September, though LeEco's restructuring did indeed pick up pace after Sunac came aboard.

As [discussed in last year's report](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/35401.html), if LeEco could not raise enough investment to solve its funding problems, it would have no choice but to give up businesses.

Now 16.8 billion yuan has failed to solve the problems, and Sun Hongbin's answer, as he said from the start, is to sell businesses.

At the early-month Sunac earnings briefing, [Sun Hongbin said](https://wallstreetcn.com/articles/3007601): "I've been telling Old Jia: in the future it's the listed company and cars. Cars — do whatever you want with them. Everything else, sell what should be sold." As for Yidao and sports, it was likewise "partner up quickly, sell off quickly."

![[The complex "LeEco ecosystem" on LeEco's official website](http://www.leeco.com/cn/ecology/index.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201705220634537lyW5H8FMdNiVSz9.jpg-w600)

In yesterday's all-hands letter and media briefing, Jia Yueting said that going forward LeEco would be split into only two blocks — the listed-company system and cars — and that the unlisted businesses would either be merged into the listed company or dealt with through "other integration."

The purpose of folding businesses into the listed company is to prop up the stock. That also means only businesses with prospects are worth merging in. Everything else that burns cash will be disposed of.

In March, LeEco terminated its cooperation with Russia's Far East Development Fund, and LeLive, the e-commerce platform for selling Russian food to China, was shelved as a result.

In April, LeEco called off its $2 billion acquisition of American TV maker Vizio, citing foreign-exchange controls.

Then the overseas expansion plans ground to a halt as well. Because 2016 sales in the US fell short of expectations, LeEco would cut [more than a third](http://tech.sina.com.cn/it/2017-04-11/doc-ifyeceza2028868.shtml) of its American workforce. LeEco's overseas electric-car plant also announced it would not build a new factory.

Then [Yidao's entire founding team](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/39896.html) departed, saying LeEco's diversion of investment funds had caused the cash problems. Judging by drivers queuing up every day to grab their limited daily withdrawal amount, LeEco is no longer pumping money into the ride-hailing business it controls. Whenever Sun Hongbin mentions Yidao in public, it's: "We've been pushing too — partner up quickly, sell off quickly."

LeSports, the business Sun Hongbin called "insane," is changing even more drastically: executives are leaving and live sports broadcasts are being cut off one by one. At its Series A round, LeSports was once valued at 20.5 billion yuan. By this Chinese New Year, its valuation had been halved. Now even Beijing Shougang, which had intended to invest, is unwilling to invest at the halved valuation.

LeEco's phone business looks like the next to be abandoned. Based on LeEco subsidizing each phone by 200 yuan last year, this single business burned at least 3.4 billion yuan in subsidies. At yesterday's meeting, Jia Yueting admitted the phone business had entered a "quasi-shock" state.

The remaining large unlisted businesses are film and finance.

For Le Vision Pictures, net profit attributable to shareholders of the parent company was -891 million yuan and -102 million yuan in 2014 and 2015 respectively. LeEco no longer discusses the film business's 2016 results; only the investment report Sunac China published in January mentioned that Le Vision Pictures failed to meet its target of "net profit excluding non-recurring items of 520 million yuan."

Finance is not an easy business either. Even JD.com, whose core business is profitable, spun off its finance unit separately to make its financial reports look better. Meituan-Dianping, which recently entered finance, connects directly with users and merchants — resources LeEco lacks. LeEco also holds few financial licenses, only insurance and micro-lending ones.

Cars burn money even faster and pay back even later.

None of LeEco's unlisted businesses are healthy, and most are in the process of being cut down.

The only businesses in the company that look relatively normal are the listed video site and TV business.

Whether buying content rights or producing original content, a video site operates in a market where everyone is burning cash. Baidu, Tencent, and Alibaba — the three giants — are propping up China's top three video sites at any cost.

Leshi's profits used to come from hardware subsidies: buy a Leshi membership and get a phone, a TV, Yidao credit, and so on. The losses of the unlisted businesses were converted into Leshi's profits.

With those businesses declining, LeSports losing content rights, and users watching LeEco's negative news day after day, can it still sell memberships the way it used to?

As for the TV business: TCL's total market value is just 42.8 billion yuan, and LeEco TV is far smaller in scale.

What Sun Hongbin may also have his eye on is LeEco's land. [Sun Hongbin has said](http://www.sohu.com/a/130927141_132560) that LeEco previously obtained government approval for 10,000 mu of land in the Moganshan area, and that apart from industrial and other projects, the remaining land still has substantial development value.

Starting from a video site, Jia Yueting rode storytelling and stock pledges into film and TV production, television, sports, phones, cloud computing, alcohol, ride-hailing, car-making, and other markets, making LeEco at one point a company with a market value of 100 billion yuan.

Today, those businesses are toppling one after another like dominoes.

There is little hope left in LeEco's businesses. As for its stock, it remains to be seen how many [inscrutable A-share investors](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/39396.html) will believe they are sharper than everyone else and can ride what Jia Yueting yesterday called the "bottoming-out rebound" to make a quick buck.
