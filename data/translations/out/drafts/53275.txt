This is part one: what kind of competitiveness a city should display; the official misconceptions about cities; and why, looking at the origins of the ancient Chinese city, Max Weber said China had no cities.

**“Sometimes the key is not to change the world, but to explain it.”**

**At a critical moment like this one — a moment when we need to decide how to explain the world and how to live with the future — we need to hear wise, independent voices.**

**We invited more than 20 professionals from fields including economics, law, sociology, and communication studies to answer our questions.**

**The questions are numerous and complex, but they bear on the future lives of the young generation. Our interviewees hope that the world left to young people of the future runs well and advances civilization, and that amid a complex, bewildering world, people hold on to independence and reason.**

Are there slums in China's cities?

City officials care a great deal about this question — it bears on their performance records and on stability. City residents are equally sensitive to it: dirt, disorder, whether it is safe, the city's image, property values... many of their own interests ride on it.

On a larger canvas: competition between city and city; within cities, the contest between the "new XX" and the native population over resources and services; the trade-offs among the services migrant workers provide, the resources they occupy, and the city's image and positioning — between rich and poor, between those with hukou and those without — there are countless disputes and contradictions. And behind every policy that sparks these disputes, or that is shaped by them, lies a different understanding of what a city is.

How to understand our cities, how to understand urban governance — everyone's view is not necessarily the same. And those differences easily begin with the different readings of the slum question mentioned above.

Tang Shiping believes that in China's urbanization, precisely because the government is powerful and regulation can be proactive, China may have done better than Brazil or India — what he means is that our cities may not have large-scale slums. Zhang Qianfan agrees with him, but on the other hand he points out: "Compare China with the West, especially the United States, and we find that America has slums — above all in the downtown cores of big cities, which tend to be where the poor concentrate. By comparison, that phenomenon is uncommon in China. What we commonly see is the enormous gap between city and city, and between city and countryside — and that gap will only keep widening."

Dai Jinhua says that the economic cycles of big cities rise and fall, so the appearance of urban slums is normal.

The consensus was punctured by Zhang Yan, who comes from New York — a city of both skyscrapers and slums. He said: "You can tear down the countryside and put up a lot of towers, but what are the towers for? That's the question. Sometimes they build development zones, but no jobs turn up. So the real work is still in the big coastal cities, where the economy is (doing well). I understand the government's aim, but (controlling the population) is very hard to achieve. They often have this image of the floating population: when it rains, water flows in every direction, and then someone says we should spread out in all directions, not all run to Beijing. That's fine — but how do you do it? They all live in Baoding, but are there jobs there?"

The opportunity for people to choose freely comes first. The right to pursue happiness and a good life comes first.

Xiao Han wrote in an article for the media: "When it comes to basic human rights and people's livelihoods, however complex a problem may be, at bottom it is simple: treat people as people."

This matches what Dai Jinhua said in her interview. She said her first reaction, no matter what, is to stand on the side of the weak. "Between the strong and the weak, between the powerful and the powerless, I don't think I could make any other choice. There is no other choice."

The discussion of urban topics that begins with slums will be published in two parts.

Part one covers what a city should be in the first place; the official misconceptions about cities; and why, looking at the origins of the ancient Chinese city, Max Weber said China had no cities.

### The Value of the City

**1) A city is itself composed of people from every level of society living here collectively; its operating mechanisms form naturally**

**Ma Yong, Research Fellow, Institute of Modern History, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences**

I think (what happened) was a series of absurd things. As a scholar, (this) actually has nothing to do with my life — they can't clear me out, we have no billboards of our own for them to tear down — but I think it's absurd. It's entirely making trouble for yourself; it's "smearing" the system. Think about it: those billboards formed over history, and they're all legal billboards. You say tear them down and they're torn down — aren't you saying this system is rotten? That the system does whatever it wants? That's "smearing" the system.

Moreover, the "XX population" — you want to clear them out. This shows a fundamental ignorance of the basic laws by which a modern city operates and takes shape. A city is a product of industrialization, a product of modern society. A city is itself composed of people from every level of society living here collectively, and its operating mechanisms form naturally. For everyone to become someone living in an apartment block, someone paying several thousand yuan a month in rent — that's impossible.

In fact, every (country's) big city has shantytowns like this; old Shanghai had its shantytowns. I'm in Beijing; the hourly help my family uses can only rent houses that are about to be demolished. Wherever a demolition is happening, that's where they rent. No water, no electricity — as long as they have a roof. At first we didn't understand either; after talking with them a lot, we came to understand: if it's a decent house, what they earn simply can't cover the rent, or they feel that paying that much is a losing deal.

So how exactly should this situation be handled? I think Beijing's best approach would be to build some of this kind of low-end (housing) inside the Third Ring, inside the Second Ring, for bottom-rung migrant workers, renting to them at low rates. On one hand it would give them a genuinely dignified life — no more (squeezing onto the subway for hours on end). A few times, heading to work in the morning, I happened to pass and watched Line 1 coming in from Tongzhou: I squeeze you, you squeeze me. However much money a person earns, even dignity is gone. If this group could truly live near where they work, that would solve this problem too. And the whole order of the city would be different. On this point, everyone has probably seen it. Sociologists who study this professionally must see it more clearly than I do. Because this isn't a professional question — I'm (just) offering a few personal reflections.

**2) A high-density, diverse city brings more opportunities and desire to learn — that is the source of innovation**

**Zhang Bin, Research Fellow, Institute of World Economics and Politics, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences**

First, don't set urban renovation against de-urbanization. Beijing really does need renovation. Beijing has a large number of urban villages. Much of Beijing's infrastructure — the hardware is decent, but the software services are poor. Let me start with the urban villages: land use in Beijing is extraordinarily wasteful. Many places with excellent locations have very low density. Many urban villages sit there for years with terrible efficiency of resource use. That's not a good thing, so they should be redeveloped. Urban renovation and improving infrastructure are still very necessary for Beijing.

But you have to know why you're doing urban renovation. You do these things to make life more convenient and more comfortable for the people living in Beijing, to make them feel the city is better. All the people living in Beijing — not just Beijingers, but also those who came from elsewhere — it should be open to everyone. Renovating the city is about making the city more attractive, more beautiful, so that if people want to come, they live better here. Make it an attractive place.

I strongly endorse urban renovation, but the way it's done should be something people can readily accept. Two examples: right outside my door they're also redeveloping an urban village — Beijing's biggest, five to six thousand households. From my own observation, the whole process was pretty good. They told everyone very early on that renovation was coming, and there were bonuses. The earlier you leave, the more money you're awarded; the later you leave, the less you get. The rule was set and never changed — they wouldn't compromise just because you held out to the very end. The compensation was also basically satisfactory — the signing rate reached over 90 percent, 98, 99 percent. Watching the whole process: the houses were all demolished, the people all moved out, and the result was pretty good.

But look at the urban village redevelopment in the suburbs. The whole process took months — half a year — and the preparation probably started even earlier. Then suddenly, all at once, such a huge commotion. I think the methods and the approach still have problems. But that doesn't mean that because a place is dirty, chaotic, and poor, with inefficient resource use, it shouldn't be redeveloped — it should. The point is that after redevelopment, people should be able to live better there. You have to be clear about that starting point.

Is there an optimal city size? Left to spontaneous market forces, a size will emerge of its own. A city first of all has a centripetal force, an attraction. Why do cities have economies of scale? Why is everyone willing to come to big cities? Why does every great country in the world have great cities? Because a city is first of all a high-density place. Why does density matter? Because costs come down, and you can easily learn things from others. Second, a city is a diverse place. People come from everywhere, of every color. Why does diversity matter? Because I'm different from him, you're different from him, we're all different — you get to see many different things. And those differences actually create opportunities for mutual learning and create more desire to learn. I want to learn; I see something someone else has is good, and I want to do that well too.

Why do I keep stressing this learning function? Because this sort of thing is actually the source of new knowledge and of innovation; change begins right here. Why are cities important? Through this diversity and density, you become a fountainhead of knowledge. And the spillover effect of knowledge is the most primal driving force for all social progress.

But density comes at a cost; too much density becomes congestion. Once a city is excessively congested, it brings higher costs again. The market itself has a force that keeps a city's population hovering around some level. There's another question here: is city size capped? Not necessarily. If my urban planning and design, my urban services, are very good — public management services very good — then I can accommodate more people. Even if density is very high, if I manage it very well, I can house more people. Conversely, if my management is a mess, my services are a mess, I can't keep up, I can't do it — then with too many people, congestion, household garbage, and pollution everywhere, you can't accommodate that many people.

Our problem now is: why limit population? True, a big population brings a lot of congestion and a lot of pollution. But is all that congestion entirely caused by population? You can't look at it that way. By density, Manhattan is ten times denser than Beijing; many cities in the world are far denser than Beijing, yet they don't have this much congestion. So where does the problem lie? Our public management and services fall short — they can't reach that level — so congestion, pollution, and every kind of urban disease stand out, and the answer becomes: don't come, I can't manage it.

You can see that when the government says it doesn't want people to come, it is in effect admitting its own incompetence in public management and services. There's nothing to be done, so you just don't come. If real progress could be made in public management and services, urban construction, and infrastructure, I believe cities could carry far more people.

**3) Urbanization is a result of industrialization**

**Wei Sen, Professor of Economics, Fudan University**

On urbanization I have a different view. If you look at the broad historical course of human society, you'll find that urbanization is a result of industrialization. Before industrialization there was no urbanization. But once industrialization arrived, everyone came to work in the factories, and urbanization followed. At that time I judged that, after more than 20 years of economic growth, China's economy had already completed the process of industrialization. What we're doing now is cutting overcapacity, destocking, and deleveraging — in effect, de-industrialization. But once you de-industrialize, a large share of people are pushed into services. More restaurants, more foot-washing parlors — but the whole move into the city slows down. So China's urbanization will decrease, not accelerate. That's my basic judgment.

Of course, there's another view of urbanization. Some say China's real urbanization rate is only about 61 percent, that China's urbanization has to rise to 80 percent, that there's still a great deal of room. But if you get to the city and there's no work, what am I doing there? In that situation, I feel urbanization hasn't arrived, and real estate will run into trouble. They build houses that won't sell; real estate goes through an adjustment; and China's economy will keep heading down.

Another point: Cai Jiming and many others believed that China's real urban population — those holding hukou, social insurance, and medical insurance — was only 36 percent. So many workers: they earn money here and have nothing. Much of the city's service work — selling vegetables, sweeping the streets — is done by people without urban hukou. After Beijing cleared out the "XX population," this cohort went back. These people aren't urbanized; they came to the city to earn money and work.

So Zhou Qiren makes the point: if you want to fight poverty, the key is to reach the people who have come to work in the cities. Now that you've chased all the "XX population" away, they go home and switch to farming a few mu of land — and they'll be poor forever. In the city, how much more do they earn than at home! Migrant workers returning home take tens of thousands of yuan back with them. That is the main reason Chinese farmers' incomes have risen. The reason China's Gini coefficient and the urban-rural gap haven't grown even bigger is, to a large extent, the more than 200 million farmers who work in the cities. Compared with urban workers they are still low-paid — lower than other professionals — but still far higher than what they would earn farming at home. Now this cohort is being cleared out and sent back, and China's wealth gap will widen too. Nobody does it this way. But we say it's for the sake of appearances, for city aesthetics or whatever reason. I don't approve.

A city is a spontaneous process. What we're doing now goes against that process; we have to let it grow naturally. Everyone does their own economic calculation: maybe I'm fairly well off in the village, but in the city I'm a worker. Judging by personal income alone, working in the city isn't great, but it's still better than farming a few mu in the countryside, and it earns far more than working in a county town. That's the main source of rising income for Chinese farmers. Now we say we want to develop the countryside in a big way — it's the same as saying: don't hang around here, go home and work hard, build the new countryside, and you'll get rich.

Then there's medical insurance, social insurance, children in school — none of those problems are solved. All the low-end work in the city is done by migrant workers. They get nothing, and now you send them back. Beijing and Shanghai used to be so convenient — at least around Renmin University and Peking University, you used to be able to eat at street stalls everywhere; now there are few.

### The Official Misconceptions About Cities

**4) The city in many people's minds is a blank city, not a city full of people**

**He Weifang, Professor of Law, Peking University**

Of course, in China there's a very complicated problem: urban land is not privately owned. So sometimes you find that everything can be decided on a whim. You'll find that in the minds of many planners, the city is not a city full of people; it's a blank sheet. They can take out a ruler and draw a road right here. And this is a giant city plaza. So this aggravates the government's arbitrariness.

Then, when we formulate this kind of regulation, have we fully considered the principle of proportionality that administrative law constantly stresses? When you take such measures, what gains do you obtain? What are the gains for the government? For the citizens of the city, what are their gains? For the relevant enterprises or institutions, what are their gains? I think making this kind of most basic justification is especially necessary. Because without justification, government simply becomes a matter of — whoever holds more power can arbitrarily reshape the face of the city. That is absolutely arbitrary power, unconstrained power, and it cannot be allowed.

Another point: before a relevant regulation is issued, is some kind of hearing needed? For instance, whether the people's congress deems it necessary in a situation like this? Or whether it violates a superior law — say, the Property Law? Or other norms guaranteeing citizens' freedom of movement, norms protecting personal freedom from infringement — is there a violation of those norms? I think basic hearings are needed. Even fierce debate, then broadcast it on live TV and webcast it, so that all citizens can see: why is such a rule being issued? Where does its reasonableness lie? What objections have people raised? This is the most basic requirement for democratized, open decision-making. And when Beijing issued such regulations, we never heard that they had solicited citizens' opinions, never heard that they had answered the questions or doubts raised by Beijing's people's congress deputies. A rule was issued in a rush and executed just like that. And then many people were driven away. Driving away outsiders doesn't just harm the rights and interests of the migrant population; it harms Beijing citizens' own interests. Those who rent houses to the relevant businesses, the tertiary industries that bring great convenience to our lives, all kinds of services, the delivery couriers. Now our prices have all gone up. So I think that issuing such a rule without any justification — that is not rule of law. Whatever kind of norm it enacts, it looks like an act of legislation, but in fact it is entirely an expression of the will of officials.

**5) Urbanization in the true sense must mean turning rural people into urban residents**

**Guo Yuhua, Professor of Sociology, Tsinghua University**

Population, the skyline, environmental protection — these three things from the end of 2017 are, in my view, basically one problem. Why one problem? They all revolve around the city and around the government's urban governance, so I can see them as three events, three different facets — but in essence they are the same. The thinking is basically one line of thinking; the form is also the same form. In fact they're all questions of urban governance — a kind of unconventional governance, campaign-style governance. Behind it lies a still more fundamental question: whether it's national governance or urban governance, on what should it be grounded? The two are really connected.

The first problem: throughout China's urbanization, this is a paradox. What is urbanization? Urbanization in what sense? The city grows bigger and bigger, more and more houses — (that's only the surface). Urbanization in the true sense must be the transformation of rural people into urban residents — not your city built so large while farmers remain farmers. The hukou system, the distinction between local and outside populations, the distinction between urban and rural hukou — all of it is the old system.

Under these conditions, what do you call urbanization? In my view, urbanization should be the urbanization of people. Urban construction, urban development, or urban expansion — all of it should take the person as the root. So I think Chinese urbanization itself contains a great paradox. For these people, it is in fact an urbanization with no people in its eyes. When urban construction needs them, they say: come work in the city. They are manpower, labor. In fact, China's industrialization was the same.

They were never seen as real people, or as people with the same, equal citizenship as all nationals — but as pairs of hands. When you need labor, you summon these hands — services, industry, handicrafts, all kinds of services. Then when a fire breaks out in the city — seemingly related to these people's relatively poor housing conditions, or inadequate fire-safety measures — you drive them out.

I think this is really a question of basic thinking. You use an administrative order, a one-size-fits-all approach. The tearing down of "illegal" signboards is the same. What makes it illegal? When it was installed it was legal — surely it wasn't put up carelessly; there was an approval process, various safety measures. What law are you relying on to tear it down now? You say it's for beauty and tidiness. The government gets to define what is beautiful? What is beauty? Is it defined like that? How is it defined? By what right do you define what beauty is?

Then there's the economic dimension. Installing that thing cost money. Your demolition also costs money. And tearing it down creates a big pile of garbage. When it went up, there wasn't just the material cost — much of it, celebrity calligraphy for instance, had to be paid for. It was basically like advertising, with commercial and economic (interests) inside. So the government uses this kind of coercive means to directly interfere in people's production, business, and even daily life. Many ordinary people can't even find places anymore; it causes real inconvenience. Yet another case of the one-size-fits-all mentality.

Coal-to-gas is the same. In the north, especially in rural areas, winter heating has long meant burning coal. (The government) says we're switching to gas — there should have been a whole series of arrangements. It's a systems-engineering job. Is the hardware in place? Is the gas sufficient? Can it be delivered in time? In the promised quantities? When none of this has been properly arranged, you go one-size-fits-all, and even put up slogans like "whoever burns coal gets arrested" and "whoever sells coal gets arrested." That is completely unreasonable; many people were left unable to heat their homes through the winter.

In fact, looking at the practice, it's still a campaign-style approach. Like the campaigns of old: an order comes down from above, and below it is pushed down level by level. To use a campaign method for what is actually urban governance, an economic activity, a matter of people's livelihoods. Behind campaign-style governance there is a fixed ideology, a line of thinking — and this thinking is not at all the kind of thinking that reform and opening up should have.

**6) The government cannot judge for people where it is suitable to live; that is an individual judgment**

**Guo Yuhua, Professor of Sociology, Tsinghua University**

What sustains a relatively normal urban ecology in a city? Behind this lies a fundamental (question). Why did I just say this is the fundamental difference between a market-economy mindset and a planned-economy mindset? Because the government has always felt it can plan everything, arrange everything.

But in fact, all over the world there are many big and small cities, including super-large ones. Those cities formed relatively naturally. People feel they can make a living in a place, live a fairly normal life, and so they live there. If they feel a place isn't suitable for survival — bad, not right — they may go somewhere else. But the key is there must be a guarantee of free movement. Because ever since 1949, China has restricted the movement of population. You were fixed in the countryside, or fixed in some place at some administrative level of city. It was all arranged by a set of plans; you were not allowed to move freely.

It's still the planned-economy mindset, so people don't have that choice. I think there's always this alarmism: once you open up, the "blind drifters" will all come. How can you presume in advance that farmers are "blind drifters"? If a farmer can't survive in the city, he simply won't stay in the city. Farmers aren't stupid. When he goes somewhere to make a living, there's a basic foundation and logic to earning a livelihood — if he can't survive somewhere, he leaves. In the past few years everyone talked about "fleeing Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou" and then "fleeing back." Why do they flee back and forth? Because they judge where suits their survival. Of course, this is connected to the whole national system and social ecology.

This isn't something human beings can (decide). However brilliant the brain, however wise and capable the government — can it decide for tens of millions upon millions of people what kind of life they want? How they want to make what they consider a happy, dignified life — how can it decide that? The people should have the right to choose. Of course, a city definitely needs guarantees and arrangements of every kind — public security, firefighting, sanitation, urban facilities. Say a lot of criminals come. Crime? There is law. Use the law to deal with crime. You can't presume in advance that people are "blind drifters," a "mob" — that if they come, the city falls into chaos.

I think the people of any country, any society, are basically oriented toward survival. People want to survive, and to pursue happiness, a dignified life, a better life. That is the most legitimate human right there is. Ordinary people only make trouble when they truly cannot survive. Instability, chaos — those have their conditions. It's not that if you don't let people come, there's no chaos. If you don't let them come and they can't live well wherever they are, there will still be trouble. So I think this is really connected to that fundamental question from just now.

Power may not intervene in anything without any constraint. At the same time, power may not shirk all responsibility either. Now look at this power: much of the time it is power level by level, answerable only upward, not downward. Why answerable upward? Because of their official hats, their performance records — so the performance-record logic is an absurd logic. All it takes is one word from above. Whether the thing is right or wrong, whether it's workable or realistic — he will push it through hard.

If you complete or over-fulfill my task, I will reward you. If you fail to complete it, I don't care about the specific reason — I will punish you. I call this the "the King of Chu loved slender waists" logic: what those above favor, those below are sure to carry to excess. Because if you overdo it, you won't be punished; if you underdo it, you will be. Level by level, none answer downward, all answer upward. Call it bureaucracy or a system of power — that's its characteristic. So this too is a very fundamental problem. No one takes ordinary people seriously; no one is truly people-centered.

**7) Treating the countryside as a reversible reservoir for urbanization — this high-level instrumental thinking produces crude governance**

**Ren Jiantao, Professor of Political Science, Tsinghua University**

Throughout the process of urbanization, our cities kept absorbing the rural population into the city. But now that urbanization has reached its deepening stage, the city begins to weed out its surplus population — the city has gone from being open to the countryside to being closed to it. So this is actually not just a matter of clearing out "non-capital functions"; more critically it touches the great social event of "rural urbanization" — as a public event affecting millions upon millions of Chinese, the question of how it evolves. Of course, there are many important events in between: the development of education, economic transformation, and many matters that began as local incidents but drew attention at the national level.

Because public events are defined by stakeholders and potential stakeholders, in this sense there are big public events and small public events. Within a certain scope of society, a small public event may not draw people's attention, but sometimes a small public event also helps people's vital interests. Take the same big cities: in the process of cleaning up urban order — Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen — all are re-clearing the practice of "punching holes through walls to open doors." This issue, alongside the clearing of the "low-end population," is another important event that drew public attention to urban development. Because starting in the 1980s with the market economy, in the cities — ordinary buildings in particular — "punching holes through walls to open doors" as shops to activate private business was once one of the most important practices of China's market-economy development. But by today it turns out this is a low-end market economy: it can only punch holes and open doors, run small businesses; sizable commerce cannot be done. And now the country needs innovation, which this cannot satisfy. So now we plug the holes, block the doors, seal the walls. "Punching holes through walls" has become an effort to restore buildings' original facades. This, in fact, also affected quite a few people's livelihoods.

So in 2017 there is an overlap between major political public events and social public events affecting people's livelihoods. In this sense, 2017 may be a year we must remember. But whether it will be remembered as a good year or one of sad memories — history may still need to wash it gradually to reveal its true face.

For China's urbanization, we must first affirm that the overall process is irreversible. With China's economic development, especially the shift from an agricultural-commercial economy to an industrial-commercial economy, the city will become the knot of this economic system. But the process of urbanization has a very great difficulty — one that every country's urbanization process struggles with.

First, the chaos and disorder of the initial stage of urbanization. Because we're opening the cities, and surplus rural labor must enter them. But no preparation has been made in policy planning, resource supply, the separation of living space between old and new residents, or the allocation of urban resources. So urbanization becomes a chaotic process of everyone swarming in at once. Then, when urbanization reaches the truly critical stage, it constitutes a transformative process in the allocation of a nation's resources and institutions. So in a sense, the urbanization process is also a benchmark of a country's modernization process.

At the critical moment — when urbanization demands that resource and institutional allocation be corrected and matched to urbanization's disorderly course — that is when urbanization's real difficulty appears. And our country's urbanization, by stage, is in the second stage, moving from chaos and disorder toward order. At this point it faces two great challenges. First, the city's capacity is already saturated. How to solve the saturation problem? That becomes the puzzle leading to the second: policy supply must keep up. Because of long-term mediocre, lazy governance, we've ended up with a certain crude governance. Problems in the allocation of urban resources are solved simply and carelessly. Whether clearing urban resources or clearing urban population, "Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen" have all adopted relatively simple and crude methods. Only Beijing, as the capital, draws more attention; Shanghai did it relatively delicately and aroused no public outrage.

But the basic approach of Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen is not a policy and resource layout for urban development made in response to the deepening stage of urbanization and the nation's modernization plan. In a sense, this is closely related to our not having figured out how cities should develop — the position, role, and concrete function of the urbanization process within the national modernization process. And closely tied to that relationship is the institutional feedback: for a long time we have treated the countryside as nothing but a reversible reservoir for urbanization.

The reservoir idea was proposed in the mid-1990s by scholars of "sannong" research — a group of scholars with deep ideas about the countryside, peasants, and agriculture. But in fact it is nothing more than high-level instrumental thinking. High-level instrumental thinking is linked to a certain low-level instrumental thinking. So-called high-level instrumental thinking is: the city needs you, come; the city doesn't need you, go back. That is reservoir theory. But it never occurred to anyone that the peasants had no intention of returning to the countryside, and the countryside itself could no longer take back the peasants expelled by the land. They had already become new members of the city.

So how the city opens sources and throttles flow to absorb this group must involve corresponding policy planning. And at this point, policy planning may again produce low-level instrumental thinking. The so-called low-level instrumental thinking: because at the high level the countryside is the city's reservoir, policy fails to follow, leading policymakers to feel that peasants coming to the city is spontaneous, so peasants returning to the countryside is also spontaneous — making our policy response very slow. Even today, we haven't reckoned with the irreversibility of the urbanization process. So the reservoir claim is fundamentally misleading to policy. The result of misleading policy thinking is that we simply have no policy feedback for the irreversible flows of people, goods, and money in the urbanization process. Without policy feedback, we don't know how to lay out our cities.

Because in the agricultural-commercial era — before reform and opening up we were all in the agricultural-commercial era — we basically used our dual hukou system to completely separate city and countryside. The result of that separation: for a long time we in effect plundered the countryside, agriculture, and the peasants. The result of that: there was no long-term agricultural policy at all. Even when the city reached a stage where we couldn't make it a self-sufficient economic, social, and political space, our response was to send city people back to the countryside. That's the "send-down movement" everyone knows best: educated youth going up to the mountains and down to the countryside.

So, lacking stable, balanced, mutually matched urban policies, we turned the city into an upper-class living space and the countryside into a lower-class one. In a sense, the only policy response we could make was to fix this discriminatory policy in place through the dual hukou system. But we didn't expect that since reform and opening up, the real political effect of the hukou system has been completely lost. With the effect of the dual hukou system completely lost, people — though still constrained by hukou — move regardless of it, and large numbers of rural people and small-city people have swarmed into the big cities.

### Today's Ills, Seen From the Origins of the Ancient Chinese City

**8) "China's early cities were not the product of economic takeoff, but tools in the political sphere."**

**Xiao Han, Associate Professor of Law, China University of Political Science and Law**

"In comparing Chinese and Western cities, Weber placed the political status of the city resident foremost; he even denied that cities had ever existed in China, because of the dominative and power-expanding character of the ancient Chinese city; for he believed that only a city whose citizens held political rights could be called a city."

"All under heaven is the king's land; to the shores of the land, all are the king's servants." These sixteen characters from the *Book of Songs* sum up, to a great extent, China's dynastic history — they might be called an "outline of national history." They made China a bureaucratic empire unlike any other place in the world, whose basic feature was the arbitrary willfulness of bureaucratic power represented by the Son of Heaven or the emperor. For at least the nearly 3,000 years attested by reliable written records, the fate of ordinary Chinese was usually a storm-tossed state of servitude. In this long river of history, the fate of individuals before the empire — whether prominent or obscure, rich or poor, high-born or low — was insignificant. In the eyes of emperors and their bureaucrats, people were not individuals but clumps, like wild weeds. Forced migration is a highly typical phenomenon.

After Qin conquered the six states, Qin Shi Huang, in order to build a vast Legalist bureaucratic empire, needed to construct a new capital at Xianyang, and so he devised a new policy: "he moved 120,000 wealthy households from across the realm to Xianyang" (*Records of the Grand Historian*, Annals of Qin Shi Huang).

As the structure of the bureaucratic empire grew ever more refined, this tyranny requiring powerful institutions gradually became systematic, and thereafter emperors' casual large-scale relocation of the people became commonplace — not only to fill out the capital's population, but to ease control and strengthen economic power. "By the Western Han, 'strengthening the trunk and weakening the branches' was not only migration policy but became basic state policy. Thereafter, from Wei of the Three Kingdoms destroying Shu, to Western Jin destroying Wu, to the early Northern Song destroying the states of the Five Dynasties — whenever one regime was destroyed by another, a migration of varying scale followed: the fallen monarch (if still alive) and his family, officials, capital residents, even the main population of some important region would all be moved to the conquering state's capital or some designated place — in numbers from a few dozen or a few hundred households to tens of thousands, even over a hundred thousand households." Beyond this political forced migration there was penal forced migration: "Qin Shi Huang banished merchants and sons-in-law living with their wives' families to the frontier, in the hundreds of thousands. Major cases in successive dynasties often produced tens of thousands of exiles. Zhu Yuanzhang moved the wealthy households of Jiangnan to his native Fengyang and to the capital Yingtian (today's Nanjing), subjecting them to strict control... Migrations of (Han Chinese) among the surrounding populations were often organized by ethnicity: in the Western Han, the Yue people were moved to the region between the Yangtze and the Huai, and surrendered Xiongnu were moved to the northwestern borderlands; the Eastern Han moved the Southern Xiongnu inside the passes; the Eastern Han and the Wei-Jin moved the Qiang, the Di, and other peoples into the Guanzhong region; during the Sixteen Kingdoms, the Later Zhao, Former Qin, and other regimes moved the populations of various peoples near their capitals; the Tang moved surrendered peoples such as the Turks to the Chang'an region and moved households of Goguryeo into the Central Plains; the Liao moved the Balhae people inland; the early Ming moved surrendered Mongols inland. Because these were mostly concentrated relocations, the numbers were considerable — sometimes exceeding a million."

Only by understanding the Eastern despotism that China has carried with its bureaucratic politics since antiquity can one understand the distinctive problem of the Chinese empire. Although Karl Wittfogel's claim in *Oriental Despotism* — that Chinese despotism originated in hydraulic control — has not won academic acceptance, the strongly despotic character of China he displays in that book is beyond doubt, as the historical records of successive dynasties attest. It is scarcely contestable that China had already formed, 3,000 years ago, a bureaucratic network as the dominant organizational force of society. The bureaucratic network became the right and left arms of royal and imperial power and became society's decisive force, producing a bureaucratic empire in which politics, economics, culture, thought, art — nearly all spheres of national and social life — were dominated by the bureaucratic stratum, and this endured for millennia. Wang Yanan, the economist who first raised the problem of bureaucratic politics, offered a profound revelation of the consequences of ancient Chinese bureaucratic politics in his famous book *A Study of China's Bureaucratic Politics* (《中国官僚政治研究》): "Precisely because China's despotic bureaucratic politics mobilized or exploited various socio-cultural factors from the very start to expand its influence, the dominant, penetrating effect of bureaucratic politics gradually made itself into an inescapable net over thought and life, so that officials and people, rulers and ruled, all living under this political condition, unconsciously came to regard this political form as the most natural and the most reasonable."

What Wang Yanan was saying is that the ancient Chinese bureaucratic empire possessed an almost hypnotic power of enslavement, making it very hard for Chinese sunk within it to break their shackles without external assistance — whether the shackles of institutions or the prison of ideas. The arbitrary relocation of the people by emperors through the ages, described above, is precisely a display of a bureaucratic empire's capacity for enslavement. This historical phenomenon of the bureaucratic empire also, to a great extent, confirms the theory of the origins of the Chinese city put forward by the historian Chang Kwang-chih: namely, that "China's early cities were not the product of economic takeoff, but tools in the political sphere — and rather than tools for oppressing the ruled classes, they were tools by which the ruling class acquired and maintained political power." Mr. Chang's point is mainly to show that the Chinese city was not economic in its origins but arose from the needs of rule. Behind this view lies an evident implication in comparison with the origins of Western cities — that the latter originated for economic reasons. This insight largely corroborates the earlier related judgment of the economic historian Fu Zhufu. Although Mr. Fu had problems in his use of the concept of feudalism — following old conventions and thus inaccurate — and his claim that Chinese and Western cities arose from the same source fails to distinguish earlier scholarship, his view of the functions and roles of ancient Chinese cities remains incisive. His essay "The Position and Role of Ancient Chinese Cities in the National Economy" (《中国古代城市在国民经济中的地位和作用》), for instance, contains the following discussion:

> "One of the greatest characteristics of China's feudal system is that the nature of its cities and their path of development were entirely different from those of the cities of Europe's feudal age; consequently, the position of ancient Chinese cities within the entire feudal economic structure, and the role they played in economic development, were also entirely different."

> "The cities of medieval Europe were industrial and commercial centers — free cities independent of the direct control of feudal lords. Each city formed an economic unit of its own, with its own autonomous administrative and judicial bodies, even its own armed force for self-defense; each minted its own free coinage and set its own weights and measures for its own use... In China's feudal society, lasting roughly three thousand years, all the people's political, economic, and social life was, from beginning to end, under the direct control and interference of the feudal system, and a whole set of strict, complex, all-encompassing feudal norms — the 'kingly system' — was formed very early as the theoretical basis for that control and interference, handed down in one unbroken line for three thousand years and becoming a tradition everyone had to follow without the slightest transgression or violation. Under the grip of this traditional force, no person and no thing could stand outside this constraint and go its own way."

The views of these two scholars have won support from a growing number of historians. Fu Zhufu's discussion, through the comparative study of Chinese and Western cities, also confirms the character of the ancient Chinese bureaucratic empire. Lu Xiqi, professor of history at Xiamen University who studies the relationship between history and space, expressed a similar view in his 2014 essay collection *The Spatial Structure of Chinese History* (《中国历史的空间结构》). While endorsing Chang Kwang-chih's thesis, he states more explicitly that the ancient Chinese city originated in power and in the need to proclaim legitimacy. He writes:

> "Dynasties throughout Chinese history mobilized vast human and material resources to build central cities at every level centered on the capital. There were of course many concrete considerations, but the most basic motive was still chiefly to proclaim the dynasty's legitimacy or orthodox authority, to make manifest the state power towering above its subjects, and to seize more power across a wider spatial and social range."

Lu's splendid thesis on the origins of the ancient Chinese city also corroborates the insight in Gu Zhun's essay "On the Primitive Accumulation of Capital and the Development of Capitalism" (《资本的原始积累和资本主义发展》) comparing Chinese and Western cities. He said:

> "China never produced a commercial-standard political entity, nor could it ever have produced one. China's cities developed very early, and its navigation technology developed very early as well... China never lacked commerce... But China's cities, marketplaces, and shops were always under imperial control — they were the dynasty's money tree, and the dynasty would never permit the emergence of commercial-standard cities or city-states."

That is to say, from their origins through their later evolution, the functions of ancient Chinese cities never underwent major change. This nearly stagnant urban function largely determined the basic characteristics of the modern — even the contemporary — Chinese city. This difference in origins from the Western city, which produced city-state politics, marks the great divergence of Chinese and Western politics and economics. It caught the attention of the great sociologist Max Weber a century ago. Comparing Chinese and Western cities, Weber precisely placed the political status of the city resident foremost; he even denied that cities had ever existed in China because of the ancient Chinese city's character of domination and ruler power-expansion — for he held that only a city whose citizens possessed political rights could be called a city, and that a city's function must be political — political in Aristotle's sense of handling public affairs, not in the sense of ruling cunning. He said:

> "Fundamentally different from the West, the Chinese city — and all Eastern urban forms — lacks the character of a city. The Eastern city is not at all like the ancient 'city-state' (polis), and knows no medieval 'city law,' for it is not a 'community' (Gemeinde) with political privileges of its own."

Weber's thesis directly bears on whether the Chinese city possessed rule of law, and whether city residents possessed political freedom and political rights. Although these Chinese and Western scholars differ in their formulations of the functions and roles of the Chinese city, they all point to the same problem: the ancient Chinese city originated in and was subject to violent, arbitrary power. All affairs — in short, everything about the city: economics, culture, the military, trade, the movement of people — lay under the control, the flogging, even the life-and-death power of that arbitrary will. This is the plainly evident character of the bureaucratic empire. The Western city-state, by contrast, from ancient Greece onward formed on the basis of trade and spontaneous communal life. The many city-states of North Africa, the Greek peninsula, the Apennine peninsula — among them Carthage, Athens, Sparta, Rome, and the city-states of Asia Minor — were all most closely tied to trade and seafaring in their formation and development. Even a city-state like Sparta, which began in agriculture, turned to commerce because its agricultural output could not sustain survival. The governors of the city-states were not their founders; even the kings of Rome's regal or imperial age held no violent authority comparable to the kings and emperors of the Chinese bureaucratic empire. According to Plutarch's biography, Numa, the second king of regal Rome, overthrew the first king, Romulus, precisely because Romulus was too tyrannical — and after overthrowing Romulus he immediately tried to rule in a more plebeian way, seeking to regain the people's trust. And Publicola (Latin "Poplicola," meaning "one who cherishes the people like a father"), who overthrew the monarchy and founded the Roman Republic, went so far as to raze to the ground his own mansion — wealth not obtained through the corruption of power — simply in order to make the people feel he was no different from them. Athens' democratic politics, Rome's rule of law and constitutionalism were all closely connected to their city-state origins, and these cultural traditions were carried on in Europe in an unbroken thread. The European cities of the medieval feudal age, the commercial city-state republics such as Venice and Florence since the Renaissance, and the constitutional revolutions of the Low Countries are all excellent illustrations. The saying "city air makes you free" captures the atmosphere of the medieval European cities well: though often interrupted, it was never wholly extinguished — even after the rise of absolutist monarchies in the 17th century it suffered no extinction. Tocqueville mentions in *The Old Regime and the Revolution* that by the 14th century "no taxation without the consent of taxpayers" was already common talk in England and France — a gift of Europe's city tradition going back to ancient Greece and Rome. And in much of contemporary China that saying may still be unheard of. Why? The cities' origins differed, their original functions and purposes differed, their developmental paths differed, and so the outcomes differed. As Weber put it, China never formed a city in the sense of "a community with political rights"; it has always been, and remains to this day, a tool of the rulers' enslaving rule and power expansion — whether in its political function of stability or its economic function of revenue.

**9) Continuing to today: how the positioning and functions of the city affect the present**

**Xiao Han, Associate Professor of Law, China University of Political Science and Law**

Precisely because of this difference, the countryside in every Chinese dynasty was nothing but an appendage of the city. Beyond harvesting industrial and commercial taxes from the cities, the rulers relied — equally importantly — on cities of different ranks to tax and rule the surrounding countryside. Therefore, as a bureaucratic empire, ancient China's urban-rural relations could neither possibly form a tradition of a political-economic spatial structure in which city and countryside were relatively equal, as in England, nor even form, as did feudal medieval continental Europe, a dual political-economic spatial structure of "cities of contract" versus "countryside of status" — it was only the equalized servitude under the emperor that Hegel described, with city and village having no fundamental distinction in the sense of legal right.

It was precisely the bureaucratic empire's political-economic-cultural spatial structure — in which everything is subordinate to rule — that caused all phenomena arising from power to exert a siphoning effect on everything else social. The hukou system, which sought to nail the agricultural population to the land as a bond of status, could not stop people far from the centers of power at every level from swarming to the cities to make a living. Because once a city — whether newly built or long established — was chosen by the rulers, its power-based political, economic, and cultural activities produced a huge resource-integrating effect: daily administrative expenses, the ordinary lives of imperial grandees, normal trade and commerce, the extravagant lives of high officials and nobles — all provided no small number of jobs, drawing over the nearby imperial subjects who were struggling or even bankrupt for survival. Those who left their native places and toiled for survival were called, in ancient times, "drifters."

Perhaps only with this background can one understand the enormous work enthusiasm and extreme diligence shown by Chinese people — especially the Chinese peasantry — since Deng Xiaoping's reform and opening up. Though, as the economist Chen Zhiwu says, for institutional reasons that hard work did not make them rich. It is the product of the instinct for survival, the experience of insecurity, and fresh hope and joy combined. And so the city — as in every era of history, as in every country in the world — once again became Chinese people's directional choice for changing their life circumstances, and the urbanization process was thereby naturally set in motion. In his famous book *Urban and Rural China* (《城乡中国》), the economist Zhou Qiren summed up this phenomenon in a very simple formula: agglomeration of economy exceeding agglomeration of population is the dynamic mechanism of urbanization — "when economic density exceeds population density, it is bound to attract more population to gather."

But urbanization being naturally driven by human instinct and economic laws is one thing; whether a society's, a country's institutions are prepared for it is another. The urbanization process needs what Weber called the basic institution of "a community with political privileges of its own" — where "political privilege" does not mean privilege in the hierarchical sense, but the modern basic human rights: personal freedom, freedom of movement, property protection, and education, health care, social security, judicial justice, and the like. In other words, the city is a contractual existence among equals, not a status existence in the hierarchical sense — and personal freedom, freedom of movement, property protection, judicial justice, and so on are the premises of that contract. Yet the peasants who come to the city to make a living under the hukou system, lacking freedom of movement, in fact have no modern citizen status freely able to make contracts — in this sense, their status shows no notable improvement over that of the historical "drifters." This is the basis for understanding the unstable fate of the city's outside population: the city humiliates them with expulsions that offer no protection of personal freedom, treats their property destructively — all of this rests on that fact.

In this sense one can also understand why there are so many laws and regulations restricting movement and concerning custody and repatriation: by 2002 there were more than 30 local regulations legislated specifically on the custody-and-repatriation system, and the local regulations touching on the custody-and-repatriation system totaled 199 by the end of December 2002. This situation changed thanks to the 2003 Sun Zhigang case. Driven by overwhelming public opinion, and under a measure of enlightened reflection from the new government, the custody-and-repatriation system was formally abolished by the State Council on June 24 of that year; the custody stations were converted into relief stations, and relief was stipulated to require the active request of the person to be relieved.

The abolition of the custody-and-repatriation system was progress: the personal freedom of the urban outside population could no longer be wantonly stripped away, and even though forcible expulsions of outsiders still occurred from time to time afterward, unlawful deprivation of personal freedom had clearly decreased by a large margin.

Although a number of cities have abolished their local hukou restrictions and allowed the surrounding rural population to settle in the city, super-cities like Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen still have very strict hukou limits. For outside workers, the big city is no place to settle for long. Those without constant property lack a constant heart; the channels of advancement are blocked, and everything is kept in a perfunctory, makeshift state in order to save up money and return home.

Thus, in the giant cities and big cities where economic activity is most vigorous: on one hand, public services have neither the will to create better living conditions for the bottom-rung outside residents, nor indeed any real action; on the other hand, the outside bottom-rung residents have neither the will nor the ability to create better living conditions for themselves. Caught in this pincer, the outsiders create wealth for the city while also making the rulers feel that the city is expanding without limit and is hard to control — education, health care, housing, public security, transportation, countless pressures, hard to cope with. Though hard to control by normal means of governance, the dependence on coercive force keeps such discriminatory, campaign-style crackdowns recurring.
