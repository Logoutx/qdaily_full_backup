This week's editor's picks from QDaily.

The film *Avengers: Endgame* continues to break records. As of April 26, *Avengers: Endgame* had grossed more than 1.4 billion yuan in box-office receipts in its first three days on the Chinese mainland, and its Douban rating stands as high as 8.9.

“Only someone who knows nothing about Marvel would be surprised by the presale records set by *Avengers: Endgame*,” we wrote in an article published on April 22, before the film's release, [*What Rules of the Game Created the Most Successful Film Product Ever, the "Marvel Universe"?*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63081.html).

In that article, we analyzed why the Marvel Cinematic Universe has been so successful. For example: “In this sense, only the Marvel Cinematic Universe has grasped some of the core truths of contemporary entertainment. Audiences want strong entertainment stimuli, so it uses a rigorous assembly-line process to churn out films above a certain baseline. Audiences find it hard to sustain long-term attention and interest, so it uses a relatively coherent and suspenseful story line to tell viewers that this film's story is connected to the next. The personas of Iron Man and Captain America shaped by the Marvel Cinematic Universe have become spiritual icons for contemporary fans. To some extent, the Marvel Cinematic Universe has also catered to demands born of a certain subculture—after the character Loki became a big hit, for instance, it strengthened the brotherly bond storyline between Thor and Loki. All of these things together make up the Marvel Cinematic Universe, and it is also why concepts such as the star system, visual-effects blockbusters, IP, and hero team-ups cannot fully explain its success.”

Yet beneath the optimism there is cause for concern. “All attempts to find a formula for a successful entertainment product face a fundamental contradiction: audiences change far faster than any advance plan…… If this film (referring to *Avengers: Endgame*) marks the closing chapter of the Marvel Cinematic Universe's first season, then the next question is naturally whether it will enter a cycle of decline, like many TV series that are canceled after a few seasons.”

As a successful pop-culture product, *The Avengers* has inspired all kinds of interpretations. Among them, the book we recommend, [*Avengers and Philosophy*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63050.html), interprets the films from a philosophical angle and partly explains their appeal. For example, philosophy professor Mark D. White believes superheroes face the same moral dilemmas as ordinary people: Iron Man is a utilitarian, while Captain America is a deontologist. And whichever method of moral decision-making—utilitarianism or deontology—requires firm convictions to be effective.

Captain America once movingly expressed this conviction to Spider-Man: “It doesn't matter what the media says. It doesn't matter what the politicians or the mobs say. It doesn't matter if the whole country decides that something wrong is something right. This nation was founded on one principle above all else: we must hold fast to our convictions, no matter the adversity or the consequences. When the mob and the press and the whole world tell you to move, your job is to plant yourself like a tree beside the river of truth, and tell the whole world—‘No, you move.’”

And “the only thing that word can mean is conviction, the determination to stand by one's own moral choice, which is often confused with stubbornness. In fact, holding fast to one's convictions is a virtue,” [wrote Mark D. White in the book](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63050.html).

**Because virtues are usually easier talked about than practiced, it is fortunate that such people exist in reality. Whether it is American writer Jennifer Egan, Chinese photographer Yuan Xiaopeng, or the founders and participants of the rap competition *Underground 8 Mile* 《地下 8 英里》, we see, in each of them, the power and stories of holding fast to one's convictions in different ways.**

[At 57, Egan decided as a teenager that she truly wanted to become a writer](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63016.html), feeling that writing was a deep connection between her and the world and gave her life meaning. She kept writing, published many works, and received much recognition, including the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and the National Book Critics Circle Award. Today, as president of PEN America, she hopes to assert and uphold the value of literature today and contribute to freedom of expression. “I think telling the truth and being allowed to tell the truth are both crucial to democracy.…… I believe that through good writing and good reading, we can stay awake and alert, and our minds are less easily manipulated. We have to be careful about everything now, because too many forces want to control people, and those who don't think carefully are always more easily controlled.”

[Photographer Yuan Xiaopeng is the founder of the self-publishing studio Same Paper](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62489.html). Over the past five years, he and his colleagues have produced 11 photography books and one magazine, and run an independent bookstore now in its third year. In their view, “in the face of the relentless, ever-renewing Instagram feed, photobooks are a perfect antidote, hitting the spot and always offering a more refreshing and in-depth reading experience.” In addition, the author of this article says it was the hardest piece he has written in the past six months, because he feels that, at heart, he is like the photographer: “Finding your own form of expression may be the hardest part of any creative act.” “Although it is about photography, what the article probably wants to convey is more about the effort that the search itself demands.”

[*Underground 8 Mile* 《地下 8 英里》 is one of the most important rap competitions in China](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63282.html), founded on June 20, 2012. According to the event's organizer Ye Nan, the original intention behind it was simply that he and his friends had been busy with work and life and “needed to find the music we loved, and not let the flame in our hearts go out.” Contestants that year included Ding Fei and Beibei of the Honghuahui collective, and Ding Fei also helped organize the competition. This year, after the Chengdu stop was banned by the Ministry of Culture, the organizers announced that the 2018–19 *Underground 8 Mile* season would be suspended entirely. In a sense, the story of *Underground 8 Mile*'s founders and participants is also the story of rap in China—full of twists and perseverance. “For more than 20 years of Chinese rap, has any year been easy?” said Ye Nan.

**Besides the Marvel Universe piece, this week we also highlight two business reports, one on the mid- to low-end sneaker market and the other on changes in the express-delivery industry.**

[On the former](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62988.html), based on our interviews with brands, distributors, sneaker designers, and consumers, the mid- to low-end sneaker market is now in a state of convergence and free-for-all competition, with changes underway in design, distribution, marketing, and supply chain.

[As for the latter](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63205.html), we found that over the past two years the express-delivery industry has slowed considerably: the growth rate of delivery volume has fallen from 51% before 2017 to 26.46% now, returning to the level seen after the 2008 financial crisis. How should we understand these changes? We offer several interpretations. For example, express delivery is the infrastructure of the e-commerce industry, and e-commerce has been an important driver of Chinese consumption over the past decade, so the slowdown in express-delivery growth essentially mirrors the broader slowdown in consumption. The growth of express delivery is increasingly reliant on low-priced goods from Pinduoduo, yet rural growth is also starting to decline. Market share in the express-delivery industry is becoming ever more concentrated in the hands of large companies, but those large companies will not necessarily gain pricing power as a result.

**In recent years, the changes in the business world have involved not just markets and industries in a macro sense; many large companies have also run into plenty of trouble, exposing many problems.**

After the Ethiopian Airlines crash and the grounding of the 737 Max, [Boeing released its latest quarterly earnings](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63254.html): in the first three months of this year, Boeing's revenue, net profit, and core operating profit all posted rare negative growth. [The *New York Times* found, after reviewing hundreds of pages of internal emails, company documents, and federal government records and interviewing more than a dozen current and former employees](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63190.html), that Boeing's corporate culture often valued production speed over production quality. Faced with prolonged production delays, Boeing repeatedly ignored concerns raised by employees and simply urged workers to quickly build Dreamliners.

“The question Boeing now faces is whether, in order to speed up work on the Max and catch up with rival Airbus, it overlooked safety risks in the design—such as the anti-stall system involved in both crashes,” [the article noted](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63190.html).

In just one week in February, YouTube ran into a string of troubles: it served ads on videos promoting anti-vaccination; videos instructing young children to commit suicide appeared on the platform, sparking panic across the United States; a user made a popular video exposing pedophiles' revelry on YouTube; and there was also a “bestiality” controversy. Amid all this chaos, [the *New York Times* focused on the coolest, most rational person behind YouTube—CEO Susan Wojcicki](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63274.html).

“I wanted to know what kind of CEO she is, how she deals with one crisis after another, and whether there is any sense of urgency inside YouTube. So over the past few months, I met with more than a dozen current and former employees of YouTube and Google, and interviewed Wojcicki three times. The result is both reassuring and worrying. Behind the world's largest and most chaotic video platform stands a calm, clear-headed person. But given how often and how widely terrifying incidents and foolish behavior erupt on YouTube, her composure may seem out of place,” [the article noted](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63274.html).

**By the way, this week's Bauhaus centenary series has been updated with [the fourth installment](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63231.html). When we talk about a "Bauhaus city," what exactly are we talking about? The truth is paradoxical: there is no such thing as a true "Bauhaus city," yet Bauhaus has indeed shaped our urban spaces.**

The article says: “Today, cities are being taken over by two new architectural forms. One might be called postmodernism, using computer technology and more economical shells and space frames to create newer shapes that defy the principles of mechanics. The other uses local materials and antiquated forms. Some cities are dramatically divided into new districts and old districts to display these two forms.…… For the latter form, sometimes it stems from intellectual architects' reflections on the International Style and globalization.…… Although Bauhaus devotees shaped today's urban architectural style with their bare hands, they also made earlier styles stand out more clearly in history.”

**Finally, a few more things worth noting:**

[The Ministry of Natural Resources, which oversees the release of state-owned land, recently issued a notice](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63200.html) reiterating the need to increase residential land supply. Economists believe that local governments are the sole suppliers of land. With land supply tangled up with local finances, local governments have little incentive to increase supply to stabilize prices when land or housing prices are rising, and may even choose to implement a “land-hoarding policy,” thereby driving up prices for commercial residential land. This looks more like a business decision.

As the country's largest cargo airline by fleet size, [SF Express disclosed in a recent 6.5-billion-yuan financing plan](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63272.html) that 1.6 billion yuan of it will be used to buy aircraft and maintenance materials. Adding SF Express's own contribution, total aircraft acquisition costs will exceed 2.1 billion yuan. By the end of 2018, SF Express's all-cargo fleet stood at 66 aircraft, operating 65 scheduled routes. Counting chartered belly-hold cargo capacity on passenger airliners, SF Express covered 2,134 air-cargo routes in 2018, earning roughly 7 yuan per kilogram of air freight.

Four major companies released earnings this week. [Starbucks' second-quarter earnings report](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63294.html) pushed its share price to the highest point in the past year. This quarter, Starbucks' total revenue was $6.3 billion, up 4.4% year over year, slightly below market expectations; [China Mobile also released earnings this week](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63230.html), with Q1 2019 revenue of 185 billion yuan, down 0.3% from the same period last year, and net profit of 23.7 billion yuan, down 8.3% year over year; for the fiscal third quarter ending March 2019, [P&G's net sales were $16.5 billion](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63267.html), higher than Wall Street's expected $16.36 billion, with organic growth of 5%. Net profit rose 9.2% to $2.776 billion; [according to Facebook's Q1 2019 earnings report](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63263.html), Facebook revenue grew 26% year over year to $15.08 billion, beating market expectations of $14.98 billion.

[QuestMobile released its *Spring 2019 Big Report*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63249.html). From January to March 2019, the number of new monthly active users on the national mobile internet was only 7.62 million, less than the average quarterly increase of more than 10 million in 2018. User time spent also slowed. To sustain growth, JD.com and Taobao have long been paying more attention to finding new users in third- and fourth-tier cities and below. As a tool that can quickly gain traffic from large-company platforms, mini programs are experiencing rapid growth: products with more than 5 million monthly mini-program users are now growing faster than mobile apps.

Since 2012, Peter Ashlock has been working for Uber, completing more than 25,000 trips. His Nissan Altima has driven 218,000 miles (about 350,000 kilometers), nearly the distance from Earth to the moon. Passengers have given him a high score of 4.93 out of 5. Compared with the people who got rich from Uber's startup success, [what kind of life does he lead as an ordinary driver](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/62921.html)?

[What exactly is "private photography"?](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63156.html) On this subject, Japanese photography critic Kotaro Iizawa once wrote a book, *The Theory of Private Photography* 《私摄影论》, using the four photographers Takuma Nakahira, Masahisa Fukase, Nobuyoshi Araki, and Shigeo Gocho as examples to explore the question. We talked with him and offer you some clues for thinking about it. For example, “private photography” actually seeks to escape ideology and the obligation to document reality, no longer taking precise description of events but a certain emotional atmosphere within lived experience as the standard for creation. But is this really possible? If so, to what extent?

Canada hosted a debate on the theme of ["Happiness: Capitalism vs. Marxism"](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63138.html). The two sides were Jordan Peterson, defender of the free market and known as the “lobster professor,” and Slavoj Žižek, standard-bearer of the global left-wing movement. But, “People had expected a spark-filled 'debate of the century,' but it turned out to be a cordial exchange of views,” the prominent left-wing magazine *Jacobin* commented, not without irony. On human beings and the future world, Žižek and Peterson seem to have more in common than differences.

[Several elementary schools in Barcelona are considering removing hundreds of children's books from school libraries](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63169.html), including *Little Red Riding Hood* and the legend of Saint George. A gender-equality committee formed by parents believes that some of these children's books contain strongly sexist content, imprinting children with “stereotypes” and lacking educational value.

On April 25, [a relief law for victims of the former Eugenic Protection Law was unanimously passed at a plenary session of Japan's House of Councillors](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63276.html). The bill stipulates that the state will “reflect and apologize” to the victims and pay each victim a one-time compensation of 3.2 million yen (about 190,000 yuan), but most victims are not satisfied.

Physicist [David Thouless died in Cambridge, UK](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63197.html), at the age of 84. He was awarded a share of the 2016 Nobel Prize in Physics for successfully using mathematical methods to explain the remarkable states of matter, such as superconductivity and superfluidity; behavioral scientist [David Hamburg died in Washington, D.C., USA](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/63241.html), at the age of 93. He was a behavioral scientist well known to the public, and his conflict-resolution theory was validated in negotiations with Soviet leaders during the Cold War and with African guerrillas who had kidnapped students.
