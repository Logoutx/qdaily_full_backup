@@QD_BODY@@
The age of the "Superman" is over.

With the retirement of "Superman" Li Ka-shing, the biggest change seems to be that people can no longer put questions to him at shareholder meetings of the Cheung Kong group. Questions for this 90-year-old elder of the business world — about Hong Kong's economy and politics, and about where Hong Kong's young people should go from here.

These were the questions Hong Kong people, inside and outside the meeting hall, cared about more. By comparison, hardly anyone discussed whether the Cheung Kong group companies might not survive — for years they had been bound up with Hong Kong's real estate, ports, retail, telecommunications, water and electricity; nor did anyone worry that Li Ka-shing might mishandle a deal — since people bestowed the title "Superman" on him in 1979, it had gone unchallenged for nearly 40 years. Yet the questions above had, in the past few years, left people barely able to breathe.

And apart from Li Ka-shing, who else was there to ask?

In 2016, when the international credit rating agency Moody's downgraded Hong Kong's long-term debt outlook from "stable" to "negative," people grew more anxious about an economy already showing no signs of recovery, and Li Ka-shing's view mattered enormously. At a press conference he said the Hong Kong government's finances were sound and its reserves ample, ["there's nothing wrong, so Moody's should take another careful look."](http://gbcode.rthk.hk/TuniS/news.rthk.hk/rthk/ch/component/k2/1249156-20160317.htm?share=facebook) He also rebutted the view that Hong Kong would be dragged down by the mainland, saying the mainland was the foundation of Hong Kong's politics and economy, and that without the mainland's support, "I believe the Hang Seng Index would fall by at least half."

Then just last Friday, March 16, at the CK Hutchison annual results announcement, Li Ka-shing announced he would step down. A reporter again asked him: you sold The Center — are you really pulling your capital out? Li said such talk of capital flight showed a lack of basic economics. He remained bullish on Hong Kong and would keep bidding for land. Coming from Li Ka-shing, that was some comfort, at least.

Also at Friday's press conference, someone asked his view on the idea that "a girlfriend won't marry you until you buy her a flat." His take: "Don't place too much importance on buying a home. If a girl says no flat, no marriage, then if it were me, I'd say: you're still young, better go find someone else."

Since starting his business in 1950, this Hong Kong man of Chaozhou ancestry had built from nothing a legend of his generation. He began as a watch apprentice and, riding Hong Kong's opportunity as the world's entrepôt, became the city's plastic-flower king. Over the decades as Hong Kong real estate took off, he moved into property, ports, retail, energy, telecommunications and pharmaceuticals, assembling a vast commercial empire — first in Hong Kong, then quickly across mainland China, and eventually around the world.

As Lee Kuan Yew observed, Li Ka-shing never created product brands worth praising; he mostly bought and sold. But with uncannily precise judgment, he again and again bought at the very bottom and sold near the very top, becoming Asia's richest man in 1999 and holding the title repeatedly.

Hong Kong writer Chip Tsao told QDaily in an interview that Li Ka-shing's standing in Hong Kong peaked from the 1980s to the early 1990s. Unlike the tycoons who had grown old, he was neither a local comprador like Robert Ho Tung nor a rich man from Shanghai like Y.K. Pao or Run Run Shaw. Li was the model of the self-made man: once he had money, he went around the world acquiring assets under the banner of Hong Kong capital. At the time the television drama [*Below the Lion Rock*](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/2132.html) was a hit on Hong Kong TV. "Li Ka-shing was the embodiment of the Lion Rock spirit," Tsao said.

Li Ka-shing represented Hong Kong at its best. Hong Kong, spared the worst of the war, had built up its manufacturing sector in the late 1940s and then, with the growth of global trade, became Asia's logistics hub. By the late 1980s Hong Kong was the world's busiest container port, and Hong Kong merchants were the single largest source of outside investment into the mainland. A newly risen Hong Kong middle class began upgrading its consumption, driving communications, supermarkets, entertainment — and, of course, property. Li caught every one of these waves, at first riding the trend and later leading it; at one point his family's companies accounted for 13% of the entire Hong Kong Stock Exchange's market value.

But as the controversy over surging home prices grew from the mid-to-late 1990s and the wealth gap widened, when people talked about Li's businesses again — his private residential properties totaling 1/7 of all of Hong Kong's; Hutchison Whampoa still holding 12 berths at Kwai Chung and handling nearly half the port's containers; ParknShop's 70% share of the city's supermarket retail — Li's symbolism was no longer what it had been. People even coined a joke about the "Li Force Field" — it was Li who weakened typhoons as they neared Hong Kong, because he needs you to show up for work as usual.

Many young people still take Li Ka-shing as an idol. But many also complain that in the Hong Kong he helped build, the old glory is gone and there is no longer any chance of becoming "the next Li Ka-shing."

But when an era ends and people look back to appraise it, they often set aside the "present" and remember the best of times. So it is with Li Ka-shing, and so with Hong Kong. Over the past three days, the front pages of Hong Kong media talked mostly about the curtain falling on an era, about Li Ka-shing as the symbol of the Hong Kong spirit.

Li Ka-shing's 68 years in business are also a history of Hong Kong commerce.

### Hong Kong's manufacturing began in the 1940s, built on Shanghai capital and mainland migrants

In the early years of British rule, Hong Kong's first role was that of a free port. In population and industrial capacity alike, Hong Kong could not compare with Shanghai of the same period. In 1930, Hong Kong's population was just 27% of Shanghai's.

Li Ka-shing had come to Hong Kong from Chaozhou with his parents, refugees from the war. In 1940, when the 12-year-old Li arrived, the city was a free port. The colonial government was pro-business: it kept profits tax low, limited spending on social welfare and on minimum labor protections, and practiced free trade with free movement of capital.

Li started out working as a shop assistant in the watch workshop of his uncle, which later became a watch company.

After the war ended, the capital and migrants flooding into Hong Kong reached a peak. Between 1946 and 1950, no less than US$500 million flowed into Hong Kong from mainland China in the form of goods, securities, gold and foreign currency. In the same period, more than a million people poured into Hong Kong; together with Li Ka-shing, they would help rebuild Hong Kong's postwar economy. In the decades that followed, Hong Kong's birth rate also peaked, producing the "baby boom" generation.

Commercial ties with mainland China were something Li Ka-shing cared about from the very start.

In 1950 Li decided to found Cheung Kong Plastics, intending to export to the mainland; the trade prospects between Hong Kong and the mainland then looked optimistic. The war had ended, and in January 1950 Britain's Labour government recognized the People's Republic of China. But Hong Kong businessmen soon sensed the political instability: Sino-British relations deteriorated during the Korean War. This shook the confidence of the British trading houses — foreign merchants are always quicker to lose faith in Hong Kong's future. "Our good days are over," sighed John Keswick of Jardine Matheson.

Fortunately, the United States allowed Hong Kong-made goods to enter, and America gradually developed into Hong Kong's largest export market. Hong Kong became one of the earliest regions to enter the postwar world trading system.

The Cheung Kong Plastics that Li founded then, however, was an ordinary one among more than 300 plastics factories.

The two largest plastics factories — Zhongyuan Plastics and Kader Industrial — had each been moved to Hong Kong in 1947 by a wealthy Shanghai merchant fearful of war and nationalization. The workers they hired were mostly mainlanders who had fled to Hong Kong around the war, like Li Ka-shing.

But while the others made plastics, Li saw plastic flowers. Seven years into his venture, he read a report on Italian plastic flowers in a trade magazine and flew to Italy to investigate. Cheung Kong Plastics became the world's largest plastic-flower manufacturer, and Li was dubbed the "Plastic Flower King."

### In the era of transition from British to Chinese capital, Li Ka-shing's commercial empire truly began

In 1963, two Texas real estate men brought Hilton to Hong Kong and spent US$14 million opening the Hong Kong Hilton — a 900-room hotel in the bustling Central district, Hilton's largest outside North America, its rooftop Eagle's Nest nightclub a gathering place for the city's elite.

According to The New York Times at the opening, such a big-bet investment was taken at the time as a sign that Hong Kong would remain independent of China for the foreseeable future.

But the handover of power began quickly, starting in the business world. From the late 1960s to the early 1970s, the baby-boom generation of Hong Kongers came of age. "There was nobody ahead of us," Chan Koonchung recalls.

When the baby-boomers entered Hong Kong's workforce, in many expanding, rising industries they were often the first well-educated Chinese employees, reporting directly to foreigners or capitalists. "It seemed that whether in family or school, culture or society, everything happened to have prepared my generation with precisely this economic orientation, to welcome the quarter-century of Hong Kong's rapid economic growth that followed," Chan said.

Plastic flowers gave Li the first capital to move into real estate. In 1958, Li bought his first building.

Li's generation of Hong Kong merchants all accumulated wealth rapidly through property; even after branching into dozens of industries, people still habitually called them "property tycoons."

In the 1960s, Hong Kong's relationship with the mainland became once again a test of businessmen's judgment. The Cultural Revolution, beginning in 1966, unsettled Hong Kong: turmoil, a property market crash, businessmen fleeing. Li judged this was not the end of prosperity and calmly bought up large amounts of real estate — the start of his fortune.

Cheung Kong Real Estate was founded in 1971; in 1972 it was renamed Cheung Kong Holdings and listed. Before the 1973 stock market crash arrived, the Hang Seng Index reached an all-time high in 1972 — up 7-fold from 1969, with trading volume up 16-fold.

In the late 1970s, China and Britain opened negotiations over Hong Kong's return, culminating in the 1984 signing of the Sino-British Joint Declaration.

During that transition, Li Ka-shing was seen as the successor chosen by the colonial government. As of July 1978, he still showed no interest in acquiring foreign trading houses, transferring his large stake in Wharf to Y.K. Pao. But in September 1979, Li announced he would buy HSBC's 22.4% stake in another trading house, Hutchison Whampoa. He kept increasing his holding, and by 1981 had become chairman of Hutchison Whampoa, whose business would later spread across China and overseas.

"By the late 1970s the colonial authorities had decided to pull out. They wanted someone decent, obedient and reliable. The merchants from Shanghai had grown old, and the second generation of local compradors wasn't up to much, so they settled on Li Ka-shing and had HSBC lend him the money. He took over Hutchison Whampoa and began his commercial empire," Tsao recalled.

Having taken over Hutchison Whampoa, Li thus became Hong Kong's first Chinese taipan (the head of a foreign trading house, a word from Cantonese) — and the nickname "Superman" arrived at about that time.

### Hong Kong capital brought manufacturing to Guangdong while transforming itself toward property and finance

In 1978 Xi Zhongxun inspected Shenzhen and Zhuhai, and later, as Guangdong party secretary, granted generous policies to Shenzhen, Guangzhou and the Pearl River Delta. Xi said at the time: "Whatever gets production up, do it. Don't start by criticizing what -ism it is. They are capitalist, but some of their good methods we should learn."

That September, Li Ka-shing, as a member of the Hong Kong and Macau delegation, was invited to the National Day ceremony in Beijing. Apart from the occasional incident of mainlanders slipping across the border into Hong Kong, and the official supply of goods to Hong Kong through the famine years, Hong Kong and the mainland had had no open contact for years. Even this delegation's trip came through an invitation from the Xinhua News Agency's Hong Kong branch — at the time the main channel of contact between the two sides.

It was also Li Ka-shing's first visit to mainland China in 39 years. But no publicly available record captures how he felt at the time.

Less than three months after Hong Kong businessmen were invited north, on December 18, 1978, at the Third Plenary Session of the 11th Central Committee, the Chinese government announced reform and opening. The first place to open would be Shenzhen — word had been out since the start of the year.

Shenzhen hoped to replicate the free port next door. Thus the mainland's first export-processing industrial zone, the Shekou Industrial Zone, was built, complete with factories, a port and rail links. Though it could not go as far as Hong Kong's simple, low-tax system with no foreign-exchange controls and no meddling in business operations, it did put in place preferential policies to attract foreign companies.

The Shekou project was undertaken by China Merchants, one of the state-owned enterprises that had quietly operated in Hong Kong since the war; the others included China Travel Service, Bank of China, and China Resources, which had monopolized goods traffic between the two sides.

While the Shekou zone was being developed, a group of Hong Kong businessmen were invited to visit, to whet their interest in moving manufacturing and exports to Shekou. Li Ka-shing asked whether Hong Kong merchants could buy in and take part in Shekou's construction — a deeper level of involvement. Yuan Geng, then in charge of Shekou, gave no response.

The authorities remained somewhat cautious, but business contacts between Hong Kong and the mainland did become frequent.

Hong Kong merchants' investment in the mainland began here. From 1979 to 2004, 66.2% of the foreign direct investment attracted by Guangdong came from Hong Kong, worth US$996 billion. And from 1985 to 2003, 30% of all the foreign direct investment attracted by China went to Guangdong.

Meanwhile, Hong Kong became the gateway for overseas companies entering mainland China. In 1978 Hong Kong's economy grew 12%; foreign companies set up offices in Hong Kong one after another, and the Hang Seng Index rose 22%.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319124004rboAc9NVBFQtfqum.jpg-w600)

In the first years of the special economic zone, Hong Kong's trade volume surged. From the 1980s to the mid-to-late 1990s, total trade grew 20% every year. In 1988 Hong Kong's total trade surpassed South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore, putting it first among the Four Asian Tigers and second in Asia only to Japan. When *A Better Tomorrow* was released in 1986, it swept Asia.

By 1998, Hong Kong manufacturers employed about 5 million workers in Hong Kong and the mainland — five times the 1984 figure. This was the stage when the phrase "front shop, back factory" (Hong Kong as the shopfront, the mainland as the factory) emerged. Because multinational corporations chose Hong Kong as their regional headquarters, Hong Kong both enabled this arrangement and benefited from it. Hong Kong merchants knew the mainland way of doing business; more importantly, their services in finance, remote management, accounting, transport and logistics were first-rate. Mutual prosperity.

For Hong Kong, though, the crisis had already begun. In the early years, Hong Kong-capital companies simply moved simple production to Guangdong without investing much in R&D. As European and American companies began building R&D centers in China, Hong Kong capital did bring more modern technology and management models into mainland factories, but it no longer had an edge. In the end, as Hong Kong's manufacturing shifted out, it produced no company like Apple or H&M — a company that hands off manufacturing but firmly holds onto the value of its products.

In 2011, after the publication of Lee Kuan Yew's memoir *Hard Truth*, he [was interviewed by a Straits Times reporter](http://hudcsingapore.com/why-spore-cant-produce-a-fortune-500-company/why-spore-cant-produce-a-fortune-500-company/) and asked about Singapore's economic outlook. Lee said scale limits the development of small places, industry in particular. He gave an example: "Look at Hong Kong — what does it have? Property developers and investors. Does Li Ka-shing have any product that sells well around the world? No. He just buys property, ports, retailers and telecom companies."

But for Hong Kong businessmen shifting into property, this was not yet a problem. The mainland property business was only just beginning.

### From the 1990s, Hong Kong capital shaped Shanghai's commercial property, and the Hong Kong Stock Exchange helped state enterprises raise money

China's two stock exchanges were founded in the early 1990s, but domestic cash was insufficient to meet financing needs. Meanwhile, the Hong Kong Stock Exchange sent people to inspect the mainland, studying whether mainland state enterprises could be attracted to list there — and solve the problem of HKEx-listed companies being too heavily weighted toward property.

After Vice Premier Zhu Rongji met with HKEX chairman Charles Lee, they decided to select about ten state enterprises to list in Hong Kong. Tsingtao Brewery listed on June 29, 1993.

This chain of events can also be traced back to Li Ka-shing. Cheung Kong Holdings listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange in 1972 under the code 0001 — there were then four exchanges in Hong Kong. For administrative and regulatory reasons, the four formally merged in 1986, and all stock codes were rearranged; Li Ka-shing's Cheung Kong Holdings became 0001. The common view is that this was because Cheung Kong was then the company that best represented Hong Kong.

CITIC's Hong Kong branch, founded before the reform era, had early on invited Li Ka-shing to serve as chairman. By 1990, Li had helped CITIC acquire a HKEx-listed company for HK$700 million, achieving a backdoor listing.

After CITIC came the "red chips" on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange. The term denotes both a listed company with a Chinese background — whose largest controlling stake is directly or indirectly held by a mainland department or enterprise — and a company whose value sits between the best blue chips and the lowliest "white chips."

The mainland property projects of Li Ka-shing and other Hong Kong merchants also mostly began around that time. Hong Kong capital began flooding into the mainland.

The mainland needed property developers more than Hong Kong did. In Hong Kong, the government had already declared war on the developers; one commentator said Li Ka-shing was "like a giant whale stranded on the beach." On the mainland, the government was short of money with nothing to trade — its most valuable asset was land. In a documentary about Shanghai's old-city redevelopment at the time, district governments negotiated with developers from the United States and Hong Kong, transferring land development rights for central plots and moving residents to the suburbs. At a groundbreaking ceremony, one developer invited the most influential Hong Kong star of the day, Jackie Chan. The changes on the mainland are enormous, Chan said.

[According to the Shanghai Local Chronicles Office](http://www.shtong.gov.cn/node2/node2245/node74728/node74740/node74854/node74858/userobject1ai89831.html), the number of Hong Kong merchants investing in real estate and construction rose from 5 in 1990 to 327 in 1994 — second only to the 379 in trade and commercial services — and "the majority of extra-large projects over US$100 million were real estate investments."

By 1995, [half of the property projects in the Lujiazui Finance and Trade Zone](http://www.shtong.gov.cn/node2/node2247/node4589/node79617/node79623/userobject1ai103692.html) came from Sino-foreign joint ventures and wholly foreign-owned enterprises. Of the 37 projects with foreign participation, two-thirds were mainland-Hong Kong joint ventures or Hong Kong-owned.

Looking back, Shanghai's commercial property was more or less shaped by Hong Kong capital. K11 belongs to New World Development; IAPM and the International Finance Center in Lujiazui were developed by Sun Hung Kai Properties; Xintiandi belongs to Shui On Land; and Plaza 66 belongs to Hang Lung Properties.

Li Ka-shing had the biggest name among Hong Kong merchants, but he was not high-profile in mainland property. His investment style did not change significantly upon coming to the mainland. In Hong Kong, the prime properties in the best districts of Hong Kong Island had long been taken by old foreign trading houses like Jardine Matheson and Swire before the Hong Kong merchants made their fortunes.

Before the 1997 Asian financial crisis, property development contributed most of Cheung Kong Holdings' sales revenue — but usually in lower-priced districts, mostly residential projects, with quite a few low-cost public housing estates built in cooperation with the government.

In Beijing and Shanghai, likewise, landmark commercial property rarely was — or at least was rarely publicly known to be — owned by Cheung Kong Holdings or Hutchison Whampoa. The most conspicuous was Oriental Plaza on Beijing's Chang'an Avenue, invested in 1992, but it is generally considered a failed investment.

Li Ka-shing's early mainland property projects were mainly in the south, closer to Hong Kong and his ancestral Chaozhou. For instance, Shanghai's "Evernight City Center," the Shenzhen Foreign Trade Center, a residential villa district in Guangzhou, and an international school for expatriate children. Residential projects in Beihai, Guangxi and Dongguan, Guangdong, and the controversial renovation and reconstruction of Fuzhou's Three Lanes and Seven Alleys.

Beyond that, Li Ka-shing invested in the mainland in docks, power plants and highways. In 1992 he invested in building the Jinshan standard container terminal and the international deep-water port terminal in Shanghai. In 1993, Hutchison Whampoa was permitted to buy into Shenzhen's Yantian Port, holding a 70% stake and jointly investing 6 billion yuan with Yantian Port to build a container terminal — the largest foreign-capital project introduced into the mainland that year. One director of Yantian Port was Ma Chenshu — Pony Ma's father.

Shantou University was completed in 1986; in 1980 Li Ka-shing decided to fund this public university, which is sustained by a private foundation — a cooperation model without precedent anywhere in the world. That same year, the Li Ka-shing Foundation was formally established. In 2002 the foundation donated to found the Cheung Kong Graduate School of Business.

Watsons and Chow Tai Fook are two important Hong Kong-capital retail brands, belonging respectively to Li Ka-shing's Hutchison Whampoa and Cheng Yu-tung's New World Development. By 2017, Chow Tai Fook had 2,246 retail outlets in the mainland. Operating profit from the mainland market accounted for 75% of the entire Chow Tai Fook Group. Watsons' main market is Europe, but in 2016 its mainland business still generated HK$20.9 billion (18.5 billion yuan) in revenue.

But the outside world generally considers property the main business of Hong Kong merchants on the mainland. New World held the most land among them: 26 million square meters of mainland land reserves in 2015, equal to 1.26 times the area of Shanghai's Huangpu District. At the peak in 2007, Cheung Kong Holdings held over 18.6 million square meters of mainland land reserves. According to China Economic Weekly, over the past 10 years the share of mainland property and hotel business in Cheung Kong Holdings' total turnover grew steadily year by year from 10% in 2005, overtaking the Hong Kong market in fiscal 2013 to reach a peak share of 50.9%.

But after 2005, several of Li Ka-shing's mainland plots long remained undeveloped and were eventually resold. For example, in 2007 Li bought the Bihai Yinhu project — 3,800 mu — in Jiangmen, Guangdong, planned as "a comprehensive large-scale commercial property project integrating a yacht club, polo, shopping, entertainment and high-end housing." In the end it was sold on February 28 this year.

### Having merged into China's economy, the mainland's needs of Hong Kong changed too

As manufacturing migrated, the factory buildings vacated in the early years were turned to property development; some people even closed or relocated docks to build housing on the land. Property's share of Hong Kong's fiscal revenue kept growing, and the big developers kept profiting from it.

In this period Li Ka-shing invested heavily in infrastructure and moved into livelihood services. In 1985 his Hutchison Whampoa launched mobile telephone service in Hong Kong. In 1985, Hutchison acquired Hongkong Electric Holdings (now called Power Assets Holdings). In 1996, Hutchison consolidated the marketing and operations of Hong Kong's mobile, paging and fixed-line services, announcing the establishment of Hutchison Telecommunications.

Li Ka-shing was so confident about the future of mobile phones that he became a salesman himself. When Hutchison began its mobile business, it was only two years since Motorola launched the first commercially sold mobile phone; not many people felt the need to spend tens of thousands of dollars to make phone calls at any time. One day Li showed up at a land auction, and in the middle of the bidding picked up his mobile phone to make a call, appearing to listen to important auction-related information. The next day, photos of Li on his phone ran in the South China Morning Post and other major newspapers.

Together with the previously acquired ParknShop supermarket chain and Watsons, plus Fortress, acquired in 1986, it became hard for the vast majority of Hong Kong people to get through daily life — clothing, food, housing, transport — entirely clear of Li Ka-shing's businesses.

Describing the changes of Hong Kong's property tycoons in those years, Ng Tak-wing, a professor of political science at the University of Macau, said: "They increasingly resemble Japanese kabushiki kaisha like Mitsubishi, or Korean chaebol like Samsung" — sprawling groups spanning property, hotels, retail, telecom, container terminals, power and media — "no longer like the Western commercial giants — Microsoft, Philips, Volkswagen — which focus on a single industry."

In 1997, the ten largest Hong Kong merchant families already controlled 45% of the Hong Kong stock market's capitalization.

Hong Kong business lawyer Mike West told us in an interview that the monopoly of Li Ka-shing and the other Hong Kong tycoons made Hong Kong's economy oddly distorted. "Hong Kong people feel both positive and negative emotions toward these tycoons," said Joseph Fan, director of the Institute of Economics and Finance at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, in an interview with QDaily.

This became especially obvious after the 1997 Asian financial crisis broke out.

In the first half of 1997 home prices were still rising — residential prices climbed 63.62% from early 1996 to the third quarter of 1997. Buyers had to queue a day in advance. But then the property bubble began to burst; prices kept falling for six years, losing nearly two-thirds of their value. The middle class was saddled with debt as a result.

Some believe the Sino-British Joint Declaration signed in 1984 was an important factor in inflating the property bubble. "From the date the Joint Declaration takes effect until June 30, 1997, new land granted under paragraph 3 of this Annex shall be limited to 50 hectares a year, excluding land granted to the Hong Kong Housing Authority for rental public housing." The economy grew, the middle class had demand for homeownership, and the limit on land grants reduced supply, pushing prices up. Hong Kong's Jones Lang Wootton residential index shows that over the 13 and a half years from January 1984 to July 1997, residential prices in Hong Kong exactly tripled.

The property tycoons, though, seemed to ride out the disaster unscathed. When Hong Kong's property market was damaged, Li Ka-shing's Cheung Kong and Hutchison expanded their property business into the mainland, Singapore and Britain. In 1997 he began heading into the mainland's west, building the Metropolitan Plaza in Chongqing, and told reporters that any Hong Kong merchant who looked down on the western mainland "would be making a huge mistake."

After the Asian financial crisis ended in 1999, Li Ka-shing became Asia's richest man for the first time, ranking tenth on Forbes magazine's world rich list and being named Time magazine's "Entrepreneur of the Millennium." He went on to top Asia's rich list repeatedly and was Hong Kong's richest man for 20 consecutive years.

Meanwhile, Hong Kong was getting worse, especially compared with the mainland's high growth at the time. In 2005 Hong Kong lost its status as the world's busiest container port, falling to third behind Singapore and Shanghai. Hong Kong's GDP was 25% of mainland China's in 1990; by 2017 it accounted for just 2.48% of the mainland's — the equivalent of only nine days of mainland output.

People began to ask: what did Hong Kong do wrong, and who should be held responsible? The property tycoons?

Milton Friedman, the Nobel laureate in economics, had long championed the "Hong Kong model," calling Hong Kong "the last bastion of free-market economics." In his 1980 book *Free to Choose*, he wrote: "If you want to see the free market truly at work, go to Hong Kong."

Some Hong Kong scholars and critics no longer followed Friedman's view as they once had. Dr. Joseph Fan and business lawyer Mike West, both interviewed by us, argued that Hong Kong is not the best example of a "free market," and that the change did not happen only in the most recent 20 years.

Fan cited unreasonable land policy, arguing that compared with other emerging Asian markets, Hong Kong was nothing special — "even quite typical." Mike West pointed to the monopoly for which Li Ka-shing is much criticized, which prevents Hong Kong from competing as freely, healthily and vibrantly as British and American markets. Such views have been accepted by more and more people.

Still, plenty of people take the view of the most recent dozen years or so. After all, Hong Kong's economic decline happened only in the last dozen years or so. The late Hong Kong political economist Tsang Shu-ki noted in a 2007 paper that over the previous dozen years, the economic integration of the two places had been asymmetric integration. In that integration, Hong Kong did not optimize its industrial structure but simply did nothing, so that most industries lost their agglomeration effects.

This became even more conspicuous after the 2004 implementation of the Mainland and Hong Kong Closer Economic Partnership Arrangement (CEPA) and the 2003 opening of individual visit permits for mainland travelers to Hong Kong.

In addition, as China joined the World Trade Organization and gradually opened up investment, foreign capital no longer needed to go through Hong Kong as an intermediary.

After 1997, cross-shareholding became increasingly common among mainland and Hong Kong companies. By the end of 2016, half of Hong Kong's 20 most valuable listed companies had mainland backgrounds — the largest being Tencent. They may raise capital in Hong Kong, but they do not necessarily invest there.

### Good at buying low and selling high, Li Ka-shing turned to new targets

A common verdict on Li Ka-shing's business acumen: he not only knows when to buy, but even more knows when to sell.

For example, [Woody Wu](https://www.wsj.com/articles/li-ka-shings-moves-in-china-reveal-good-timing-1441567980), a professor of accounting at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, said: "Li Ka-shing is a master of timing when it comes to selling. He always manages to sell at the best possible price — financially, he's a genius."

From the Cultural Revolution, the handover negotiations, the repeated upheavals before reform and opening, the 1997 handover and the Asian financial crisis, the Hong Kong market's confidence in the future had swung several times. Li always used the downturns to snap up what he favored at low prices — whether property or companies.

But now he started selling.

By QDaily's count, over six years Li Ka-shing sold at least HK$250 billion of mainland and Hong Kong assets. In 2017 alone he sold roughly HK$90 billion of assets.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319124613zlai0Q9Apj1nr2Tu.jpg-w600)

In 2015, Li Ka-shing stepped up investment in Europe, especially Britain. About a quarter of Britain's electricity distribution market, nearly 30% of its natural gas supply market, and nearly 7% of its water supply market belong to Li Ka-shing. Li said: "We understand the situation Europe is in, but I believe that within five years, the businesses here will have upward momentum."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319083723MK71qpF6CHvB3yoL.jpeg-w600)

Also in 2015, Cheung Kong Holdings and Hutchison Whampoa — both chaired by Li Ka-shing — jointly announced a major restructuring plan: the newly formed CK Hutchison and Cheung Kong Property would choose the Cayman Islands as their place of registration, meaning they would in theory no longer belong to Hong Kong. This broke Li Ka-shing's promise not to relocate the companies' registration.

A generation of tycoons leaving the business world adds to people's worries about the uncertainty of Hong Kong's future economy. "What Hong Kong needs most in the future is this: after a generation of tycoons retires and passes away, can Hong Kong produce a new entrepreneurial spirit, new ventures? I think it will be very hard, and hard for a long time," said Professor Joseph Fan, director of the Institute of Economics and Finance at the Chinese University of Hong Kong.
