"I'm a tide rider," he likes to say.

**"To record the actions and everyday lives of ordinary people," Walter Harrington once said — "there is too little of this kind of record in our trade. Such stories record people's actions, motives, feelings, beliefs, attitudes, dissatisfactions, hopes, fears, achievements and longings as they search for meaning and purpose in their lives. They help people understand where they stand in the world."**

**He once wrote a story about a family whose teenage son had committed suicide two years earlier. "I tried to explain to them the kind of story I wanted to write, the depth I wanted to reach. The father listened for a long time, and finally said, 'So you want to know what I'm thinking in my heart when I pray alone in a quiet room.'"**

**In its simplest form, Harrington said, it is this: how do people live, and what values do they hold dear?**

**Each of our lives makes up this world.**

**"2018 Stories" is a new column from QDaily.**

**"I'm a tide rider — and a tide-player, too. All the changes of the 40 years of reform and opening-up can be seen in me."**

**Wang Dong is a man who likes to praise himself after recounting each episode of his life. This sentence may serve as the summary of his whole story. He is 70 years old exactly, from Leping, near Jingdezhen in Jiangxi Province, and settled in Shanghai in the 1990s. He stands 1.85 meters tall but doesn't stoop, slicks his hair back, dislikes logos but trusts name brands.**

**Apart from getting up at six o'clock every morning, Wang Dong's daily routine hardly resembles that of a 70-year-old: three generations of his family are under one roof, yet he barely involves himself in raising or educating the third generation; two apartments in downtown Shanghai, totaling more than 200 square meters, are set aside for his son and daughter respectively; he himself bought a new 150-square-meter apartment next to the Kunshan high-speed rail station and lives there alone, while his wife and children stay in Shanghai.**

**He has a porcelain business, but it is only a side pursuit. The industrial company he founded in 2007 is still running, and every day he spends three hours on high-speed rail and subway transfers commuting between his home in Kunshan and his office in Hongkou, Shanghai. Occasionally he drives another 50 kilometers to a warehouse he keeps in Nanhui, Shanghai, to deal with shipments or product quality issues. What time remains goes to the porcelain shop — no more than ten hours a week in total.**

![The porcelain showroom. Photo / Gong Fangyi, QDaily](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223213140NdezfrwnUC7.JPG-w600)

**Wang Dong originally rented a 3,000-square-meter warehouse in Nanhui, with storage on the ground floor and offices upstairs. He then sublet the entire ground-floor warehouse to a European port machinery company as its off-site warehouse outside the bonded zone. In good times, the rent alone brought in nearly 40,000 yuan a month in gross margin, and with other storage fees and tire-mounting income, annual operating profit could exceed one million yuan.**

**Even when falling orders from the upstream port machinery client ate into Wang Dong's mounting and goods-handling income, a fixed site-rent payment still landed in his account every month without fail. For Wang Dong, who employed only three workers, there was still room for 300,000 yuan a year in profit.**

**But because the industrial park where the warehouse sat failed its environmental review last year, all 12 factories in the park had to move out by New Year's Day this year — Wang Dong's included. He says he does no production or processing, just manual work mounting and storing tires, and can't see what that has to do with the environment.**

![The 3,000-square-meter warehouse Wang Dong originally rented in Nanhui. The ground floor was the warehouse, the second floor the office. The entire park has now been completely vacated. Photo / Gong Fangyi, QDaily](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223212251BgF9j5VHDpZ2seP4.JPG-w600)

![The 3,000-square-meter warehouse Wang Dong originally rented in Nanhui. The ground floor was the warehouse, the second floor the office. The entire park has now been completely vacated. Photo / Gong Fangyi, QDaily](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223220509cgEw9WIFYvhmZb2i.JPG-w600)

**On November 25, Wang Dong received word that the Shanghai Hongqiao Friendship Store, where his porcelain shop was located, would soon close, and he needed to find a new site as quickly as possible. Wang Dong, who had planned to expand the porcelain business, actually already had several candidate locations, including Takashimaya and the LV Tower, both also in the Hongqiao business district.**

**But the vacate-and-move-out notice issued the very next day upended his plans. At four o'clock on the afternoon of the 26th, the mall's manager went from door to door bowing in apology. What Wang Dong cared about was whether he could claim the nearly 40,000 yuan he had spent fitting out the shop. He grumbled that when he signed the lease at the end of August and finished the fit-out and opened for business at the end of September, nobody told him the mall would close this soon.**

**What made him more anxious was that the mall had given only three days to clear out; November 30 would be the last day. Everything in Wang Dong's shop was fragile, and packing and storing it would be no easy task.**

**"I've jumped into another small fire pit," Wang Dong said on the phone.**

**Also terminated was the storage contract between the foreign port machinery company and Wang Dong. Losing this business — income guaranteed rain or shine — was the real pit, in Wang Dong's words.**

**Around 2015, domestic heavy-industry groups, including ZPMC, began taking the majority share of the domestic port machinery market, squeezing the room for European and American giants such as Krupp and Cargotec to operate — including the company that partnered with Wang Dong. As that company's orders declined year over year in 2016, it stopped managing the global supply chain centrally and delegated some decision-making power to regional companies; the newly appointed head of the China region was the first to pull the tire-mounting business back in-house. Next came cutting out raw-material middlemen, with tires and wheel rims procured by the group's own purchasing arm through market price comparisons rather than being handed to middlemen like Wang Dong.**

**The final step was not renewing third-party warehouses when their leases expired. Wang Dong complained that he received not a cent of compensation for the 370,000 yuan he had spent fitting out the warehouse, and that he also lost the 11 days' rent not yet fulfilled under the storage contract.**

**Now Wang Dong's industrial business is down to a single tire-mounting order from a heavy-industry group in Wuxi. He found a warehouse some 400 to 500 meters east of his old one, built by local residents on their homestead plots — 280 square meters, at a monthly rent of 5,000 yuan. The Wuxi client will only pay a mounting fee of 20 yuan per tire, one-nineteenth of Wang Dong's previous quoted price. At roughly 240 mounted tires a month, Wang Dong's monthly income is 4,800 yuan.**

![The warehouse Wang Dong now rents, with a total area of less than 300 square meters. Photo / Gong Fangyi, QDaily](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223215315KfYByruHtqFlmC5s.JPG-w600)

**"At this rate I couldn't even cover one person's wages, let alone the storage fees," he said. "The Wuxi client also demands that if a single mounting run reaches 80 tires, I have to deliver them, and I pay the freight. One delivery run costs 1,500 yuan, which adds another 12.5 yuan per tire — what money am I making?" With the first three quarters over, Wang Dong posted a loss for the first time in nearly a decade — a six-figure loss.**

**Back when business was good, around 2013 and 2014, Wang Dong, who had already accumulated more than five million yuan in capital, wanted to borrow ten million yuan from a bank to expand. After providing the required upstream and downstream contracts and bank statements, he never got the call approving the loan. "The bank went back and forth reviewing it and never told me outright whether it was approved or rejected — they just said it hadn't come through yet. After a few rounds I got the picture: this is too hard for small and medium-sized businesses like ours. Later I stopped thinking about it."**

**At the end of last year he considered shutting down the company, but couldn't figure out what he would do with idle time. If he kept doing business, whatever it was, at least he would still have the chance to deal with people. On a trip back to Jiangxi to visit family in 2017, his half-brother — same mother, different father — floated the idea of going into the porcelain business together. When his industrial business collapsed "off a cliff" this year, he finally decided to invest 200,000 yuan and open a porcelain shop "for fun."**

**The following is Wang Dong's own account:**

I was born in 1948 and only went to school for five years — I never even finished elementary school. By today's standards, that makes me illiterate. I blame that mostly on the times. My family was poor too; I was the sixth of seven siblings. My father died before I finished elementary school, and afterward my mother remarried, which is how I gained two half-brothers.

When I officially started working in 1961, I was only 14. The city assigned me by age to the Jingdezhen Yue Opera Troupe as a props apprentice. The national economy was very underdeveloped then, and folk performing arts were all on the decline; the troupe could barely survive. I asked for a transfer, and though times were hard, the state still found you a job. I was assigned to the Jingdezhen Administration for Industry and Commerce as a courier. Telecommunications were undeveloped back then; when companies exchanged documents, the couriers would carry the files to the gate of the municipal party committee and lay them out in a V shape like street vendors, and each company's courier would go from "stall" to "stall" like shoppers at a fair, hunting for their own documents.

My work equipment was a bicycle from my work unit. Owning a bicycle was a happy thing in those days. And I was excellent — the fastest cyclist among more than a hundred couriers.

**But Wang Dong stuck with the job for only a year. He was dissatisfied with the monthly wage of 18 yuan — the standard rate set for apprentices at the time. He applied to the Jingdezhen labor bureau for a transfer, and was instead assigned to the Jingdezhen porcelain factory to press saggars — a trade found only in the ceramics system. Porcelain used to require saggars for firing: craftsmen first placed the porcelain inside a saggar, then placed the saggar into the kiln. Because saggars came in different sizes, and different sizes carried different grain rations, Wang Dong tried to press the size that would earn him 42 jin (21 kilograms) of rice a month.**

![Saggars. Porcelain once required saggars for firing: craftsmen first placed the porcelain inside a saggar, then placed the saggar into the kiln. Photo / Alibaba seller](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223212811RG0aMbtL8OKryuHs.jpg-w600)

**The decade of the Cultural Revolution and the "[One Strike, Three Anti campaign](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%B8%80%E6%89%93%E4%B8%89%E5%8F%8D%E9%81%8B%E5%8B%95)" that followed affected Wang Dong as well. "One Strike, Three Anti" was a political movement of the era: it struck at "counter-revolutionary sabotage" and opposed "embezzlement and theft," "speculation and profiteering," and "extravagance and waste." Dissatisfied with the labor bureau's decision to assign him to the Nanchang Sewing Machinery Factory, he took to the streets to protest with a few fellow workers — and was punished by being sent to dig coal at the Yongshan coal mine in Leping.**

**[That coal mine is still operating today](http://jx.ifeng.com/jdz/detail_2015_03/03/3605560_0.shtml). In 2015 an accident there killed four people.**

The fall was too great, you know? I had been a model worker in Jingdezhen — the fastest in the workshop at pressing saggars, and able to do the most types of work, like clay splitting. And then, all of a sudden, I was down in the mines.

I was in terrible spirits and took sick leave constantly. There were none of today's scientific diagnostic methods; you'd go to the hospital and say some part of you hurt, and the doctor couldn't confirm anything anyway. Getting a sick-leave note this way meant no pay was docked. When they told me to do transport work, I refused.

But I still had a laborer's head for getting rich. Even without going to work, I could earn money.

I loved photography — to this day I can recite the formulas for developing D72 and D76 film. When I wasn't working, I'd put on an army cap, wear a pair of plain-glass spectacles, sling a Shanghai-brand camera over my shoulder, dress up in disguise, and go to the countryside to take photos for farmers. But I didn't dare take cash directly — one transaction like that and it was speculation and profiteering.

So I asked for eggs instead: six eggs per photo. The farmers had no money anyway, and they were delighted to trade eggs for photos. When I had too many eggs, I'd invite other people to eat them; the people who came to eat couldn't believe it. In the end I really did end up with far too many eggs, and by the time I'd finished treating everyone it was time to sell the rest. See — trading photos for eggs, and eggs for money: this way it didn't count as speculation and profiteering.

Back then I went down to the countryside once a week, spending half a day taking photos for eggs and half a day selling eggs. Well-off local farmers who had money but no chickens would come buy them too. I sold each egg for one mao — 0.1 yuan — and in the end it worked out to about 20 yuan a day, equivalent to half a month's wages (38.87 yuan a month). When I got married in 1995, my finances were actually in poor shape; the few hundred yuan I spent on the wedding was what I had saved up from photography.

It was on the first anniversary of Mao Zedong's death — September 9, 1977 — that I started working in purchasing. I remember that date too well.

**Perhaps through sheer luck, Wang Dong was never punished by the mine for taking leave and going to the countryside to take photos; after being transferred above ground, he even succeeded in getting transferred to the Jingdezhen Porcelain Chemical Factory as a general purchasing agent. It was work with porcelain again. A "general" purchasing agent meant buying whatever the factory lacked — lead, rhodium powder, and so on.**

Rhodium powder — you know it? I suspect it's worth more than gold even now. To buy it, I entered a vault dedicated to storing precious metals, somewhere near Beijing's Sanlitun I think, with layer upon layer of doors, under the Reserve Bureau of the State Materials Bureau. Unlike gold, which was under state monopoly purchase and sale, it had no such restriction and could be stockpiled anywhere, so I traveled around accordingly.

The state had already declared it would vigorously support the development of light industry, including our Jingdezhen ceramics. Without rhodium powder you couldn't fire porcelain, so on that basis it wasn't too hard to buy — other goods were another story. It was a challenging job: China suffered material scarcity, and most of the raw materials you needed to buy were in short supply. But the factory needed them urgently, so sometimes I had to beg people. Maybe that's why to this day I'm so courteous to everyone.

I didn't have much schooling, but I knew Beijing was China's cultural, political and economic center, and to get purchasing done you had to run around Beijing. Walking, bus, subway — you just ran everywhere, and I did the job with tremendous flourish.

Around 1982, I pulled off something remarkable. The Porcelain Chemical Factory wanted to import an electronic color scanner for ceramic plate making, but state regulations allowed each province to import only one, and Jiangxi's quota had been used by Nanchang. I was the first purchasing agent in China to import a second electronic color scanner for a province. You should know that even the People's Printing Houses of Beijing and Shanghai each imported only one.

The purchase application had to go to the Ministry of Light Industry in the name of technical renovation. The general manager of the ceramics company took people to the ministry and then reported to Zhu Rongji, and I attended that meeting as well. Zhu Rongji approved 1.44 million yuan — my feeling at the time was that I had asked for too little, and Zhu Rongji approved it with a smile on the spot. Zhu Rongji was director of the Technical Renovation Bureau of the State Economic Commission, while Jingdezhen porcelain fell under the Ministry of Light Industry.

I was excellent, let me tell you. Although Zhu Rongji approved a procurement quota of 1.44 million yuan for this order, the whole process still had to involve the Ministry of Light Industry, the State Import-Export Commission, the State Instrumentation Bureau, the State Economic Commission, the State Planning Commission and the Ministry of Foreign Trade — and I collected six seals around Tiananmen in nine days.

I had photocopied the paper bearing those six seals, but I can't find it now.

This was not long after the state had brought down the Gang of Four, and party and government conduct were very good — they got things done. I also used a few small tricks. For instance, after I finished my business at the Import-Export Commission, they would tell me where to go next — say, the Planning Commission — and I would ask the Import-Export Commission people to help phone ahead to their counterparts at the Planning Commission. The ministries were all connected to each other, so with advance greetings laid down, my work went more smoothly.

![A porcelain market in Jingdezhen. Photo / [Zhang Banggai](http://jingdezhen.h.baike.com/article-119877.html)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223213925KaDFfSZzUkedTVmX.jpg-w600)

**Though Wang Dong recounts these experiences with great animation, the vast majority of them can no longer be verified. In fact, the ability to apply for a second electronic color scanner at the time also rode the tailwind of the state's [efforts to revive light industry, including printing](http://www2.pccu.edu.tw/chineseprint/pdf/ch19.pdf). And the claim that "each province could import only one" is open to question: according to [the records of the Shanghai Gazetteer of Foreign Trade and Economic Cooperation](http://webcache.googleusercontent.com/search?q=cache:wWp6Q7kjjiAJ:www.shtong.gov.cn/node2/node2245/node74728/node74735/node74899/node74903/userobject1ai89755.html+&cd=14&hl=en&ct=clnk&gl=vn), eight electronic color scanners were imported between 1979 and 1990.**

**After enough trips to Beijing, Wang Dong persuaded the company's party committee, on the grounds that "purchasing requires knowing high-level people," to raise the accommodation allowance for Beijing business trips from 3–5 yuan a day to 5–10 yuan a day, saying it gave him the chance to stay in star hotels such as the Heping Hotel, the Beijing Hotel, the Friendship Hotel and the Xin Qiao Hotel.**

When I worked in purchasing, I made one extra demand of the factory: the accommodation allowance had to be higher. Only then would I have more chances to meet people of a higher caliber. Our purchasing agents' accommodation allowance for Beijing trips had originally been three yuan, but after my request the party committee actually agreed to raise it to five to ten yuan. At ten yuan I could stay at the Xin Qiao Hotel, which served foreign guests.

After many trips to Beijing and some familiarity with the place, officials from Jingdezhen or the province would ask me for help with everything — food, drink, lodging — when they came to the capital. I had very good relations with several governors of Jiangxi Province, which would count as a marvel for any Chinese purchasing agent in any era. Around 1985 I made deputy section chief at the Porcelain Chemical Factory, though I felt my official career had probably reached its ceiling, since I could neither rise nor fall.

There were various reasons. Maybe others were jealous of talent; maybe it had something to do with my limited schooling. People at the factory knew I had a complicated web of social connections. Another reason was probably that I had indeed gotten a bit carried away and committed a taboo. After the electronic color scanner purchase was approved, the factory sent me to operate it in Japan. Before departure I had a conversation with our factory's party secretary that I remember very clearly: I'm off to Japan — thank you to the organization — from now on I feel like I'm on my way up.

That was the gist of it. And I was never promoted again.

So what was the point of staying? I submitted a request to transfer to the sales department of the Jingdezhen Ceramics Company, and wound up selling porcelain at the National Tourism Porcelain Office instead. My colleagues there all said I was an oddball, because the others sold goods at an eight-percent markup while the deals I handled returned over 100% profit.

You need a nimble mind for this work. The sales department's usual practice had been to supply goods to small-commodity markets and department stores everywhere; the profit margin was the eight points set by the state, and the products became wholesale porcelain. I wouldn't do it that way — I sold it as tourist porcelain instead, and it just so happened that the extra contacts I'd made in purchasing now came in handy. I deliberately sold to Friendship Stores and star hotels.

But then it was the same old problem: I couldn't climb. I did deliver results, right? The people above wanted to promote me, but someone said I had no education and couldn't be promoted. Another objection held that I did the job fine but was hard to control — too mobile, with too much capacity for action.

When I told people around me I planned to leave, nobody tried to keep me. During the 1985 New Year, the manager of the chemicals company under the Jingdezhen Materials Bureau came to my home to pay holiday respects and asked me to run supply and sales for the chemicals and light-industry company under the bureau. The salary went from under 60 yuan a month to over 100 yuan, so I went.

**In the planned-economy era, China ran its raw-material purchasing and supply under a dual-track system: materials within the plan were priced uniformly nationwide, while those outside the plan were market-priced. Wang Dong says he excelled at arbitrage using foreign exchange quotas and the dual-track system. The reason for all this elaborate maneuvering was that buying raw materials with foreign exchange quotas counted as quota control outside the plan, while the purchase price still referenced the unified state price inside the plan — but when it came time to sell, every contract was executed at the market price outside the plan.**

Some companies couldn't secure material supplies; some places had no idea what a foreign exchange quota even was. My advantage was that I had connections on both sides — the foreign exchange administration and the materials bureau. For steel, for instance, I bought from the metals company under the materials bureau, which had also been my main supplier of rhodium powder. And while I had never done a caustic soda deal before, I placed a single 300-ton order, and when I asked around, people said, "Wang Dong, this stuff you can sell to the Zhejiang Ningbo Chemical Fiber Company."

Business has to be done in hundreds of tons at a time.

The materials bureau helped me for a reason too. Because I was on good terms with the foreign exchange administration, I had once interceded to solve an eight-figure foreign exchange quota approval problem for the bureau. But I was very careful in how I did things — that was also the administration's requirement of me. One official there told me: "Cuz, walk inside the lines — don't step outside them." Walking inside the lines meant don't break the rules or the law.

This way, I effectively solved the state's foreign exchange problem for the national materials bureau as a man of the Jingdezhen Materials Bureau — tell me, wasn't that something? Because I made a lot of money for the materials bureau, higher-ups sent people to investigate me, and I left in a huff. I'm a man who cares about face.

When I left, I was chief of the general section of the chemicals and light-industry company. Before I arrived, the company couldn't even complete the sales quotas the state had assigned it; by the time I left, it had become one of Jingdezhen's best-performing work units.

**As with his previous jobs, Wang Dong didn't stay long at the materials bureau — just two years. The difference was that this time he gave up his seniority and went into business for good.**

**After the launch of reform and opening-up in 1978 and the establishment of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone by the National People's Congress in 1980, provinces across China successively set up liaison offices in Shenzhen to better attract investment and conduct external exchange. A considerable share of these Shenzhen offices ran parallel government and commercial teams. Huagan Company was the other signboard of the Jiangxi Provincial Government's Shenzhen Liaison Office, funded by the provincial government; apart from the company's top leader and a handful of managers, nobody else held an establishment position — including Wang Dong, who gave up his seniority and went south to Shenzhen in 1989.**

**Huagan Company paid Wang Dong a monthly salary of more than 1,000 yuan — ten times what he had earned at the chemicals and light-industry company under the Jingdezhen Materials Bureau, and more than six times the national average wage at the time. The profit target Huagan set for him was no less steep: one million yuan a year.**

![A propaganda slogan outside the Shekou Industrial Zone in Shenzhen, 1979. Photo / [Shenzhen News Network](http://www.sznews.com/news/content/2018-11/22/content_21231884.htm)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223214635aoC49PbKfRjiz2Dt.png-w600)

When I left the materials bureau, I was earning a little over 100 yuan a month; at Huagan Company it became over 1,000 yuan a month. I'm a man who cares about face — when the materials bureau wouldn't release me to Huagan, I simply gave up my seniority, and just like that, I had gone into business.

Although Huagan belonged to the Jiangxi State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, it was responsible for its own profits and losses; apart from the party and government top leaders, nobody else was inside the system. The company had good conditions, with its own office building.

I have a classic door-opener story from there. In the early 1990s I got in with a large, newly established Shanghai chemical group — I made it onto their supplier list. The price of entry was a 20-ton rubber order, sold at a loss of 500 yuan a ton — 10,000 yuan. My boss was furious: making money in business is real skill; anyone can lose money.

He was right that I had lost money, so I didn't talk back. But whether judged then or in hindsight, the price was worth it. Because the other side lacked nothing — money, foreign exchange quotas, market — except raw materials; the raw-material quotas under the planned economy fell far short of their needs. So whether it was carbon black, industrial tire cord fabric, rubber or any other material, basically as long as I could find the supply at a suitable price, they needed it.

In my second year at Huagan I proposed a contract system. The money was too easy to make.

**"I was too excellent, let me tell you. In my second year at Huagan I took on a contract." Every time Wang Dong finishes recounting a stretch of his work history, he praises himself like this. But a trading partner who worked with him for many years told us: "Lao Wang has a real head for business and tremendous drive — that's true. But the reason he could get into so many companies and build relationships with bureau- and ministry-level leaders is that the signboard behind him played a big part. At least when we agreed to let him supply us, a very important reason was that Huagan Company had the backing of the Jiangxi provincial government."**

**His eight years at Huagan Company left Wang Dong with nearly ten million yuan in assets. On the eve of Hong Kong's return to China in 1997, he fell out with Huagan Company over personal income tax and promptly resigned, returning to Jingdezhen. "At the time I thought, I've made so much money — I can make money anywhere, right? So I left and went back to Jingdezhen."**

**Fully leaving a company and going it alone turned out to be anything but smooth for Wang Dong. Right after returning to Jingdezhen, he spent 800,000 yuan on a fully imported Infiniti, and over the next five years its repair bills cost as much as the car. Two million yuan he had lent out vanished; of three apartments he bought in Shanghai for four million yuan, he kept two and gave one away; he invested two million yuan in four photo studios and print shops in Shanghai, and only one survived.** **By 2007, Wang Dong had just 500,000 yuan left in cash. That was when he founded the industrial company he runs today. Some of his friends didn't understand it: what was a man nearly 60 still hustling for? Wang Dong said it was because he couldn't stand being idle. The gradual draining of his savings was another reason. "My thinking is this: the purpose of making money is to spend it with panache."**

![Wang Dong's Infiniti, on which he spent 800,000 yuan on repairs. The car has not been scrapped and still carries Jingdezhen license plates. Photo / Gong Fangyi, QDaily](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2018122321490103flS25qGdcYyrOa.JPG-w600)

On September 6, 2007, I registered the company I run today. Through an acquaintance's introduction, I partnered with a port machinery company headquartered in Europe. The initial idea was to return to my old trade and supply them with things like OTR tires. But they weren't planning to change tire suppliers for the time being, so they asked whether I knew how to mount tires — I could start with that. OTR tires, like most vehicle wheels, consist of a tire and a wheel rim, purchased and stored separately, and only assembled when needed for use.

The economic crisis had just passed, and domestic imports and exports were insanely busy; that port machinery company's warehouses in Waigaoqiao weren't big enough. After we'd worked together for a while, they asked if I could find a warehouse outside the bonded zone — and if so, they'd rent from me. Of course I said yes; I asked in the morning and had found one by the afternoon, in Waigaoqiao, 1,000 square meters, and later moved up to 1,400, then 3,000 square meters.

The warehouse rent margin was excellent — let me do the math for you: cost was 0.42 yuan per square meter per day, and I rented it out at 0.85 yuan per square meter per day, the full 3,000 square meters on a whole-lease. Mounting was 380 yuan per tire: in the busy season we'd mount 20–30 a day, and even in the slow season 10–20, averaging 4,000 tires a year, with roughly 150 yuan net profit per tire — so mounting alone brought in 600,000 yuan a year.

Storage fees added another 400,000-plus a year. Then anything else — goods moving in or out, one nudge of the forklift — I charged for: 25 yuan per ton inbound, 20 yuan per ton outbound, and 200 yuan per forklift move. In good months the storage fees alone could reach 150,000 to 160,000 yuan. Business has its ups and downs. Around 2015, the orders I got from them started to shrink. My own judgment was that domestic port machinery makers were cheaper and their quality had improved too. For a comparable machine, the import sold for four million yuan while the domestic one went for 1.5 to two million — half the price right there. And the performance gap between domestic and imported was maybe 20 to 30 percent, so foreign companies' markets were steadily taken over by domestic ones.

But the advantage of working with them was this: whatever happened to their business — even if they gave me no orders at all — they still paid the warehouse rent, which meant I had a guaranteed 100,000-plus yuan a year, rain or shine.

![The small chalkboard left inside Wang Dong's warehouse, which originally recorded the number of tires mounted each day. Photo / Gong Fangyi, QDaily](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223214747os16mX4ESkqgLdT8.JPG-w600)

![The ramp outside the big Nanhui warehouse, used for loading and unloading containers. Photo / Gong Fangyi, QDaily](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181223215143mSTI2aOUAHFxl9RD.JPG-w600)

**"But costs have risen too fast these past few years, and margins are getting thinner and thinner." The rising costs in Wang Dong's telling include personnel and welfare, taxes, and storage costs. Still, his employees are somewhat dissatisfied with Wang Dong's pay standards.**

**Tang Lei has worked for Wang Dong since 2008. In his first year, after 400 yuan in social insurance was deducted, his take-home pay was 1,600 yuan a month. Ten years later, Tang Lei says he still takes home only 3,000 yuan. Counting social insurance and housing provident fund, his monthly pay comes to a little over 4,000 yuan — equivalent to a raise of 200 yuan a year. The only reason Tang Lei stays is that the working hours are flexible, making it convenient to pick up and drop off his daughter at school.**

**Wang Dong doesn't see it that way. He believes that by paying the employee-share of Tang Lei's 400 yuan social insurance, the company has already made a concession. As for the size of the raises, he shot back: "If I raise a worker's wages, can I raise his output? Raise them and he'll immediately threaten to leave. His wages have gone up all these years, and I haven't raised his fixed mounting quota either. We've already compromised."**

**Last year's nationwide 1-percentage-point cut in the value-added tax rate also made him feel shortchanged.**

**The sales contracts between Wang Dong and his European client are executed at pre-tax prices, while his purchases of raw materials from domestic companies are executed at after-tax prices. Take the same item priced at 10,000 yuan pre-tax: Wang Dong buys at the original price and sells at the original price; at the 17% VAT rate, both his purchase and sale prices are 11,700 yuan. When the rate fell to 16%, his sale price became 11,600 yuan, while the domestic company still sold to him at 11,700 yuan. Wang Dong says he lost one percentage point of margin.**

**With his business shrinking drastically, Wang Dong gradually handed the industrial company over to his son and daughter to run, spent several months riding high-speed rail across half of China, and took up selling porcelain as well.**

**But he still can't let go of that business.**

**In mid-November, Wang Dong returned to Jingdezhen to meet an old friend, who is also a potential partner for his porcelain business. Approaching his lodgings in Jingdezhen, it was nearly 12:30 a.m. His daughter sent him three WeChat messages in a row, asking how to respond to the Wuxi client's demand that they lower tire prices.**

**That client is his only one left.**

**Wang Dong put on his reading glasses, pulled up the handwriting input on his phone, wrote and deleted, deleted and wrote. By the time he got out of the car, that reply had still not been sent.**

At the interviewee's request, Wang Dong is a pseudonym.
