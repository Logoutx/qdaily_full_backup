@@QD_BODY@@
They were born in the '90s. They never lived through the Soviet period, and the internet has shaped them deeply. These are the two labels that define them most. What do they think about who should be president?

This is a family of four. The mother, a journalist who comes from the Soviet era, plans to vote in this presidential election for Pavel Grudinin, the candidate put forward by the Communist Party of Russia; the father, also from the Soviet era, has retired from his job as an engineer, and he too supports Grudinin; the older sister, Varya, 28, has no interest in politics and knows the candidates only through her mother — she figures her mother's profession gives her access to more of the truth; the younger brother, Dmitry, born in 1995, currently supports a man named Alexei Navalny.

On March 18, 2018, Russia will hold its presidential election. We started with the question "Why is Navalny so popular with young people?" Although the Russian Central Election Commission barred Navalny from the list of presidential candidates on December 25, 2017, on grounds that he did not qualify, he quickly called on voters to boycott the presidential election and stay away from the polls — a call that won support from many young people.

Navalny is a lawyer. Beginning in 2008 he actively used his blog to expose corruption in the Russian government, and in 2011 he founded the Anti-Corruption Foundation. He is one of Russia's fastest-rising and best-known internet celebrities.

![Alexei Navalny](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226002233LdpAXqz9OVfoiSxB.jpg-w600)

Our question soon became: what are Russia's young people thinking — those too young to have experienced the collapse of the Soviet empire in 1991, what attitudes do they hold toward politics and toward history? They live in the internet age just like young people elsewhere — so what makes them different?

This family came to our attention because of Varya. Varya is currently pursuing a PhD in Beijing and is fluent in Chinese. Besides studying, she works part-time at a friend's travel company, and during winter and summer vacations she also works as a tour guide in Russia. "Anything that makes money, I'll do," she says.

Compared with her indifference to politics, Varya cares more about work. "Young people in Russia are under a lot of pressure — you have to support yourself, and after graduation there's the question of buying a home," she adds. Russian parents make their children fend for themselves once they reach adulthood, so many young people start working while still at university. She herself started working in her fourth year; her brother Dmitry started in his third.

As for Dmitry being the only one in the family who supports the opposition leader Navalny, Varya says she doesn't really understand Navalny, but she thinks calling on voters not to vote is a "selfish" act. Rather than do nothing, she believes there are other ways to make one's views known to the state.

Still, Navalny's appeal to voters won support from some young people. Besides Dmitry, Nastya, born in 1996 and currently living in Moscow, has made up her mind: she will not vote in this year's election.

### "Another Six Years? No! Thanks"

On January 28, 2018, Dmitry stood protesting in Moscow's Pushkin Square, at the junction of Tverskaya Street and the Moscow Boulevard Ring, just two kilometers from the Kremlin, the center of Russian power.

Dmitry, 23, works as a high school math teacher in Moscow. That day the temperature was only minus four degrees, and around him stood many young people his own age, wearing overcoats and holding signs. [The sign held by one 20-year-old read](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/49680.html): "Another six years? No! Thanks."

This was a protest action in the name of boycotting the presidential election, and the man who launched it was Navalny.

![The January 28 protest](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226002436jfNech5DVYWOi7qx.png-w600)

Dmitry, who has long followed Navalny, describes him with the word "down-to-earth": "He's not a billionaire — he lives with his family in the suburbs of Moscow, he uses the internet, he makes videos, and most importantly, he understands memes." These, he feels, are the things that set Navalny furthest apart from traditional politicians.

Dmitry often reads the reports on government corruption that Navalny posts on his blog. He believes these truths, covered up by the authorities, hold Russia back. "Navalny is the only opponent today who can mount a protest movement and compete in federal elections. In the 2013 Moscow mayoral election, he finished second in vote share."

Navalny, born in 1976, earned a law degree at the People's Friendship University of Russia in his youth, then joined the Russian United Democratic Party, but was later expelled over ideological differences. In 2008, Navalny began working as a lawyer while actively writing blog posts and contributing to media outlets such as *Forbes Russia* and *The Moscow Times*.

Both his blog posts and his media articles centered almost entirely on the truth about government corruption. His investigative reports drew wide attention. One, exposing corruption at Rosneft and other state-owned oil and gas companies, made him famous overnight. In the piece, Navalny pointed out that officials at Russian state pipeline company Transneft skimmed US$4 billion from a pipeline project running from Russia to Asian countries, setting up shell companies specifically to contract work on this more than 3,000-mile-long pipeline project for the state pipeline company.

These reports circulated widely, especially on social networks. Dmitry says he first read these corruption reports online eight years ago, when he was 15, and has followed Navalny's activities regularly ever since.

![Navalny regularly posts reports on Russian corruption on YouTube (Image / [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qrwlk7_GF9g&t=16s))](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226002513eBTJb5FH26aPl7q0.png-w600)

As the number of Navalny's investigative reports grew, more people took notice of him. In 2013, Navalny ran in the Moscow mayoral election for the first time and, unexpectedly, became the candidate with the second-highest vote share. But his political career soon hit one twist after another.

[In 2014, Navalny wrote in a contribution to *The New York Times*](https://cn.nytimes.com/opinion/20140324/c24navalny/): "Last September I ran for mayor of Moscow as a pro-reform, pro-democracy opposition candidate. Despite being barred from the state-run media, I still won nearly a third of the vote. After I called for friendly relations with Ukraine and adherence to international law, my blog was shut down today on charges of 'extremism.' Until recently, that blog had more than 2 million visitors a month."

On March 2, 2017, [in a YouTube video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qrwlk7_GF9g&t=16s), Navalny publicly released a video about the vast wealth held by Russia's then-prime minister Dmitry Medvedev; it has been viewed 26.37 million times.

His numbers on social networks indirectly confirm Navalny's influence on the public. But more often, he is simply seen as "Putin's rival."

[*The New York Times* once offered this analysis of the Moscow mayoral result](https://cn.nytimes.com/opinion/20130830/c30geshen/): "This was a referendum on Russia's future, a unique chance to say 'no' to Putin's continued rule by saying 'yes' to one of Putin's most outspoken and best-known opponents."

![Navalny is supported by young people at protest sites](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226010134XfCeEzJax21iUTHN.jpg-w600)

Nastya also admits that she decided to boycott this year's election simply because she is unhappy with Putin — she doesn't genuinely support Navalny at all. "The Putin government is fairly corrupt, which is a big flaw. Apart from the two capitals, all the other cities are poor."

Svetlana, born in 1994, studied international relations at a Russian university and does not shy away from political topics. On the contrary, she follows the direction of the Russian government very closely. She believes that after so many years in power, Putin's priorities have long since shifted: "Now he cares more about his own power and about how the international community judges him. Since the Crimea crisis we've had serious economic problems, but Putin doesn't pay much attention to this — he just focuses on his own power."

Dmitry's distrust of the Putin government shows in his repeated participation in protest actions. Still, at this January's demonstration he was suddenly struck by a sense of disappointment he had never felt before: "That day I felt very let down. The main reason was that the crowd was noticeably smaller than at the last big protest in 2012. For a big city like Moscow, 5,000 people is a very small turnout." He thinks young people may be unwilling to stand up because they increasingly no longer believe change is possible.

### "We Don't Really See What Could Change Things"

*Forbes* magazine named Putin the world's most powerful person for three consecutive years, commenting: "Putin continues to prove that he is one of the few people in the world strong enough to do as he pleases, regardless of the consequences."

[Last November, the Levada Center, a Russian polling organization, conducted a survey in cities and towns across the country](https://www.levada.ru/en/2017/12/11/vladimir-putin-s-work/), sampling 1,600 people aged 18 and over in 137 regions. The topic: views on Putin's governance.

On the question "What do you like most about Putin," 19% of respondents said he is "a real man," with traits like strength, calm, and courage — the single most popular answer; another 18% endorsed Putin's foreign policy, believing his defiance of the West has made Russia respected again; and 16% said he is a strong statesman.

As for "What do you like least about Putin," 20% of respondents answered that they simply like Putin and he has no flaws; 16% said Putin simply doesn't care whether ordinary people's lives go well or badly; 10% felt he is excessively "soft" on those close to him, keeping them in high office indefinitely — and "he hasn't even fired Medvedev yet." But interestingly, the single most common answer of all was "this is just too hard to answer," from 33% of respondents.

[On February 8, Russia's Central Election Commission released the list of eight candidates for this year's presidential election](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/50149.html). Incumbent president Putin is candidate No. 4. The commission had previously said that 64 people had applied to run. But since the commission can screen candidates for eligibility, not everyone who files gets onto the ballot.

Eight candidates put forward by different parties should, in theory, make for a fierce campaign, but this year's race is unusually "quiet." The biggest reason is that the seven candidates other than Putin are all, in political strength and popular momentum, essentially far behind him. This shows up in the polls: virtually all of them indicate Putin will win the next presidency smoothly and govern for another six years, having held "real power" in Russia for more than 24 years — making him Russia's longest-serving leader since Stalin (counting his time as prime minister).

Nastya says her friends neither join protests nor pay much attention to the election. "This presidential election is fake, and most young people know it. We all know the next president will be Putin — there's simply no choice. There are still Navalny or Ksenia Sobchak, but they don't stand any chance."

![Putin's approval rating (Image / Levada Center)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226010056cwe53EZDKUvigGsL.png-w600)

![Satisfaction with the Russian government — last November, dissatisfaction exceeded satisfaction (Image / Levada Center)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226010108zvHkgs7rNEFAlc4Z.png-w600)

[The All-Russia Public Opinion Research Center (VTsIOM) recently released a survey report](https://wciom.com/index.php?id=61&uid=1498): in January and February this year, Putin's approval rating ranged between 69.9% and 73.8%. For the week of January 29 to February 4, the share of Russians who would vote for Putin was 71.4%.

Notably, [the polling organization Levada Center also conducted a survey last November](https://www.levada.ru/en/2017/11/15/the-russian-presidential-election-2/): the results showed that only 24% of people were certain they would vote in the presidential election, while 34% said they "probably would" or "probably would not."

That is why, as the election nears, the Kremlin has been racking its brains to boost turnout; officials have told the media they hope to win 70% of the vote this year. In the 2012 presidential election, Putin took 63% of the vote on a turnout of 65%.

Varya told QDaily that some organizations in Moscow have recently begun visiting households door to door. They ask residents a series of questions, then remind them to go vote on March 18. Her own family, she said, had just been visited by one of these groups.

In 2011, after the four-year interlude of Dmitry Medvedev's presidency, then-prime minister Putin had a new shot at the presidency and immediately announced his candidacy — only to be dogged by accusations of electoral fraud, sparking enormous social tension.

On December 10 of that year, after Russia's State Duma elections, thousands upon thousands of Russians took to the streets. What started as a few thousand people slowly grew into tens of thousands, and the protests spread from Moscow to several major Russian cities, evolving from protests over fraud into protests against Putin. Since then, scattered protests have broken out every few months.

A child born in the year the Soviet Union dissolved would be turning 20 this year; this might be the first vote of their lives — or perhaps the first protest of their lives.

Svetlana did not join the protests that year, but she remembers what happened clearly. "In the last presidential election, people expressed their dissatisfaction with Putin, but in the end it made no difference, because Putin was confirmed as the winner. We don't really see what could change things."

### A Political Influencer Born of the Internet, and a Putin Who Has No Phone

At 3 p.m. on February 25, Navalny reposted on VK, Russia's homegrown social platform, a popular post about memes, in which he mocked the government's ad urging people to vote as stolen rather than made in-house.

Navalny updates nearly every day across his blog, VK, Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter. Even allowing for some overlap in followers across platforms, his official YouTube channel has 1.68 million subscribers and his Twitter account 2.18 million followers.

![Navalny's latest VK post](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226002719rEWR7fu9NVJ2gAYs.png-w600)

Foreign media have compared Navalny with various other figures — the Chinese writer Han Han, for instance, whose blog was hugely influential, or the Russian oligarch Mikhail Khodorkovsky, who sought to contest the presidency against Putin only to be jailed for tax evasion, or even WikiLeaks founder Julian Assange.

"Navalny's rise is a particularly interesting phenomenon. He became an opposition star and an anti-corruption influencer through the internet. Using an influencer's huge pull on young people, he can cash in his online influence in offline political movements. This is a completely new phenomenon of the internet age — he can rattle the Kremlin's sensitive nerves with a single video," Wu Dada, who runs the long-running "Su'e Zhuanbo" account devoted to Soviet and Russian culture and has studied Russian youth culture for years, told QDaily in an interview.

![Navalny's YouTube channel](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226002736AWfim0HygzPTl5Lt.png-w600)

In January 2017, the World Economic Forum released a world map of social networks. In Russia, although Facebook is not blocked, only 20% of people use it; the local platform VK, by contrast, is more popular with young people there. According to VK's official website, as of February 23 it had 470 million registered users.

VK is Russia's equivalent of Facebook, founded by Pavel Durov, and the platform began gaining popularity in Russia in 2006.

While studying at Saint Petersburg State University, Durov founded Russia's largest student web forum. Later, Durov's friend Vyacheslav Mirilashvili, during his time at Tufts University, got his first look at Facebook, a website created on the neighboring Harvard campus. Durov, Mirilashvili, and another friend then went on to build what is today the most popular social platform in Russia.

In the second year after VK began catching on in Russia, Navalny started a blog and began cultivating his own influence on social platforms.

According to a VTsIOM survey of VK users, 78% of people aged 18 to 24 use VK every day. VK itself also released a report last year: they looked at the habits of 100,000 users aged 14 to 25, and the results showed that 64% of respondents get their news via VK and 62% watch videos on VK every day. Among them, 25% of respondents said they trust online opinion leaders and political celebrities.

Eason, born in 1995, believes the channel through which you get your information affects which candidate you end up supporting. He told QDaily: "My grandmother loves Putin, because at home there's only a TV, and on TV there's only Putin."

Compared with traditional media, Nastya believes information online is closer to the truth: "Young people all go online now, so it's easier to see what's really going on." Dmitry also says he gets his news mainly from the online outlets Meduza and TV Rain.

The ways young people and the older generation get information really are changing. According to a VTsIOM survey, the share of people getting information from print news (newspapers, magazines) fell from 77% in 2014 to 55% in 2017; online media went from 58% in 2014 to 54% in 2017. The overall numbers look like a decline, but among 18- to 24-year-olds the share is 70%.

![Navalny takes a selfie with netizens](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226004518C1U8kQZXsYlL2ixD.png-w600)

In fact, part of the reason Navalny stays so active on social platforms has to do with the Russian government's tight control of the national media. News about him rarely appears in traditional media — even when he is the one who has launched a protest of tens of thousands of people.

[In July 2017, when Putin appeared on the TV program *Non-Children's Talk Show* to take questions from young people](http://tsrus.cn/shizheng/2017/07/26/658433), one young person asked: "Are you on the social network Instagram? Do you watch YouTube?" Putin replied: "After a hard day's work, I can barely drag myself into bed, let alone look at Instagram." He added: "I have nothing to do with any account on social media."

This February, when Kovalchuk, director of the Kurchatov Institute — Russia's national research center — spoke at a meeting about the rapid pace of technological development and the growing spread of smart devices, he cited the example that most people now own a smartphone. Putin immediately retorted on the spot: "You just said everyone has a smartphone, but I don't."

Though Putin understands nothing whatsoever about how social media works, he has no intention of leaving alone the place where young people spend their days. In January 2014, the Russian parliament passed a law allowing the Kremlin to swiftly shut down any website it dislikes.

That March, the Kremlin moved through its connections to engineer the takeover of VK: Pavel Durov sold 12% of the company to Ivan Tavrin — a partner of the Putin-aligned oligarch Alisher Usmanov. They intended to reach into this platform that gives young people a place to organize and express themselves.

On this deal to buy the social platform, *The New York Times* argued that Putin ultimately cannot truly understand what young people think: "Their real hero is Durov — the entrepreneur who used the money he earned freelancing to buy a server and then built a company now worth US$3.5 billion. And now this hero is about to leave Russia."

Over these three years, the Putin government has used every means to tighten its grip on the internet. [On November 1, 2017, a new law regulating anonymous use of the internet formally took effect in Russia.](http://tsrus.cn/shizheng/2017/11/10/659611) The new law does not ban VPNs outright, but it restricts access to blocked websites — Navalny's personal website, for instance, has been blocked multiple times by Roskomnadzor, Russia's federal communications and media regulator.

But this has not diminished netizens' desire to keep watching the corruption documents and reports Navalny releases; whenever he posts anything on social media, plenty of young people share and engage with it.

*The Washington Post* recently pointed out that, against the backdrop of Navalny's video on the government's vast corruption, many viewers could see another truth in it: some 25 million Russians still live below the poverty line. Still, [according to the latest release from the research and forecasting department of Russia's central bank](http://tsrus.cn/kuaixun/2018/02/17/660681), the Russian economy grew 1.5% over the whole of 2017 and has fully emerged from recession.

When talk turns to Russia's economy and the pressure on young people's lives, Eason always grows dejected. His family is not particularly well-off, and he didn't grow up in a big city. As Varya describes it, Eason left home to start working and support himself as soon as he reached adulthood, while also helping out with the family business.

"I'm quite unhappy with Russia's economic development. Russia never has any money, and the current government should be focused on that. If Putin stays on for another six years, it will just be more of the same," Eason says.

### A New Generation With No Memory of the Soviet Union

When Eason talks about Russia's economic troubles, he often brings up the hard lives of his parents' generation, but he admits frankly that he actually knows nothing about life in the Soviet period.

In September 2017, VTsIOM conducted a national sample survey of how familiar people of different age groups are with history. Whether the questions concerned events of the Stalin era or political figures and events of the Soviet period, more than 60% of respondents aged 18 to 24 answered "not clear."

The Levada Center has also surveyed the theme of "nostalgia for the Soviet Union": among 18- to 24-year-olds, the nostalgia index is far lower than in other age groups — and of course, the older the age group, the higher the nostalgia.

!["Nostalgia for the Soviet Union" (Image / Levada Center)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201802260027588EWTaQ9eYBVnmMGH.png-w600)

For young people born after the dissolution, their first route into Soviet history usually comes from their parents.

"My dad loves the Soviet era, but my mom is more forward-thinking — she doesn't like that period. My dad is 45 now, and I think he misses the Soviet Union out of nostalgia. That era was quite closed off, and people's thinking was closed off and controlled too. I think they understand that, but they were young then — I don't think they really miss the Soviet Union; they just miss their own youth," Nastya says.

Whether in survey reports or in conversations with these young Russians, they generally admit they're not too interested in the past; compared with what lies behind them, they seem to care more about the future.

Nastya believes life in Russia today is better than in the past, but there are still many problems — the employment rate for university graduates, for instance. Eason says wages are too low; Svetlana points to economic stagnation; Dmitry cites an unfair democratic system.

![Protests in Russia in 2017](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201802260045536x5AJoIKiNTwZGzd.png-w600)

The January 28 demonstration had only recently wound down. Although the crowd was smaller than at the 2012 and 2017 protests, it quietly spread fast among many netizens. Navalny, who was briefly arrested and then released on the day of the protest, has continued to launch a nationwide anti-corruption movement; by his own account, more than 200,000 volunteers have joined in nearly a hundred Russian cities.

At the end of last year, Meduza, a well-known Russian news site, chose young people as its 2017 Person of the Year, arguing that Russia's new generation of young people may deliver a shock to existing traditional politics.

In other words, today's young people face two opposite processes: indifference to big politics, and the politicization of youth culture. The former refers to traditional politics; the latter rises out of personal preference.

"I'm not sure Russia has a nationwide opposition organization or a party system. All the street movements we see originate online. That place, with no clear affiliation or obligations, looks to me more like an improvisational political movement. And if you want to scale up and normalize this kind of improvisational political movement, instinctive emotional drive probably won't be enough — you may well need more extreme factors to make it happen," Wu Dada believes young people are not entirely indifferent to politics or to Russian society — they have simply found new ways to express themselves and to gather.

![Navalny launches an online commemoration of the killing of Boris Nemtsov](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180226002839oWO0TfzgLZqucX2P.png-w600)

As the election draws near, Varya's mother has begun recommending the candidate Grudinin to people around her. She told QDaily that the Anti-Corruption Foundation Navalny founded is a good thing in itself, but Navalny the man is another matter — she doesn't feel he has put forward substantive policies or proposals. Varya adds that her mother and Dmitry have recently clashed again.

"I don't think my parents believe this society can change, so I haven't discussed my support for Navalny with them very much. I think their lives in the past (the Soviet Union) were very hard times, full of injustice and scandals, and that's why they don't believe in change, or that the future could get better," Dmitry adds. "I can say with certainty that our generation does not want to live the way people did in the Soviet period."
