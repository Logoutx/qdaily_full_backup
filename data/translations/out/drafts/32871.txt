During the National Day holiday, we invited eight young writers to recommend books they consider worth reading. Each has achieved a great deal in their own field and is eager to share new knowledge and ideas. This is the fifth writer, Bo Qiao.

**It's time to recommend books again.**

**At this time last year, QDaily invited some interesting people from among the big names on Weibo, WeChat and Zhihu to recommend a batch of books to us. These people included [Zhihu founder Zhou Yuan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15357.html), [Guokr founder Ji Shisan](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15272.html), [Zhang Wei, founder of the WeChat official account "Shixiang"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15320.html), [Wu Miao, founder of the WeChat official account "Leviathan"](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15303.html), and [screenwriter Shi Hang](http://www.qdaily.com/cards/15617.html). The invitation to recommend books went like this: please recommend a book you think is good from the past year—it may be helpful to your profession, or it may simply be good. Chinese and English both welcome.**

**This year, we've decided to change the way we make the recommendations, just a little.**

**In any era, professional readers are a scarce resource. These people's judgments about the value of book recommendations and reviews can become an important reference for how we choose books—and the reviews themselves can become books worth reading, such as Tony Judt's *Reappraisals*.**

**Of course, our invited writers are not all engaged in Tony Judt's kind of weighty reflection on twentieth-century history. These people are younger, more interesting, sharper-eyed, and not professional writers. Each has a certain influence in their own field and is eager to share new knowledge and ideas.**

**We hope to introduce them to you in the form of "book recommendations + long-form reviews," offering a little reference for that small matter of reading.**

**Since everyone's experience differs, the books each person recommends and the style of their reviews naturally vary—and pondering the differences among them is itself a rather interesting exercise. From October 1 to October 8, we will publish one writer's reading list and review each day, in the hope that they prove useful to you. If you spot a good professional reader around you, please leave us a comment—you may see their article here before long.**

**Here is the fifth, Bo Qiao**

Bo Qiao is a film industry professional, formerly a student of minor languages and sociology, specializing in the sociology of law and social change. He is an enthusiast of classical music, history, art history and philology, and in his spare time he also writes book reviews, music reviews and academic articles for media outlets and professional journals, and dabbles in translation work. You will find that the books he recommends mix film, sociology and classical music in many flavors—very rich indeed.

### ****Book Recommendations****

****By Bo Qiao****

****Understanding Movies******** (12th edition, full-color illustrated)****

Author: Louis Giannetti (US)

Translator: Peggy Chiao

Publisher: Beijing United Publishing Co. · Houlang Publishing Co.

Publication date: September 2016

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/mn/detailApp/ref=asc_df_B01JLSWOQ42319166/?asin=B01JLSWOQ4&tag=douban-23&creative=2384&creativeASIN=B01JLSWOQ4&linkCode=df0)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930202620jVTsvMWi4ZhJ7Y8w.jpg-w600)

Film may be the fastest-growing cultural consumer market in China over the past dozen years, developing from the bleakness of annual box office of less than 1 billion yuan to today's bold charge toward 50 billion—yet one topic has never changed: why are so many Chinese films so bad. Audiences once shared a collective understanding: Chinese directors don't know how to tell a story properly. But audiences went a step further: the screenwriters of those bad films are just too lousy.

But in fact, when we feel a film's story is good, it is not merely because the screenwriter wrote it plausibly, brilliantly, with twist after twist. What we find good-looking also includes countless angles, from cinematography and editing, to costume and production design, to acting and sound mixing. Yet for those who want to understand film more deeply, the question of "how each of a film's departments makes the film good" is genuinely difficult—after all, every trade has its specialty, and truly grasping every facet of a film is no easy feat.

*Understanding Movies* can, to a great extent, help those film lovers with some aspirations to better "understand movies." The book has twelve chapters in total. The first eleven take eleven aspects—cinematography, mise-en-scène, movement, editing, sound, acting—and break down, one by one, the parts of a film that make it good: how *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon* used a moving camera to record martial-arts action as elegant as dance; why Nolan's *Batman* contains so many peculiar compositions that leave audiences baffled; what role the casting director played in big-mouthed Julia Roberts winning the Oscar for Best Actress...... And in the final chapter, the author uses the previous eleven "ways of understanding movies" to dissect the classic *Citizen Kane* from every angle—the whole reading process is like an anatomy class: first a detailed, item-by-item explanation, and then a full systematic demonstration at the end.

The brand-new 12th edition of *Understanding Movies* is printed in full color, breaking free of the predicament in previous editions where the vast majority of stills were black-and-white. Especially in the sections explaining cinematography, acting and drama, the vivid, as-new stills restore the film clips most completely—when reading a printed book about film, we no longer have to mentally fill in the color tones and lighting of the movies; this new edition conveys it all to us in the most direct way possible.

****The Big Picture: Money and Power in Hollywood****

Author: Edward Jay Epstein (US)

Translator: Song Weihang

Publisher: Imagist | Taihai Publishing House

Publication date: June 2016

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E5%88%B6%E9%80%A0%E5%A4%A7%E7%89%87-%E9%87%91%E9%92%B1-%E6%9D%83%E5%8A%9B%E4%B8%8E%E5%A5%BD%E8%8E%B1%E5%9D%9E%E7%9A%84%E7%A7%98%E5%AF%86-%E7%88%B1%E5%BE%B7%E5%8D%8E%C2%B7%E6%9D%B0%C2%B7%E8%89%BE%E6%99%AE%E6%96%AF%E5%9D%A6/dp/B01H4NTGWS/ref=sr_1_1?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1475238158&sr=1-1&keywords=%E5%88%B6%E9%80%A0%E5%A4%A7%E7%89%87)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201609302028301xIvqPRc03Y2XG46.jpg-w600)

Books on the market about film either focus on film itself—directors' interviews, actors' biographies, film history, analyses of a particular genre—or on film technique: screenwriting craft, knowledge of lighting for cinematography, the essentials of editing...... Books about the film industry and its history, however, are relatively few, though not without fine examples—such as *Down and Dirty Pictures*, which depicts the independent film movement and Miramax, and *Exploiting Hollywood*, which likewise describes the American film industry in the context of independent film. This newly published *The Big Picture*, by Edward Jay Epstein, can help us understand the more mainstream American film industry.

*The Big Picture* does not use time as its main axis but is organized by theme. The whole book is divided into six major sections and 30 pieces, each tackling one small topic: "The DVD Revolution," for instance, meticulously analyzes and explains the entire process by which the DVD went from being rejected by the studios to becoming a money-making machine, while "Hollywood's Big Six" recounts, in a mere 15 pages, the alliances and rivalries among the major early Hollywood film companies...... Epstein is like an encyclopedia: all kinds of gossip, anecdotes and curious tales come as readily as picking something out of his pocket. Even on thematically dry subjects, he can tell a story that sparkles.

The book's author, Epstein, is himself quite interesting. He holds a PhD in political science from Harvard and taught for three years at Harvard, MIT and UCLA, after which he resigned from academia and devoted himself to his writing career at his home in New York. Already while doing his master's at Cornell, Epstein was a bestselling writer; his book on the Kennedy assassination caused enormous controversy. Once he became a professional writer, he played to his strengths, wielding a masterful pen across fields including film, politics and the Cold War. Besides this book, he also published *The Hollywood Economist: The Hidden Financial Reality Behind the Movies* in 2010—and from the title alone you know it is another "deep-digging" work that exposes Hollywood.

****The Confucian-Legalist State********:**********A New Theory of Chinese History****

Author: Dingxin Zhao

Publisher: Oxford University Press

Publication date: November 2015

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Confucian-Legalist-State-Chinese-History-2015-11-13/dp/B019NDQHJM/ref=sr_1_2?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1475238383&sr=1-2&keywords=The+Confucian-legalist+State%3A+A+New+Theory+of+Chinese+History)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930203157k78sD4JGLT9mgNIc.jpg-w600)

When Dingxin Zhao's *The Eastern Zhou Wars and the Birth of the Confucian-Legalist State* was published domestically in 2006, it did not cause any particular uproar, but his approach of offering an entirely new interpretation of Chinese history through sociological methods was genuinely refreshing. The book met with collective neglect from China's historical academia at home; the few who expressed an opinion were mostly critical, especially since much of the book's historical material and factual claims could hardly stand up to scrutiny. Within sociological circles, however, it received considerable praise. In particular, Zhao's way of looking at "state capacity" through multiple dimensions—geographic distance, frequency of alliances, frequency of wars waged—and rereading the warring states of the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods through this lens was, for Chinese historical research, truly a path of its own.

The 2015 *The Confucian-Legalist State: A New Theory of Chinese History*, published by Oxford University Press, can be regarded as a fully upgraded new edition of that earlier book. A Chinese edition is due out from Zhejiang University Press at the end of 2016, and it will surely set off a round of major debates in sociological and historical circles. But the English text is itself brilliant, and readers can well get a head start.

*The Confucian-Legalist State* greatly extends and expands on the earlier work in both depth and breadth (in fact, the 2006 edition is essentially a draft of what are now chapters two through nine). Working from a social-science research approach, Zhao offers a comprehensive interpretation of the developmental trajectory of the "state" in Chinese history from four aspects—politics, military affairs, economy and ideology—integrating into his account numerous factors such as environment, ecology, technology and forms of social organization. With the rise of the new cultural history and new social history, research in environmental history, ecological history and the history of technology has flourished in recent years, but integrating many of the viewpoints and methodologies of these specialized histories into a systematic historical framework—and using it to interpret the broader and deeper reaches of political and social history—makes his arguments strikingly fresh while still, on the whole, holding water. Through a sociological lens, Zhao offers a different description of Chinese history from the Spring and Autumn period through the Qin and Han, and even on down to the late Qing—the events are still those events, but beyond the political contests of winners and losers, dynastic rise and fall, entirely new "causal relationships" emerge among the historical stories and historical concepts we thought we knew by heart.

In fact, the view of "using Huang-Lao thought inwardly while presenting Confucianism outwardly" is hardly a novel insight. What is remarkable about Dingxin Zhao is that he uses this mechanism to cleverly explain many problems along the two-thousand-year course of Chinese history: Why was the Qin dynasty so quickly replaced by the Han? Why did a standoff and tug-of-war develop between the northern nomadic peoples and the southern Han Chinese regimes? Why did the school of mind flourish at its height in the late Ming yet fail to develop fully? Why did China not develop capitalism? Why did China not form a public sphere and civil society of local self-governance like the West?—To be able to analyze such a long stretch of history with a single macro-theoretical framework, and for Zhao's book to remain essentially self-consistent, is itself a truly brilliant dance on the knife's edge.

****The Burden of Female Talent********:********The Poet Li Qingzhao and Her History in China****

Author: Ronald Egan

Publisher: Harvard University Asia Center

Publication date: February 2014

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Burden-Female-Talent-Harvard-Yenching-Institute/dp/B00IVM0KRW/ref=sr_1_2?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1475238468&sr=1-2&keywords=The+Burden+of+Female+Talent%3A+The+Poet+Li+Qingzhao+and+Her+History+in+China)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930203319BiJN9R0nICQwDL7j.jpg-w600)

In recent years, Western sinology's research on Chinese literature has begun to gain traction domestically. Apart from the several books on Tang poetry written earlier by Stephen Owen, *The Cambridge History of Chinese Literature* and *The Columbia History of Chinese Literature* have both been published in China in the past few years. Although many scholars will scoff at foreign scholars' research on Chinese literary history for its flaws, overseas sinologists have undoubtedly provided us with a distinctive perspective. Both of the above literary histories invited the most heavyweight scholars in the field to write specialized chapters within their individual research areas. Reviews of the "Cambridge history" have been basically positive, while the "Columbia history" has been heavily criticized—Martin Kern of Princeton University and Robert E. Hegel of the University of Washington, both contributors to the former book, even wrote a 21-page-long article criticizing *The Columbia History of Chinese Literature*. Yet there were also scholars invited to write related chapters for both books. To be invited by two monographs so different in style, format and readership marks one out as a leader in the field, and among them is Ronald Egan, professor at Stanford University and a specialist in Song dynasty literature.

Professor Ronald Egan's *The Burden of Female Talent*, published in 2014, is still available only in English (though it can be found online), but a Chinese edition is reportedly due from Shanghai Classics Publishing House at the end of the year. Li Qingzhao's importance in Song dynasty literature is beyond question; the scholarship on her, while perhaps not quite as vast as the thousand commentaries on Du Fu or the five hundred on Han Yu, is certainly considerable. At the same time, Li Qingzhao's surviving works are not many, and the existing texts have long been turned over and read intensively by scholars of every generation. For Egan, as a Western scholar, to put forward any new viewpoint is exceptionally difficult.

Yet Egan still strikes out on his own and offers a great many insights. For example, whether scholars or readers of later ages, people have always tended to read Li Qingzhao's ci as reflections of her personal life—both interpretation and dating take her ci as an important reference for studying her biography. But in fact, when Song people wrote ci, most of it was in invented situations and adopted personas (especially in the voice of women); men would often chant their melancholy in the voice of a woman pining in her spring boudoir or a courtesan singer, yet because we know the authors were men, we naturally understand that these are not those male literati's life stories. But when it comes to Li Qingzhao, because the gender coincides, people often cannot accurately judge the boundary between fiction and reality.

Moreover, Li Qingzhao's "happy" married life with Zhao Mingcheng, and her later remarriage and divorce, have long been a focal point (and gossip). Yet for a long stretch of time, Ming and Qing scholars, out of the moral and gender views of their day, sternly denied the history of Li Qingzhao's remarriage in order to highlight her positive image—even going so far as to tamper with historical sources and selectively ignore evidence—and research on Li Qingzhao's marital situation has also greatly influenced the interpretation of her poetry. Egan's research presents us with a clearly reasoned reception history of Li Qingzhao: the Li Qingzhao we know may not be that talented woman of middle-school textbooks and stereotypes; Li Qingzhao herself has richer, bolder, even tomboyish facets.

****Violence: A Micro-sociological Theory****

Author: Randall Collins (US)

Translator: Liu Ran

Publisher: Peking University Press

Publication date: June 2016

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E6%9A%B4%E5%8A%9B-%E4%B8%80%E7%A7%8D%E5%BE%AE%E8%A7%82%E7%A4%BE%E4%BC%9A%E5%AD%A6%E7%90%86%E8%AE%BA-%E5%85%B0%E5%BE%B7%E5%B0%94%C2%B7%E6%9F%AF%E6%9E%97%E6%96%AF/dp/B01HEH7H4I/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475239220&sr=8-1&keywords=%E6%9A%B4%E5%8A%9B)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930204721NCaWZgtrn7BGx9UE.jpg-w600)

*Violence* is probably the thickest book on this list, and its subject matter also looks the most "weighty," yet the process of reading it is full of fun—because, to put it crudely, this book studies "fighting."

But whether it is "fighting" on the battlefield, racial conflict on American streets, or grappling in the sporting arena, we are always accustomed to thinking about these questions from grand social angles: ethnicity, gender, class, nation, the gap between rich and poor...... Randall Collins, professor of sociology at the University of Pennsylvania, instead interprets violence from a micro perspective. In his view, those grand social contradictions do exist, but they do not necessarily lead to violence. America's racial problems, for instance, have always existed, but compared with the hundreds of millions of daily interactions among individuals of different ethnicities, violence between Black and white people does not actually occur all that often.

And in Collins's view, examining the mechanisms of how concrete violence happens in micro-situations is the core of understanding the essence of violence. According to his research, at the moment violence is about to occur, the dominant emotions are often not only anger but also unavoidable tension and fear, and countless potential acts of violence remain perpetually on the edge of eruption because of these emotions without ever actually erupting. But once many factors are in place, violence becomes unavoidable: onlookers crowding around (and even egging it on), the pressure of rules and institutions (such as orders and punishments on the battlefield), overwhelming advantage (such as school bullying), and the huge vacuum created by a moral holiday (such as people's looting, burning and smashing during urban riots). In particular, Collins introduces the concept of emotional entrainment: when the emotional rhythms of potential violence fall completely into sync, one side's violent tendencies are mobilized to the greatest extent, allowing the perpetrator to overcome their own fear and tension and fully carry out the violent act.

Chapter Three of the book, "Forward Panic," also re-examines, from a micro-sociological perspective, the unimaginable violence of the Nanjing Massacre. Collins explains, from an almost entirely new angle, the sociological mechanisms behind the Japanese army's massacre in Nanjing—especially using the idea of the "moral holiday" to analyze why the killing later spun completely out of control. For this writer, a native of Nanjing, it is at once harrowing and capable of provoking deeper reflection on the mechanisms by which violence arises.

### Book Review

***Producing Excellence: The Making of Virtuosos***

Author: Izabela Wagner

Translator: Huang Yanning

Publisher: East China Normal University Press

Publication date: June 2016

![From: [Amazon](https://www.amazon.cn/%E9%9F%B3%E4%B9%90%E7%A5%9E%E7%AB%A5%E5%8A%A0%E5%B7%A5%E5%8E%82-%E4%BC%8A%E8%8E%8E%E8%B4%9D%E6%8B%89%C2%B7%E7%93%A6%E6%A0%BC%E7%BA%B3/dp/B01HNFC52U/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1475238618&sr=8-1&keywords=%E9%9F%B3%E4%B9%90%E7%A5%9E%E7%AB%A5%E5%8A%A0%E5%B7%A5%E5%8E%82)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20160930205131ZqKzHMs1W0Uc8vBj.png-w600)

****From Prodigy to Master: How Performers Are Made****

**By Bo Qiao**

Classical music may be the field in the world where "prodigies" cluster most densely. Whether on television or at small public events, those young children always look utterly bewildered—until they sit down at the piano or pick up the violin, and their fingers fly up and down. After a rustic yet supremely fleet excerpt from Mozart or Paganini, the audience erupts in applause, as if witnessing the birth of another Kissin or Menuhin. At which point the prodigy's parents are invited on stage to describe, tireless in their patience, the following key facts, including (but not limited to):

1. How exceedingly difficult the piece the prodigy just played is;

2. That the prodigy took an interest in music before learning to speak;

3. That the prodigy could play some melodies on their own, without a teacher, at a very young age;

4. The astonishment and praise of professional teachers at their talent;

5. How reluctant the parents were to let the child's gift go to waste......

**Same Prodigies, Different Lives**

It is not only in China; in European and American television programs, such "prodigy" stories are just as common. But if we think back carefully, most of those musical prodigies hailed as once-in-decades eventually vanish into the videotapes of television programs. We cannot even summon up a memory of their faces and names—we can only vaguely recall having heard some legend about a "prodigy" somewhere. And then, when we turn around, there are always new prodigies on television, with the same bewildered, dazed faces, the same fingers flying up and down, even the same repertoire.

Whenever we talk about those performers who have already achieved great success, there is always an illusion: people assume the masters were already peerless musical wonders in their youth. People are so forgetful, because there were more of those "peerless" musical wonders once active on every media platform, who were ultimately selectively forgotten by us. And those prodigies who once enjoyed a burst of fame either became music teachers somewhere locally, or occasionally took on some not-quite-standard small performances, or even made cameo appearances on certain entertainment programs—and some ultimately chose to change careers entirely.

Among those prodigies, how many in the end become a Lang Lang, a Kissin, an Itzhak Perlman? What kind of grinding must those artless musical prodigies endure to grow into soloist masters? And how many fall by the wayside on the road from prodigy to master? What exactly happened to those "failed" prodigies? Why are there so many stories of "Shang Zhongyong"—prodigies who never lived up to early promise? Does superlative musical talent plus diligent practice guarantee them a place on the star stage of the classical music world?

The ethnography *Producing Excellence: The Making of Virtuosos* by Polish sociologist Izabela Wagner may offer us a panoramic view of "from prodigy to master."

**The Professional Child Musician: A Mysterious World**

The book *Producing Excellence* interviewed, across six countries, 90 child musicians aspiring to become violin soloists; conducted statistical analysis of 339 students who entered competitions; collected documents on the teaching activities of more than 40 teachers; and carried out sociological (more precisely, anthropological) participant observation. It systematically examines the career trajectories of violin learners as they rise from "schoolchild" to professional student who hears nothing of the outside world, and finally either step into the spotlight or vanish into the crowd.

The field of professional music education has its own closedness. Although millions of children join the army of music learners every year, professional performance education aimed at a career in performance has always been unknown to outsiders. For one thing, this kind of specialized education has a high threshold, requiring parents to invest large amounts of time and money while also supervising and accompanying their children's practice. Most parents, whose aim is to let their children cultivate artistic sensibility and delight their temperament, will not venture too deep into it.

For another, because the circle of professional music education is small and highly specialized, the teachers, parents and children in this field frequently face various social prejudices (such as the idea that only students who can't do well in academic subjects choose arts majors), hidden rules of the circle (behind-the-scenes manipulation of performance competitions), and silent competition (competition among students, and competition among teachers for students). This makes them, when facing the outside world, habitually and self-protectively tight-lipped about many questions—on the one hand using silence to resist prejudice, and on the other unable to break the many industry secrets not to be shared with outsiders, lest their careers suffer a devastating blow.

And the author Wagner herself both plays the piano and is the mother of a violin soloist—so not only could she see through the operating logic of the entire solo music field, she also knew firsthand, with shared feeling, those secrets the outside world may have heard of but half-believed. Participant observation thus became Wagner's best weapon. She could observe more closely: the interactive relationship between teacher and student in lessons; the different standards by which teachers take on apprentices; the distinctive considerations of parents choosing teachers; the open rivalry and veiled struggle among contestants at competitions; the hints and reminders teachers gave parents; and how a priceless violin passes from one soloist to another.

Taking the growth trajectory of the child musician as her main axis, Wagner shows us, at each stage, the simple yet complex relationships between the child and parents, teachers, classmates and the outside world. She lets us see how those heaven's favorites, the prodigies, first come to prominence in small local circles, then pass through brutal rounds of elimination, shuttle between different masterclasses, and finally—through music competitions, with the help of first-rate instruments worth astronomical sums, the protection of their teachers at competitions, their parents' networks of connections, and nonstop travel to every corner, and of course, most importantly, that damned thing called luck—manage to become a "somewhat famous" soloist.

**Ethnographic Insights and Blind Spots**

Academically, this book has three points well worth noting. First, the research in this book is clearly an exploration of the life course, and such research is generally more commonly done with quantitative methods, because life-course research usually targets a particular group and requires a sufficiently large sample to track over the long term how different variables affect a living person's life trajectory. If a qualitative method is adopted, although the quality of the qualitative interviews will be higher and the depth of interviews can reveal more facets, the overall sampling is lower, and the sample loses representativeness.

But because of Wagner's special identity as a parent of a child musician, and her long observation of this question, she could not only get to know a large sample of child musicians and come into contact with a large number of people at different positions on the "industry chain"; her paradoxical dual identity also meant that, in many cases, she could efficiently produce high-quality field notes that ordinary people could hardly imagine. For example, when a teacher discussed with Wagner the progress of her child's playing, Wagner the mother would naturally ask very seriously how to keep improving the child's technique, whether to increase the intensity of practice, and so on. But at the same time, Wagner the sociologist was also observing the conversation: the teacher's tone and attitude, the questions parents care about, how the teacher responded to the parents...... This firsthand experience and observation made up, to a great extent, for the fatal problem of insufficient samples.

Second, this book was translated from a series of Wagner's papers. Although the English *Producing Excellence* was published last year by Rutgers University Press, the Chinese edition is not a Sinicized product of the English book. On the contrary, because the Chinese edition was compiled from a series of Wagner's papers, it retains more vivid details and lively descriptions, though the structure is slightly cumbersome.

Perhaps because of the nature of ethnography, the thick description that ethnography relentlessly pursues replaces the exploration of theoretical questions found in traditional social-science works. Wagner gives only a brief explanation of her methodology at the end, and that explanation strikes us as rather sketchy. In fact, although ethnography from its very birth contained a kind of reaction against the social-science tendency to pursue theory excessively, the career of learning music depicted in Wagner's book actually leaves plenty of room for theoretical discussion: symbolic interaction in certain situations, interaction ritual chains at the micro level—all could be deeply elaborated through the process of the child's musical training. But what is lost in one place is gained in another: precisely because this book abandons theoretical entanglements, its readability as an ethnography is greatly enhanced.

Finally, Wagner's book relies to a slightly excessive degree on individual works and memoirs. For a book on the "life course" that already faces a sample crisis, when consulting literature on teaching lineages and mentorship, such overly single sources of material inevitably invite regret.

The road to the elite world is so cruel. Although we always like to describe the High Arts world that classical music represents with words like "noble," "elegant," "excellent," "perfect" and "universal love," in fact this world is likewise filled to the brim with calculation, injustice, insinuation, narrow-mindedness and the exchange of interests. Wagner's book does not aim to reveal the darkness and filth behind the music world. For the author, what matters more is presenting the internal system and operating mechanisms of the profession of the classical music performer: set in its ways yet keeping pace with the times; chaotic and tangled yet a world unto itself; rife with insiders pulling strings yet sometimes favoring the strongest of all—in a sense, it is precisely this ambiguity, this back-and-forth, this uncertainty that makes this ethnography so brilliant.
