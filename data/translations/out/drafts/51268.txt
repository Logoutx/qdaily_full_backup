A Conversation with Art Historian Fred S. Kleiner: How to Read Art, and Why Studying It Still Matters Today

@@QD_BODY@@
*Gardner's Art Through the Ages* was the first global art history to break the "Western" boundary. Where did it come from, and why does it matter?

Art historian Fred S. Kleiner has been teaching at Boston University for 40 years. He believes art history is the best way to understand a culture. A scholar of ancient Roman art, his daily work is answering questions like this: why does the emperor on this relief look more like his successor than like himself?

Grasping these subtle distinctions requires combining imagery, archaeology, politics, and more. For 13 years, Kleiner served as editor-in-chief of the *American Journal of Archaeology*. The book he is currently writing is about art and politics in the Roman Empire.

One student described his lecturing style to us this way: "Many art historians take a simple idea and dress it up in abstruse vocabulary and expressions so it sounds lofty and grand, but he would never do that. In his class, you never feel the discipline is so high up that it's out of reach — in fact, a lot of the art history theories and writings we see today aren't all that complicated once they're translated into plain language."

In fact, Kleiner's students are not only in Boston but spread all over the world. That is thanks to an authoritative art history textbook: *Gardner's Art Through the Ages*. This was the first global art history to break the "Western" boundary, published in 1926. [The famous modern artist Roy Lichtenstein used this very book in college](http://www.nytimes.com/2006/01/10/arts/lichtenstein-meets-the-american-indian.html), and the Native American art in it — art beyond the scope of traditional art history — inspired his later work.

In 1990, Kleiner joined the marathon revision of *Gardner's Art Through the Ages*. Under his direction, contemporary art and women artists have appeared more often in this traditional discipline. In an interview with QDaily, he described it as a "missionary" undertaking. That brings to mind a conjecture he raised in a lecture at the Central Academy of Fine Arts in early March: the ancient Romans, he suggested, transmitted the decorative styles of Pompeian frescoes eastward in book form, all the way to the distant East, where they inspired local painters.

Almost at the same time that Kleiner took the podium, Chinese attitudes toward art began to shift. Today, art history has become one of the most popular courses in general education at some universities; many people feel that the passion for art among those around them is rising: "going to exhibitions" has become a way of life for young middle-class urbanites and college students; and in the art market, Chinese buyers have shown astonishing purchasing power — [a survey by UBS and Art Basel shows](https://www.artsy.net/article/artsy-editorial-art-market-hit-637-billion-2017-key-takeaways-art-basel-report) that China overtook the United Kingdom last year to become the world's second-largest country by art transaction value.

Yet art is never just a money game for dealers. As ordinary art lovers, what people care about is: how do we appreciate art? How do we understand art? How do we turn art from a lifestyle into a culture? In the book publishing market, we have seen bestsellers in popular art history represented by Jiang Xun. This highlights both market demand and people's knowledge anxiety — even identity anxiety.

With these questions in mind, QDaily interviewed Professor Fred Kleiner. We talked about everything from art history textbooks to how to better understand art, how studying art history can make us better citizens, and how to connect art with the present we live in.

**Q – QDaily
K – Fred S. Kleiner**

**Q: *Gardner's Art Through the Ages* is now in its fifteenth edition. Why does it need to be revised so frequently?**

**K:** Knowledge is updated every year. I need to update a lot of the latest information, expand the contents of the textbook, and keep adding new artworks. Fifty years have passed, and art history has gained fifty years of content. I also know that students — whether in China or in the United States — are very interested in contemporary art. That is why we insist on including contemporary art.

**Q: Which artists have been newly added?**

**K:** I'm sure it's a long list. I'd probably have to put the old and new editions side by side to tell you the complete list. The current book has more women artists and more minority artists — what we call minorities in the United States, like Latinos, Asian Americans, and so on. So this edition is more representative.

But even for content that was already in the old edition, some things have changed. Take the famous ancient city of Pompeii: we all thought Pompeii's city center was built between 250 and 150 BC, and that after the Romans conquered the city, it didn't change much. Recent research has found that the buildings we thought formed the city center only became the main buildings after the Romans arrived. I need to correct this kind of knowledge, even though it concerns art from more than two thousand years ago. Every four or five years I update the book, and it keeps me very busy.

**Q: The first edition of this book came out more than ninety years ago. How did you join this long-running project?**

**K:** Ah, I can tell you the history of this book. It's quite interesting. In the early twentieth century, roughly a hundred years ago, American art history books only recorded American and European art; nobody studied global art history. E. H. Gombrich's *The Story of Art* was one of them.

The person who first proposed the concept of a global art history was a woman named Helen Gardner, who taught at a very famous art institution in Chicago. But because she was a woman, they never granted her a professorship. The world back then was completely dominated by men, and the field of art history was no exception. She wrote the first global art history, and it was a huge success. That was in 1926, and the last chapter of the book was on Picasso and Claude Monet. She lived about twenty more years after that and wrote the second and third editions of the book. After she died, the publisher owned the copyright and hired many scholars to update it. I took over starting with the tenth edition; they invited me to do it in 1990, and the tenth edition came out in 1995.

There's an interesting story: I didn't actually volunteer to do this — they came to me. One day the publisher came to me and said he would be in Boston within two weeks and asked whether we could have lunch. I assumed they wanted to pitch their books to me over lunch, so I said no need, but you can come to my office. And he really came to my office. We made small talk for a while, and then he said, "The real reason I'm here is to ask whether you would like to become a co-author of *Gardner's Art Through the Ages*?" I answered honestly: "I don't know — I've never thought about writing a textbook." I asked him, "How soon do you need my answer?" He said, "One month." I said yes on the twenty-ninth day. Later on it became my main job.

**Q: Why do you insist on revising rather than writing a new textbook?**

**K:** I never thought about writing an art history textbook. First, writing a book like that could take eight to ten years; second, I didn't know where to find a publisher, or whether anyone would want to buy it. But revising an existing art history book means you instantly have a large readership without much effort, and that was quite attractive to me. Because even though I teach many students — in Boston I have hundreds, even thousands — there are so many students in the world I have never taught. Without this book (*Gardner's Art Through the Ages*), I would never have the chance to teach students like you. So I actually do this with a missionary spirit, hoping to make people all over the world fall in love with art and art history through this book.

Starting with the thirteenth edition, the book has been entirely my responsibility. Now the sixteenth edition is about to come out. The reason I've been coming to China so often lately is also that the book is being translated into Chinese. My wife is also an art history professor, but our spring breaks don't line up, so the two of us can't go places together. She's at home now; the snow in Boston is this high, and she's angry that I've left her to shovel it alone. Our research fields happen to be exactly the same. Normally a married couple can teach at the same university, but if you study the same field, that doesn't really work. So we teach at different schools — she's at Yale. In English our marriage is called a "commuter marriage," meaning that because the two spouses work in different places, one of them accommodates the other by constantly commuting between two cities. And I'm the one who commutes — conventionally it's the wife who revolves around the husband, but we're not like that. Wherever my wife is, that's where the kids and the family are.

**Q: How do you define a successful art history textbook? What qualities does a good art history textbook need?**

**K:** Ah, that's a good question. It seems a bit hard for me to say my own book is the best. But I can tell you one thing: I have never forgotten how difficult those materials were for me to understand when I was an introductory art history student. So for every sentence I write and every decision I make, I consider what I needed to know back then when I was just starting out. Many art history books are written for the author's colleagues, for people with a very high level of art history. But that won't work for an introductory textbook. That's my approach, and it seems to work pretty well.

There's another element that actually has nothing to do with me: you have to have a publisher willing to back you, one that spares no expense in printing — color printing of images demands very high standards — and that also pays special attention to binding, design, and so on. I'm lucky to have such a publisher.

**Q: I wonder what you think of books like *What Are You Looking At?: 150 Years of Modern Art*, which write art history in a storytelling way?**

**K:** I imagine they're fun to read, but they may not contain all the information you need for an exam. There are art books aimed specifically at the general public, and there are books meant for the college classroom. These are two different markets. A book that succeeds in one market will never succeed in the other. So my book is not the former but the latter — it's prepared for the college classroom, with detailed information on artworks, a glossary of terms, and so on. It may not be very suitable for the general reader, and conversely, art books for general readers are not very suitable for art history majors in college.

**Q: Many Chinese art lovers ask themselves the question: "Do I really understand this painting?" What do you think "understanding art" is all about?**

**K:** I'm just about to start another project, which is quite interesting — and should be quite interesting for you two as well. The book of mine that has been translated into Chinese was actually written for Americans, but what Chinese readers really need is a history of Western art written for them. Let me give two examples. For American readers, the American Declaration of Independence is common knowledge that needs no explanation, but for some Chinese readers it may be unfamiliar. And the Cultural Revolution is something every Chinese person knows about, but I might have to explain to American readers what it was. So Hunan Fine Arts Publishing House invited me to write a new book for Chinese readers, rather than translating a book written for Americans.

When I wrote books in the past, I would ask myself: what do American students need to know? Now the challenge is bigger for me: I need to ask myself, what do Chinese students need to know? I'll start writing this book in the second half of this year, and I hope it can be published in 2019 or 2020.

Ah, my problem is that I always say "yes," which keeps me very busy. I sleep very little now. But I enjoy doing these things. You know, nobody becomes a professor to make money. We become professors out of a love of teaching, out of the love of traveling the world and learning about art in other places. Writing art history books lets me teach on a scale I never had before — that's why I agreed to write this book, even though I'm still not a rich man.

**Q: In your lecture at the Central Academy of Fine Arts, you mentioned that the ancient Romans were very familiar with certain elements in frescoes and reliefs, and that changes in these elements reflected shifts in the social order of power. What implications does this have for how we observe and analyze the relationship between contemporary image culture and social reality?**

**K:** That's a very good question. We live in a world where everyone gets information by watching rather than reading. People get all kinds of information from videos, television, and the internet. As I said in my lecture, you need to understand visual language. For example, in today's politics, politicians always use lots of images to promote their political agendas. In the United States, if the president goes on television to announce a new family-related policy, they will set up cameras in the White House, the president sits in his seat, and next to him are his family members. In another situation, if he needs to tell people that the United States is going to war with North Korea, then the people beside him will no longer be his family but American soldiers. In short, we are engaged in a kind of visual reading.

![On March 8, U.S. President Donald Trump signed a bill raising tariffs on steel and aluminum products. Screenshot from [Donald J. Trump's official Twitter](https://twitter.com/realDonaldTrump/media)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320000109qmSVre5UB4FEc2zf.jpg-w600)

If you study art history, you learn how to read images. Whether you study Roman art, Renaissance art, or modern art, you will become a better citizen in the modern world. That is also why I think art history is a very important discipline. Unfortunately, though, not many people study it these days. Science, computers, engineering — these are what most students want to study. But I think everyone should study art history; it teaches exactly what you're asking about: how to decode the images of contemporary life?

Don't forget, artists are the people who create images, but most of the time they create them for other people. Your impression of an artist today may be someone in a T-shirt and jeans who gets up in the morning, goes to Starbucks for a coffee, and says, "What shall I create today?" But in the vast majority of art history, artists worked for others: Egyptian kings, Roman popes, princes, all kinds of royalty... In contemporary political art (propaganda images), artists work for governments. It's very different. If you study art history, you'll understand contemporary life better.

**Q: Fashion shows today often draw inspiration from art history and incorporate art history elements into products. [Louis Vuitton released a famous-paintings bag collection a couple of years ago](https://www.louisvuitton.cn/zhs-cn/women/masters-lvxkoons/da-vinci/_/N-1dso9x4Z1i5q6hq), printing paintings by da Vinci and Monet onto bags. In your view, is this promoting art, or vulgarizing art?**

**K:** Interesting question. I think it depends on how they use the art. You know, Marcel Duchamp has a famous work — *[L.H.O.O.Q.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L.H.O.O.Q.)* — in which he drew a mustache on the Mona Lisa. I think he was vulgarizing art. But if you're just printing artworks on bags and clothes, I don't think that's vulgarizing art; I think it's wonderful — it spreads the love of art. I'm happy to buy beautiful ties myself; I don't happen to own a Mona Lisa tie, but I don't think such a tie is vulgar.

In fact, many companies have made big money through art, and if they put that money back into the art world and support the development of art, I think that's wonderful. Take Italy: the Italian government is having a lot of financial problems these days, some of their ancient buildings are about to collapse, and it's private enterprises that put up the money to restore them. If you go to Rome now, there's the Trevi Fountain (the famous wishing fountain), [which was recently restored by the well-known brand FENDI](https://www.fendi.com/us/fendi-roma/fendi-is-rome/the-restoration-of-the-trevi-fountain). FENDI put its own name on it, but it really did a good thing.

![Surrealist photographer Man Ray's 1926 image "Black and White" was used by NARS last year on the compact case of one of its products. Image source: [wikiart.org](https://www.wikiart.org/en/man-ray/black-and-white)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180320003229HjUmKzQS0hWdsAni.jpg-w600)

**Q: Today, museums and art galleries have received a lot of investment, and people are increasingly interested in these art venues. But on the other hand, art history books are still very expensive. Is art history still a discipline studied by a small elite?**

**K:** Hmm... no, I don't think so. You could say art history belongs to the elite because they have the leisure time to visit museums. But in every school in the United States — whether it's a famous school or one attended by children of working-class families — you can study art history. In the United States you can never study only art history; that's what British universities do. Every student here has to study all kinds of subjects. Even if you plan to major in art history, in your freshman year you still have to take English literature, chemistry, biology, foreign languages, economics — we call it a liberal arts education. Likewise, students who don't major in art history still have to take humanities courses: philosophy, classics, religious studies, art history, history, and so on. Only a small number of people major in art history, but medical students, law students, and computer science students also come into contact with art history.

Yes, art history textbooks are expensive, but they don't cost as much as tuition. Tuition at American universities is extremely expensive — a year's tuition exceeds many people's annual income, and if a family has several children, it's even harder. By comparison, textbook costs are not a real worry. On the other hand, we are trying to reduce textbook costs. For example, we offer electronic editions of the textbook, which can save about half the money. There will probably be more and more e-books in the future. Personally, though, I love printed books — my ideal book would be a fully hand-written medieval one.

Ah, the move from printed books to e-books is human progress all the same. When I was at a North American art history academic conference in Los Angeles two weeks ago, I met a professor from Nigeria who said he wanted to use my textbook. The problem is that shipping textbooks there is expensive and takes a long time — by the time they receive the books, the semester is over. So I suggested he use the electronic edition. He asked me, "Have you ever been to Nigeria?" I said no. He said, "You don't understand our situation — electricity and internet are extremely unstable there, so we can't use e-books." That is a big problem, and I hope it can be solved one day. China is very fortunate: most people live in the online world and have electronic devices, so you have access to a lot of information. Of course, you need some tricks to access certain websites.

**Q: You just mentioned liberal arts education. Specifically, why is this kind of education important?**

**K:** Well, I have to say it's very, very important. Students today have some mistaken views. They always think that if you want to become the boss of a company like Ford or IBM, you need to study business, finance, and so on. But if you look at what the CEOs of America's Fortune 500 companies majored in, you'll find they almost all studied history, political science, literature, and so on. Because those are the disciplines that teach you **how to think, how to think creatively**.

Take my son as an example. He works on Wall Street, mainly in investment. But he majored in history in college and then went to Harvard Business School for his master's. He tells me his colleagues almost all studied finance, but whenever they need to give a presentation to clients, they always ask him to do it, because he knows how to use the right language and how to speak in public. That also explains why so many successful business leaders come from liberal arts backgrounds. But it's hard to convince students of this. Today's students rush excessively toward science, business, and the like. Twenty years ago, there were probably twice as many people studying art history in the United States as there are now; the number of students studying economics now may be four times what it was then. As for computer science — not many people studied it back then at all.

**Q: How would you persuade art history students to take computer science or science courses? Do you think those courses are important for them?**

**K:** No. I think it's necessary the other way around — computer science majors should take some humanities courses to help them become better citizens. But I don't think... I use computers too, just like you, but I only know basic things like which key to press; I don't know anything more complicated. We modern people can't survive without knowing how to use phones and computers, but we don't need to know how to design a computer. In the United States, art history is a very popular discipline in adult education. Some people have graduated and worked for many years, have some leisure time, and want to go back to school to learn something new. Nobody goes back to school to study chemistry or biology; art history has become the most popular subject instead. They regret not having learned these things before, feel a void, and want to fill it. I don't feel a void because I never studied economics in college — the humanities fulfill me.

**Q: You first came to China fifteen years ago. In your view, what changes have taken place in Chinese art history education over these fifteen years? What progress have we made? What problems still need to be solved?**

**K:** The biggest change is that when I first came to China, the professors in schools were all students from the Cultural Revolution era, and they had no opportunity to be exposed to Western civilization or Western art. So if they really wanted to teach Western art, they could only teach themselves or go study abroad. I'm not criticizing them, but it's a fact: back then, even the teachers' knowledge base in art history was really too poor.

Fifteen years later, young people like you are genuinely learning from professors who have real knowledge. What they teach is also more authoritative. Many young Chinese have very good English, and they have access to books they previously had no chance to read. Academic exchange between China and the West is also growing, and progress in art history is truly great now. Of course, the expansion of China–West exchange is also related to historical factors like the establishment of diplomatic ties between China and the United States and Nixon. I myself firmly believe in the power of international exchange; I believe it can promote the development of education.

In fact, I think if scholars ran every government, we would have no wars at all. Because scholars always care about knowledge and are willing to share it, and this sharing has no political boundaries. I live in the United States, and nobody is constantly asking me whether I prefer the Chinese government or the American government. I think scholars have really made great contributions to world peace.

**Q: So if we want to further develop art history education, what should we do?**

**K:** Well, I think — of course this applies to the United States too — first of all there needs to be more funding, so that students have the opportunity to go abroad for education. I think a typical Chinese college student, even one studying Western art, probably has no experience of studying in the United States or Europe. If there were more funding to support them in going out to study, that would be great progress.

Second, you need to build up your libraries. Because of the Cultural Revolution, books on Western art were not imported; now is the time to buy those books, though that also takes a lot of money. Last year I went to Xi'an Academy of Fine Arts as a visiting professor. I gave many lectures, and some students made a request of me: when I returned to the United States, could I help find some relevant electronic materials? Some books are needed for research but are unavailable in China. I think that is a problem.

**Q: (To get more funding) you first have to persuade the people who set policy that studying Roman art benefits students. How would you persuade them?**

**K:** Ah, that would be hard. But I must reiterate: I believe everyone should study history, otherwise they will only repeat its mistakes. I don't think people necessarily have to know about the Roman Empire or any specific thing, but I think people should have a general sense of history — for example, knowing why wars happen. Ignoring history is bad for contemporary life. I don't think Chinese college students necessarily have to know Roman art. But I find the relationship between Tang dynasty art and Roman art fascinating — that's also part of Professor Zhu's (Zhu Qingsheng's) research, and I believe we will eventually find a connection between them.

!["Go to a museum with an open mind and an interest in art — that's enough. Helping you understand is what art curators are supposed to do." Image source: [Douban](https://movie.douban.com/photos/photo/1035773715/)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803200114286FMqfgnrmZHGIbDK.jpg-w600)

**Q: As China–West exchange grows closer, works from many foreign art institutions can now be seen in China. What do you think people need to prepare before visiting an art museum?**

**K:** That's a good question. My answer is: no preparation at all. Curators should provide all the information visitors need. Just hanging a famous painting in a gallery is not enough. Every visitor should see specific information about the exhibits. Ideally, as in many good art institutions, the moment you enter the gallery you see a rack with exhibition brochures. You can consult the brochure at any time while browsing, learning as you look.

So my answer is: go to a museum with an open mind and an interest in art — that's enough. Helping you understand is what art curators are supposed to do; you can't require visitors to study books before going to a museum. There are many well-run art institutions in the world, and many poorly run ones. That's not a difference between China and foreign countries. In the end it's still a question of money: can you afford excellent curators? Is there funding invested in curating?

**Q: But many curators, or other scholars involved in planning, write exhibition introductions in very academic language that ordinary people can hardly understand.**

**K:** Hmm, I understand. This comes back to the question of writing textbooks. You have to know how to write for readers who know nothing, rather than for your colleagues. That tests your skill and experience. A curator needs to think about his positioning: does he see himself as a researcher, or as a teacher of the general public?
