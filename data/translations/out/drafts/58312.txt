Wu Guosheng is a professor in the School of Humanities at Tsinghua University and chair of its Department of the History of Science, specializing mainly in the history and philosophy of science. He has taught popular general-education courses on *A General History of Science* (《科学通史》) at both Peking University and Tsinghua, published the bestsellers *The Journey of Science* (《科学的历程》) and *What Is Science?* (《什么是科学》), and his other books include *The Greek Concept of Space* (《希腊空间概念》) and *Lectures on the Philosophy of Technology* (《技术哲学讲演录》).

Han Qide, an academician of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, once commented of *What Is Science?*: "This book considers and explains the nature and origins of science both from within the Western context and from the realities of how Chinese people understand science. It has original insights of its own, and it is accessible yet profound — easy to understand and accept. It is one of the best, most fitting works on the philosophy of science I have seen for Chinese readers today."

Jiang Xiaoyuan, a distinguished professor at Shanghai Jiao Tong University, said of *The Journey of Science* that it "has indeed been influential all along. On the one hand, it is well regarded in academic circles; on the other, it has had a very good influence on some young people, serving in a way as an introductory gateway."

Wu Guosheng was born in September 1964 in Guangji, Hubei. At 15 he entered Peking University as an undergraduate in the Department of Geophysics, then switched to the Department of Philosophy for his master's degree. In 1997 he became the youngest research fellow at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences. A year later he received his PhD in philosophy from the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, studying under the renowned philosopher Ye Xiushan. From 1999 to 2016 he was a professor in Peking University's Department of Philosophy, until he moved to Tsinghua the year before last, where he later became chair of the newly established Department of the History of Science.

For more than two decades, one of Wu Guosheng's persistent pursuits has been to dispel Chinese misconceptions about science, so that people can truly recognize what science is. In his view, many Chinese people today hold three major misconceptions about science. First, they do not distinguish "science" (ke) from "technology" (ji). In Chinese, talk of kexue ("science") easily becomes keji ("science-and-technology"), and when people say keji, what they mean is technology. Second, they attribute an excessively utilitarian color to science: science appears as a tool, not as an end. Third, they overemphasize the literal sense of "branching into disciplines" (fenke).

And in his view, science is an import from the West, and to understand it one must return to the Western context. In Western history, science has taken two successive forms: first Greek science, then modern science. Greek science was non-utilitarian, intrinsic, certain knowledge, born of the Greek pursuit of free human nature. Its typical representatives were deductive mathematics, formal logic, and systematic philosophy. Modern science inherited Greek science's ideal of certainty but added the demands of subjectivity and power, becoming the dominant force of today — with striking practical utility, governing the development of human society and determining humanity's future destiny. Its main representative is mathematical experimental science.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201811142119105pEZU4K2kFXWjOtG.jpg-w600)

His *The Journey of Science* came out in a fourth edition this past August. Twenty-three years have passed since the first edition was originally published. Wu recalls that when it was published in 1995, it sold 20,000 copies — a decent showing at the time. "This book mainly bears witness to the process of popular-science publishing in China over the past 20 years — how it slowly warmed up again from a low point."

Compared with the previous three editions, the fourth edition's biggest change — apart from a few revisions — is the pictures. Many of these come from Wu Guosheng's "science travels" of the past decade. Our interview also began with these travels. Wu feels that, compared with science museums abroad, China's science museums have a very conspicuous problem: they have no historical collections.

"China's so-called science and technology museums — although they're called Science and Technology Museums — actually correspond to what abroad are Science Centers; they follow the science-center model. The science-center model has no collections, only interactive exhibits. I have long called for China to build its own national science museum. The Chinese people's 100-plus-year journey of learning Western science needs something to bear witness to it," Wu told QDaily.

In January 2018, Wu Guosheng and his companions embarked on a 13-day "pilgrimage to Greek science." Taking science as their thread, they visited many places in Greece, including the Parthenon on the Acropolis of Athens, Stagira — the hometown of Aristotle — Samos, the hometown of Pythagoras, and the National Archaeological Museum of Greece.

At the National Archaeological Museum he saw the Antikythera mechanism and was extremely excited, carefully examining every gear and dial — he "couldn't put it down." "The Antikythera mechanism is the first known mechanical computer in human history, believed to be a product of the first or second century BC. Similar devices did not appear again until the year 1400 AD. In other words, in the history of European mechanical technology there is a gap of some 1,500 years; it is a solitary peak," Wu said.

Besides Greece, over the past decade the places Wu Guosheng has visited include Woolsthorpe, Newton's hometown village; Downe village, where Darwin wrote *On the Origin of Species*; Einstein's former home in Princeton; the late-18th-century Watt steam engine displayed at Sydney's Powerhouse Museum; and more.

Wherever he goes, the first things he chooses to see are the ones related to science. For instance, during a week in Paris he never even entered the Louvre; everything he saw had to do with scientists — Descartes, Pasteur, Marie Curie, Buffon, Lamarck.

Wu Guosheng believes these visits have deepened the "excitement and longing" he brings to his scholarly research and have also made his life more complete.

### **To respond to the modernity crisis created by modern mathematical experimental science, we need to return to Greece and invoke the natural-history tradition**

**Q****: Modern mathematical experimental science has achieved great things, but you note that this purely conquering, power-driven kind of science has already revealed its limits to a great degree, triggering a series of modernity crises. To overcome these limits and crises, there are two routes: one is to return to the ideal scientific spirit of ancient Greece, the other is to return to the natural-history (bowuxue) spirit. Although you discuss these at length in your book, the discussion is scattered, so could you give a brief summary: what specific limits has modern mathematical experimental science revealed, and what crises has it brought? And why are returning to the ancient Greek ideal of scientific spirit and to the natural-history spirit so important right now?**

**Wu:** I always feel that Chinese people either misunderstand science or underrate it.

As for science's Greek origins, we are completely ignorant and blind; we don't know that science's true origin was the result of cherishing, defending, and carrying forward a free spirit. Because Chinese culture talks about practicality and results. That is one misconception.

The second is that we underrate the latent possibilities of modern science. We only see that modern science is useful, without seeing that this usefulness is rooted in a very profound system within Western culture. To use the Chinese pair of concepts "ti" (substance) and "yong" (function): modern science is a kind of "substance," not a "function." If you only look at its "function," you are actually underrating it. What kind of "substance" is modern science? In fact it is built upon a system whose core is Christian culture. It takes the realization of human free will as a fundamental goal of modernity. The fundamental goal of modernity is the free play of the human will — which is what Nietzsche called the will to power. What is the result of the will to power? It turns the world into a picture, regarding the world as our object — something for us to defeat, conquer, control, and manipulate — and emphasizes the realization of its own will by objectifying the world. This is the deeply entrenched "substance" of modernity, and it causes modern science to deal with nature in this particular way.

And this is something many Chinese people don't really understand; they only see that modern science is very useful. Why is it so useful? Why has it been pushed to this point? To what point? It is a control of the world with no blind spots, an airtight net, a domination of precise calculation. So in this process, it is no longer a simple matter of "function." In the Chinese idea of "ti-yong," "function" serves to accomplish "substance," and "function" can even be contingent — I can use it this way or that way. But that's not the case: modern science does not allow this use or that use. There is only one way of using it — use nature up completely, use it to death. So the consequences of such a science are inevitable: environmental pollution, ecological imbalance, resource depletion, imbalance among human beings — these problems naturally arise. Simply put, they are the tensions between humanity and nature, and between human beings and one another. Tension becomes an unavoidable consequence of modernity.

Westerners were the first to become aware of this consequence, so they have ways to control and salvage the situation. Chinese people say "when things reach an extreme, they reverse" — "in crisis there is opportunity." The West has similar thinking: Hölderlin said where there is danger, there grows rescue. So in fact the West became aware of the seriousness of this problem earliest, and has developed schemes to overcome these dangers. Where does the Chinese problem lie? First, people are not aware of the danger. Of course, this may be related to the stage of development: you have only just started developing, so you can't see the danger. Like someone who hasn't had enough to eat doesn't yet appreciate the harms of obesity. But some problems come on ferociously — take the past 20 years, when Chinese people rapidly got fat — and so the consequences are even worse than in the West. America has plenty of fat people, but the rates of hypertension, high blood lipids, and stroke are not that high — because it happened gradually, and along the way they gradually chose corresponding medical systems, health-care systems, and hygienic habits, so the problem never worsened that badly.

China is different; China rose abruptly. And when things rise abruptly, the consequences stand out especially. So on the one hand, pollution has caused large numbers of liver-cancer cases — liver cancer is rampant in many rural areas of China; on the other hand, strokes are especially numerous, because in the past people rarely got this disease, or rarely knew its causes. Now, because living conditions are good — having just had enough to eat, people eat with a vengeance — many problems arise. And they arrive not slowly at all; this is no remote, unattainable future — it is already reality. So at this point you can't say "we aren't fully modernized yet, let's not consider these problems yet." In fact you now have to consider this problem more urgently than the West. Environmental pollution, ecological destruction, and the unhealthiness of social life and of people's bodies — China is the most serious case.

We run fast; in the sense of cultural ecology, we have no natural predators. Others have natural predators, so the ecology stays basically balanced. I often say: Western science has grown ever more dominant, but its religion has not weakened in the least, and it still has art, philosophy, and democratic institutions, all checking science. Science is powerful on one side, but it cannot make any great breakthrough. It's different here: no natural predators, no way to check it — so scientism forms, a cultural posture in which science cannot be reflected upon.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181114212635cw4qLB3xS5UTiNf7.jpg-w600)

In fact, my *What Is Science?* has a strong domestic target: it is aimed at the situation in Chinese academic and intellectual circles. So the two routes for saving the crisis that you just mentioned also carry a strong Chinese context, because for the West this problem has long been self-evident. The so-called postmodern voices, represented by Nietzsche, appeared in the 19th century — some even argue that Marx's basic line of thinking is also a postmodern one — so many people in the 20th century talked about returning to Greece. For example, virtue ethics in moral philosophy emerged to get around the shortcomings of utilitarianism or of Kantian ethics of the categorical imperative, and it seeks to return to Greece. There are many examples even in science: Heisenberg, Schrödinger, and Einstein all gradually turned their scientific goals back toward Greece.

What does returning to Greece mean? Science came out of Greece in the first place. After you try a certain path and find you have exhausted that path's possibilities, that you can't go on — even that its negative, terrible consequences have outweighed the original intentions you had when you developed it — then what is the only way? To return to your original state. So in fact Westerners long ago began saving their modernity crisis by returning to Greece. This is not a new route; it's just that we Chinese haven't realized it yet.

But my way of putting it is actually pointed. When Chinese people talk about science, they should consider this route. Of course, if you consider culture, you don't necessarily have to return to Greece, because Chinese culture did not come from Greece and is not a scientific culture. So the full revival of Chinese culture and the resolution of its crisis may have to go back to the pre-Qin era — reinvigorating a culture gone stale by returning to the pre-Qin. Returning to Greece is actually a shared identity of Western culture, and a necessary road for Western modern science to extricate itself. Westerners realized this long ago.

As for natural history (bowuxue), I do have a bit of originality here. And because it's original, people won't necessarily accept it. In the West, no one specializes in lecturing on natural history. The reason no one does is that it never died out among the people — there is a very deep natural-history tradition. This tradition is embodied in many ways. First, it is embodied in popular leisure: to counter urban ailments and the ills of modern civilization, you return to nature, to the fields. The West has always done this; it had this all along — the Romantic movement, the life-reform movement. It always had an institutionalized path back to nature — all kinds of natural-history societies in developed countries, such as the Linnean Society, the Audubon societies, the Leopold societies. Through democratic self-governance, it has already permeated down to the community level.

This tradition was severed in China. Ancient China certainly had landscape poetry, a tradition of intimacy with nature. But this tradition was considered to have nothing to do with science. Chinese people never thought this could be used to check science. This way of putting it is also aimed at the Chinese context. In the West this isn't a problem either — this line of thinking was always there. Natural history as an academic school declined, but the movement did not decline.

The 19th century can be said to be a double turning point for natural history. On one hand, the academy gradually declined: universities no longer had departments of natural history, replacing them with departments of botany, zoology, mineralogy, geology, and so on — it was split into disciplines, and natural history as an overarching umbrella was discarded. So by the 20th century, you could hardly find naturalists; a few individuals like Harvard's Edward O. Wilson or Ernst Mayr count as naturalists. But the movement actually grew among the public in the 19th century, with all kinds of civic organizations. So in a country like the United States, the environmental protection movement emerges easily. America's environmental movement never stopped — from the 19th-century national-park movement, begun under President Theodore Roosevelt, through the 20th century: Leopold articulating the land ethic and writing the natural-history classic *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), to Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* in the 1960s. It has never stopped. It lives among the people, not in academia. In China, the people lack this thing, and it has not combined with the mainstream science movement. So when I raise this matter, I actually have this motive: to enrich the soil of Chinese science culture. Science is three-dimensional; it is a system of ecological interrelations.

Returning to Greece and invoking the natural-history tradition are both aimed at speaking to the Chinese discourse. In an international context, this is nothing new.

### **For Chinese people today, the meaning of science is obviously still mainly the meaning of enlightenment**

**Q****: Against this background, what do you think science means for Chinese people today? What does it signify?**

**Wu:** For Chinese people today, the meaning of science is obviously still mainly the meaning of enlightenment. It marks a primary force in China's transformation from a traditional society into a modern one. This cannot be denied. So all manner of thinking at odds with it actually has problems. Because science has become a representative of the contemporary mainstream, the spokesperson for the modern worldview, the maker of the modern model of social development, and a principal pusher and driver of humanity's future direction. This cannot be denied.

But not denying this does not mean only one kind of science can represent it. Those of us who do the history of science, the cultural history of science — who chase the cultural origins of science — will realize that science is not just a means of achieving some goal (as Chinese culture sees it); it is itself a powerful culture. And since it is a culture, it cannot have just one single branch; it must come as a whole cluster. That cluster is what we need to supplement.

So I want to speak to the current Chinese situation. First, we need to supplement its origins. Where did it come from? This we don't know. Many Chinese people think that as long as you want to eat, you have to do science. That is completely wrong. It underrates science, holding that people must survive, that survival requires science, that science is something every people has. Not so! Wanting to eat does not require doing science. Doing science requires a unique cultural identity. That is the Greek spirit. This is what I have emphasized for many years. At the source, you have to grasp it. Without this, your science won't be done right, won't last long, can't be sustained — you cannot truly follow or catch up with the rhythm and pace of modern society's development.

Second, science has many branches; it is a very rich ecosystem, so we need to supplement the natural-history branch. This branch has another advantage: it can combine with the finest essence of ancient Chinese culture. If you only look at the mathematical-science branch, ancient China has little to boast of. The more you work on it, the more pitiable you find ancient China. Have today's science-and-engineering undergraduates read the history of ancient Chinese technology, and the consequence is that everyone comes away thinking the ancient Chinese were hopeless. Viewing ancient China through the lens of mathematical experiment, you find it crude, rudimentary, imprecise, logically lax — all common-sense-level stuff. But if you put on the natural-history lens, you discover that ancient China may be very inspiring — the lore concerning heaven, earth, people, agriculture, and medicine can be instructive for correcting certain excesses of modernization. Conversely, insisting on how many "firsts" the ancient Chinese achieved — say, the earliest discovery of magnetic declination — what's the use? I don't see what inspiration that holds for the present. You are merely maintaining a vanity; it has no enlightening significance whatsoever.

**Q****: Next year is the 100th anniversary of the May Fourth Movement, and one of its most important slogans was "science," then called "Mr. Science." Looking back over these 100 years at the fate of science in China, what are your major reflections? Is there anything worth cherishing and reflecting on today?**

**Wu:** I think that since the May Fourth era, science has remained something in the sense of a tool. From May Fourth onward, or even earlier, Chinese people's esteem for Western science was still mostly understood from the standpoint of Chinese culture itself, not from science's spiritual essence. That is, the reason Chinese people value science has mostly to do with saving the nation, strengthening the military, and changing a backward appearance — wielding it as a tool. I also consider this Confucian thinking. China's modern scientists were all Confucians; it was the Confucian family-and-nation sentiment that drove modern China's scientific progress. So I say Confucianism is not necessarily anti-science. Confucians may not understand science, but their family-and-nation sentiment could drive generation after generation of intellectuals with Confucian feeling to devote themselves to scientific research. That is one main reflection.

That generation of scientists typically carried the family-and-nation sentiment of "I will devote my body to the country." The founders of the atomic and hydrogen bomb programs were all of this ilk. The motivation for doing science was not curiosity, not the impulse to pursue truth, not an uncontainable wonder at the mysteries of the universe — but the misfortune of the family and the nation, the need to use science and technology to save a perilous situation. That was the main motivation. And this motivation indeed accomplished certain things. Don't just talk about those people — take Yang Zhenning's generation: what Yang has been proudest of all his life is that winning the Nobel Prize changed the psychology of Chinese inferiority. He said that was the greatest achievement — rather than having discovered something about the material world that delighted him beyond measure. People like Wang Ganchang, and Qian Sanqiang, Qian Xuesen — all followed this line. So for the scientists of the entire 20th century, this was the main problem.

Now, this problem is good, but it is not enough. Doing things this way keeps science forever in a state of being a tool, without changing the traditional attitude toward science. That is unfavorable for turning science directly into a kind of cultural soil. So I think the Chinese people of the future — and future science thought, science policy, and scientist policy — should actually understand science from the angle of cultivating free people. Studying science lets you understand more deeply: What is democracy? What is equality? What is truth? What is freedom? Understanding science from this angle is what truly, properly brings science into a nation's movement of renewal and rebirth. Science today is not considered to have any role in renewing the national spirit. The renewal of the national spirit is still left to those Confucians pondering "Zhou is an old state, but its mandate is new," trying to do this with "benevolence, righteousness, propriety, wisdom, and trustworthiness." I don't oppose them either, but you have to let science in. I think those with real insight — like Professor Zhang Xianglong — understand this point well. Zhang Xianglong is a Confucian, but he holds that any ideological movement or social-reform movement today that does not address science and technology is nonsense, is muddleheaded. Because what is the great trend of this society? You haven't seen it clearly.

So if we reflect on May Fourth, we find that this is the main problem. Science has not truly entered the movement to renew our national soul, our national spirit.

**Q****: You just mentioned freedom, and it reminded me that you once said that for Westerners, to become human is to become a free person. In the Greek era, to apprehend the logic of the ideas was to realize freedom; today, to realize one's own will is to realize freedom. I am curious: from your personal standpoint, what is freedom? What counts as having realized freedom?**

**Wu:** In fact, over the course of more than a thousand years, the two freedoms — the Christian and the Greek — did a great deal of merging. I personally believe that true freedom should be freedom in the sense of reason.

Freedom in the sense of the assertion of the will may be embodied mostly in the domain of ethics and morality. In ethics and morality, all compliance with external rules presupposes your ability to comply, to choose. This is what makes one a person of moral and ethical capacity. Freedom resides in this domain. But it is in the inward understanding with which one treats the world and treats life that freedom's real substance lies. So I still rather esteem the Greek kind of freedom; that freedom is more fundamental. Moreover, freedom of the will is something derivative, a side branch. The root of freedom is still the Greek thing — the so-called pure self, a hard, inward logic. That is freedom in the true sense.

And this is something extremely lacking in our culture. This lack is caused by what Chinese culture calls the organic worldview. The organic worldview blurs every individual — there is no issue of the individual. Chinese culture is extremely deficient in individual consciousness. So in the modern society dominated by science, lacking the individual and talking only about the organism, the whole — that is a very big problem. And without this, even freedom of the will has nothing to rest on. So in my view, the Greek kind of freedom is probably the more fundamental one.

### **Humanity and technology are actually a process of co-evolution**

**Q****: In recent years, it feels like more and more people are advancing a kind of "AI threat theory." They believe the current development of artificial intelligence will affect issues such as human privacy, the job market, and weapons of mass destruction — and might even destroy humanity. What do you think of such claims?**

**Wu:** My view differs from Professor Jiang Xiaoyuan's; he is a strong advocate of this — he says AI is the main calamity of humanity's future.

I believe, first, that AI does not appear suddenly; it can't pop out in a single day. As a technology created by humanity, AI has its various bugs dealt with in the very process of creation. Because when you innovate a technology there are many aspects unfavorable to you, and when you optimize it, you must take these unfavorable aspects into account. Take the car: does it have downsides? It does. It goes too fast, so it is hard to control and easy to crash; the people killed in car accidents every year rival the death toll of a war. But you're used to it; you don't stop riding in cars because riding them risks accidents. When humanity created the car, it did not create the car alone. Cars did not become fast in a single day; moreover, cars have speed-limit mechanisms, speeding on the highway is illegal, drunk driving carries criminal liability, and so on.

So the car has become part of culture — it is a culture, not merely a technology. As a technology, the car's threat to humanity is very obvious. It kills people in an instant, wiping out a whole carload — how terrifying! Human reflexes are weak, yet a car is so fast: reaching 100 km/h takes just 8 seconds, even 5 or 4 seconds — humanity simply can't keep up. These are all problems. Yet people feel there's nothing seriously wrong. Why no big problem? Because car culture has been developing for over a century, with many auxiliary supports built up.

By the same logic, the field of AI will not produce its technology in a single day either; an AI culture will form as well. And within such a culture there will be many constraints. People will tell you: a car is not just an engine — it also has a brake system. Brake systems have gone through many generations, and braking is now easy. So in the course of AI's development, many problems people cannot foresee will crop up, and new technologies will immediately emerge to cover them, to check the problem. So I don't think AI will amount to anything in the future. I don't think it differs in any qualitative way from anything else.

As for automata — automata have always existed: air conditioners, refrigerators, for instance. Some people will invoke self-learning; actually a car has a certain intelligence too. Because intelligence is, after all, something input by people. You decide how much to give it. AI has this issue too. Of course it has to learn, and such things may appear. But AI, as a machine, as a product of human technology, is always a human product and always remains subject to human control. Sure, it will have problems; if the problems are big, humanity will devote more energy to remedying that part; if they are small, we slowly ignore them — no matter. So I think AI will be the same. Because what you imagine now has not been realized, and learning is a gradual process. I'm not worried about this point.

Google's or Baidu's search systems used to do pure big data; now they can classify things and have some intelligence, but still can't get there in one step. So I think technology, like an organism, is also an evolutionary process. It is a process of humanity and technology co-evolving. Since it is evolution, nothing catastrophic will happen. What does evolution mean? Things that go too far get eliminated. If you don't go too far, you survive. A species that flaunts itself too much generally gets eliminated. What does flaunting mean? One: you eat too much, finishing off everyone else's food — and then you yourself have nothing left to eat. Two: otherwise you taste especially good, so others eat you. So I also believe that something like AI, if it had that many downsides, would have been killed off long ago.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201811142134287X2G6VPuhCWvqDzi.jpg-w600)

**Q****: I think this may involve our fundamental attitude toward technology. Traditionally, people are either optimists or pessimists about technology. But your view, it seems, actually amounts to a third path: technology is a transcendence of the human — a self-transcendence of our physical bodies. Could you elaborate on this?**

**Wu:** Technological optimism says the human can overpower technology. Technological pessimism says technology will overpower the human. The shared premise of these two ideas is that technology and humanity are "two separate skins." Once technology is made, either humanity is subject to it, or humanity controls it. Neither is true. Actually, what my third path means is: humanity and technology are not two separate skins; they are a single thing.

It looks as if technology is outside us, constituting an inner-outer relationship. Actually it is a mirror relationship. So from when humans first knapped stone tools — stone tools seem to be our exterior? Wrong! Every act of knapping a stone tool is a massage of your cerebral cortical folds. That is to say, when humanity makes external things, it is simultaneously shaping its own interior.

Humanity and technology are actually a process of co-evolution. The two are one. Whatever kind of technology exists, that kind of human nature exists; whatever kind of human nature exists, that kind of technology exists.

The co-evolution of humanity and technology is a differentiated co-evolution. This differentiation sometimes appears as technology going first — the things it does catch people off guard, producing embarrassment, human awkwardness. For example, a car, with one slip — vroom! — runs off. You meant to step on the brake but stepped on the accelerator instead. But you can't say the car is out of control. Loss of control is the exception, but that it can lose control shows there is differentiation. So sometimes technology walks ahead of the human. But sometimes technology walks behind, and it restricts people. A person who likes or is used to traditional technology will reject new technology.

Technology's going first or lagging behind is actually part of the inherent meaning of humanity-technology co-evolution. Just as, to evolve, you first need new species. Where do new species come from? From mutation. Mutation is a kind of going-first. Lagging is the current living environment selecting which genes, which species, survive. So this is a pair of contradictions. It doesn't matter. This contradiction is a basic mode of operation of our world. So the third path is no other path; it is really just the question of humanity and technology co-evolving.

In the sense of the philosophy of technology, this means you cannot treat technology as the Other of the human. If you insist on looking at it that way, then one can only say that the human originally exists in the manner of an Other. How do humans differ from animals? An animal is forever itself, while a human is forever human being plus something — humans have to have a bit more. That extra bit, you could say, is technology. Without technology it won't do — then you're not human.

So Professor Deng Xiaomang has a formulation I find quite good. He says: the human is not the animal that creates tools, nor the animal that uses tools; the human is the animal that carries tools. "Carries" usually means holding them in the hand; it can also mean wearing them on the body — and even stark naked, you are still carrying technology. You are always together with technology, never separated; what gets separated is only certain individual, concrete technologies. That separation is called differentiation. Of course, that differentiation quickly turns into a new mode of co-evolution.

### **The problem with traditional Chinese medicine is not merely a scientific one; it involves culture, politics, and national sentiment**

**Q****: You mentioned that ancient Chinese medicine and pharmacology actually also belong to the natural-history tradition — that its pharmaceutical part is mainly the bencao (materia medica) tradition within the earth-science branch of natural history, while its medical part can be classified as human natural history. That is looking at Chinese medicine from a historical angle. I am curious: from a practical standpoint, what is your attitude toward traditional Chinese medicine? How do you view it?**

**Wu:** The problem with traditional Chinese medicine is rather tricky. It's not just a scholarly question anymore; it has become a matter of national sentiment (laughs). So it's hard to talk about. To put it bluntly, I myself don't believe in traditional Chinese medicine. If I'm ill, I certainly won't see a TCM doctor. In TCM there is only one medicine I believe in — berberine (laughs).

Objectively speaking, medicine is complicated; it values results. Whatever your principles, just cure me. So the problem of TCM is not a purely scientific one. Modern Western medicine is based on mathematical experimental science, which stresses universality, repeatability, and operability. TCM fails all three. It has no universality — for instance, TCM emphasizes climate: effective in spring, ineffective in winter; and it varies from person to person — treating men but not women, treating the old but not the young. Of course, those who are cured feel it worked. But can you distinguish whether the disease cured itself or was cured by some means? That can't be made clear. Second, TCM has never done the double-blind, controlled trials, or the exclusion of psychological factors that modern science requires. So in fact it does not fall within the purely scientific domain.

And fundamentally, medicine involves a person's understanding of disease. What counts as illness? The understanding of disease is culturally dependent. Science, in principle, has no cultural dependency: gravity cannot be said to suit landlords but not poor peasants, to suit Chinese but not English. Medicine can have this dependency — conditions like shanghuo ("internal heat") or zuoyuezi (postpartum confinement), ailments closely tied to Chinese culture — you just can't say.

Some ailments are also culturally dependent. There is a lot of research on this. For example, where women's rights were not prominent, some doctors considered many women's illnesses not to be illnesses. With the rise of feminism, many things that used to be considered non-illnesses became illnesses — postpartum depression, for instance, or depression during menstruation. In the past these were not illnesses; now, with feminist ideology, they count as illnesses. What does being ill mean? Being ill means you enjoy public medical care and legal leave. Not an illness, then there is no legal leave, and it cannot be brought into the medical-insurance system. This in turn touches on politics.

So the problem of medicine is not a purely scientific one. This needs to be made clear.

As for TCM, it has a strong cultural dependency. If you look at it entirely through the eyes of modern medicine, TCM's level is about the same as that of traditional Western medicine. Westerners today would never compare modern medicine with traditional Western medicine — they say that is outdated, nothing to discuss. Of course, there are individuals who, on individual occasions, still believe in that stuff — it exists, but it is not the mainstream after all. TCM should be viewed the same way: it too should be regarded as the traditional medicine that existed before modern medicine emerged, and cannot be compared with modern medicine. It is like comparing spears and broadswords with the atomic bomb. What is the point? Right now we Chinese make exactly this mistake, always comparing TCM with modern medicine — the two are incommensurable. Westerners don't compare; why must you compare? This is related to our cultural situation: you are a backward nation, a late-coming nation, and perhaps the sentiment is hard to get over. And on top of that, medicine is a field that values results and personal experience, and mixing that in makes it all especially muddled.

So one's view of medicine cannot be a purely scientific view. My talk of natural history is in fact neutral; it cannot become the mainstream. My meaning is: if we also regard TCM as natural history in a certain sense, that too means it cannot become the mainstream — it is not science in the mathematical sense; it does not meet the requirements of mainstream science. Modern medicine meets mainstream science's requirements. So in what sense does TCM count as medicine? In the natural-history sense — just that meaning.

**Q****: Your books basically all revolve around three words: "nature," "science," and "technology." I wonder if you would be willing to try summarizing, in one sentence or one passage, the relationship among the three?**

**Wu:** Science originated from the invention of "nature." China has no concept of "nature"; "nature" is a Greek invention. Heidegger said modern technology is the completed form of metaphysics. You could also say modern technology is a certain ultimate form of science.

These two sentences basically sum up my two ideas. First, science originated in Greece, in the pursuit of freedom. Second, where does the danger of modern technology lie? Modern technology has exhausted the possibilities created by modern science. Of the so-called dangers, I have a phrase: the discovery of nature and the death of nature. Nature has now lost its autonomy. In Greece, nature was a domain of autonomy, but modern science, based on the Christian idea of creation, produced a strange combination. On the one hand, it is autonomous, independent of human will, with objective laws; on the other hand, everything in it is assembled by humans through experiment. The so-called laws of nature in modern times should really be called laws of un-nature.

The topic of nature can string together both the origin of science and the danger of contemporary technology.

Late Heidegger attached great importance to this matter; he was writing on *Physics*. He said that in Western culture, the word "nature" actually plays a hidden role. What does hidden mean? It means people can no longer make it out; the role has become very blurred. It seems that when we speak of nature today, we have specialized it, made it a marginal topic. In philosophy today we generally speak of justice, beauty, and such things; nature is rarely discussed. When nature does come up, people think of what scientists deal with — but scientists don't talk much about it either. Heidegger saw this very accurately, very deeply: he said it always plays a hidden regulatory role. He said physics is the study of nature (physis). Philosophy is metaphysics. What is metaphysics? It is "meta-physics" — what comes after physics. So throughout the 2,000 years of Western intellectual history, philosophy has consciously or unconsciously followed nature's hints. Whatever hints nature offers, philosophy goes there. Put plainly: the history of metaphysics, or of philosophy, is in fact a history of following nature. So he held that nature's being forgotten and marginalized does not mean it ceases to act on reality; perhaps the more it acts, the more it is hidden. What truly acts is often very hidden.

*(Note: In English, "physics" derives first of all from the ancient Greek "φύσις" (physis), whose original meaning is "nature." **metaphysics** is usually translated into Chinese as xingshangxue — the study of being and the essence of things. It is a category of philosophical inquiry, regarded as "first philosophy" and "the fundamental question of philosophy.")*
