"People need plenty of surface-level bustle and prosperity, things that make them laugh right away. But why do you read books and travel? Sometimes you need things with a different rhythm and depth, things that nourish and expand your experience of life."

When asked how Vertebra Theatre selects its productions, Li Qianpeng’s answer is simple: "First, it has to move me."

Since its founding in 2016, Vertebra Theatre has become a recognizable brand among theater lovers. Li Qianpeng is its founder and artistic director.

In January 2019, Vertebra Theatre’s latest work, *Father's Bed* 《爸爸的床》, premiered in Shanghai, with Wang Xueqi and Yang Yitong playing a father and daughter. Three months after his wife’s death, the father wants to start a new life immediately and remarry soon, decorating the room according to his new wife’s ideas, while the daughter resists fiercely, desperately trying to keep the old things in the family home.

For most of the play, the father and daughter stand on opposite sides of the stage, communicating by telephone from a distance. The two actors are surrounded by cardboard box after cardboard box. In one scene: the father leans against a box, speaking into the handset with effortless ease, while on the other side of the stage the daughter boxes as she demands that he not throw away the old things—the contrast between his calm composure and her breathlessness is striking. By the end of the play, the two push over the boxes stacked on the stage, a moment that also symbolizes the final reconciliation between father and daughter.

Looking back at why she turned this Dutch script into a Vertebra Theatre production, Li Qianpeng [wrote](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/8D8SRCQ8IyuTH7_H70OImA): "From beginning to end, my heart was swept by a mix of emotions, constantly shifting like the tide, but always staying at a very intense emotional level… As long as you have parents, as long as you have family, as long as you have ever felt the warmth of emotion, you should come see this play. For different people, it will touch you in different ways."

Li Qianpeng values these psychological feelings that come from life experience. She never hesitates to express her emotions and is willing to act on their momentum. "You have to feel it; don’t judge it rationally or rush to a conclusion." This has become the core of her understanding of theater. And in order to express her own experience of theater, Li Qianpeng founded Vertebra Theatre.

Before founding Vertebra Theatre in 2016, Li Qianpeng was a teacher at the Shanghai Theatre Academy. In 1995 she tested into Shanghai from Jinan, and after graduation stayed on to teach, working as an acting instructor ever since.

China’s theater circle is small, with practitioners concentrated in Beijing and Shanghai. Beijing practitioners are blunt, usually convinced their own shows are the best, and mutual criticism is common. Shanghai is the opposite: it values being smooth in dealing with people, considerate in handling affairs, and maintaining at least a surface-level harmony. Li Qianpeng is an anomaly in Shanghai. People in the circle all know that when she watches a play, she habitually buys a seat in the corner, and if she isn’t satisfied with what’s on stage within ten minutes, she leaves immediately.

When she criticizes, Li Qianpeng pulls no punches. At first she would still worry about social needs and other people’s face, but later realized she simply couldn’t hold it in. "It’s really too awful," she says. "If you ask me to speak, I can’t lie. I think this is the most basic, most basic bottom line for a person—honesty."

Later, people around Li Qianpeng told her, "Stop criticizing others all the time. What you say makes sense, but why don’t you make one yourself—how about you do it?" It happened that, after more than twenty years of watching plays, Li Qianpeng felt there were fewer and fewer plays at home that could give her pleasure, and after turning thirty she had kept thinking, "How should I live? What’s good and what’s not? What’s interesting?" Searching back and forth, Li Qianpeng decided that what she most wanted to do was create some theater that she herself thought was good, and gain some pleasure from it. When Vertebra Theatre was founded in 2016, Li Qianpeng had just turned 40.

Vertebra Theatre’s first production was *The Open Couple*, a work created in 1983 by Italian playwright and theater director Dario Fo. In the play, a married couple agrees to an open relationship, but discovers that actually practicing an open relationship is not as easy as they imagined. *The Open Couple* met the standard Li Qianpeng set for herself: "First, it moves me. And it connects with people’s current needs, memories, or experiences."

The play premiered in May 2016. Li Qianpeng handed directorial duties to Li Minyuan, a Taiwanese director who had studied and worked in France and now teaches in the Department of Dramatic Literature at the Shanghai Theatre Academy. He changed the original’s fixed ending into an open one; he felt that living environments and atmospheres still [differ somewhat](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/m8mdzXv4U5oJI7ajZEtgzA) from place to place. During a post-show talk, he [answered](http://www.kankanews.com/a/2016-05-29/0037538048.shtml) audience questions about the ending: "Are you saying this is love? I can’t find an answer. Human nature is more complicated."

Another major change was that the originally relatively realistic set was replaced with a more modern arrangement of box-like steps, with black as the dominant color. For the actors, there were almost no props to work with, so physical expression and some improvisation became the production’s biggest highlights.

After *The Open Couple*, Vertebra Theatre’s early works included *Lungs*, in which a couple discusses whether to have a child, and *Poison* 《毒》, about a couple who reunite years after losing their only son. These three plays formed Vertebra Theatre’s first 2017 season, "Shall We Talk," all of them concerned with how people communicate in intimate relationships.

![Poster for the "Shall We Talk" season](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/2019041710481885uHitapW3vfmUKl.jpg-w600)

This left some audience members with the impression that Vertebra Theatre is passionate about the subtle and complex intimate relationships between people, complemented by minimalist yet thoughtfully designed stage art. As in *Lungs*, Vertebra Theatre designed a set with two incandescent lights and a rotating seesaw, symbolizing the couple’s coming together and separation, the shifting balance of voice, and the changing closeness of their relationship.

*Lungs* promotional video

But before long, Vertebra Theatre’s repertoire grew more diverse. In the second half of 2017, *War* 《战》 used movement and physicality to portray work, family, and love, mostly in montage-like fragments. The 2018 production *Bartleby, the Scrivener* adapted a short story by Herman Melville, author of *Moby-Dick*, about the courage to refuse and to say "no." And *Momo* 《默默》, which Vertebra Theatre was rehearsing in 2019, was adapted from the famous German children’s novel *Momo*, exploring the theme of time.

What these productions have in common is that they are all connected to real life. In choosing *Bartleby, the Scrivener*, Li Qianpeng believes that "in real workplaces, people don’t have many chances to say ‘no.’ Precisely because they can’t say ‘no,’ people may lose part of themselves." And Vertebra Theatre’s attitude has always been: "We face reality. We don’t run away."

Li Qianpeng’s way of choosing plays is based on extensive experience watching theater abroad.

Starting in 2003, she frequently traveled to New York, London, and arts and theater festivals in other European countries for study. Last February she went to Latvia for a student’s wedding and saw eight plays in seven days; on one day she watched shows back-to-back in the afternoon and evening, visiting commercial theaters, underground venues, and even university drama clubs. The language barrier was no obstacle. Before entering, she would do some homework, looking at what the story was about, what the director had done before, and what their style was like. "Dialogue is important, but contemporary theater vocabulary is very rich—there’s a lot to watch for."

![Li Qianpeng watching a play in Latvia](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190417110213H63QCGWcK8mgPv5U.jpg-w600)

When she sees something suitable, Li Qianpeng tries to buy the rights and produce it herself, as an original creation. "Sometimes (some domestic practices) are like hot-pot lamb: bring it in, dip it, stage a Chinese version with the same blocking. The director doesn’t even need to come; send an assistant and copy it. That’s not creation. We’ll never make anything good that way."

Gradually, Vertebra Theatre built what Li Qianpeng calls a library. "For example, if I’m interested in this subject, or this novel, I’ll remember it… The plays are all in our heads. As long as I’ve taken a liking to a project, I won’t forget it in this lifetime."

Her experience watching plays also helped Li Qianpeng find many collaborators. Boris von Poser, the director of *Bartleby, the Scrivener*, is one of her "acquaintances." She had seen this German director’s adaptation of Pushkin’s *The Queen of Spades* and was deeply impressed. She wrote in her Moments feed: "A tiny theater converted from an apartment building, and they dared to take on Pushkin—and it was incredibly sophisticated…"

"We’ve seen so much theater. This director’s style left a deep impression on me. Maybe when I’m looking for content on a certain subject, I’ll think he might be right for it."

Ultimately, under Boris von Poser’s direction, *Bartleby, the Scrivener* took this form: the rear of the stage was lined with screens symbolizing office cubicles, which also served as curtains for projected multimedia images. In *Bartleby, the Scrivener*, Bartleby, who refuses everything, says very little; the lawyer employer played by Wang Chuanjun is the real protagonist on stage, opening with a fifteen-minute monologue that tells the audience this strange and peculiar story.

Like *Bartleby, the Scrivener*, the combination of a European director, a European script, and Chinese actors has become Vertebra Theatre’s standard production model. Li Qianpeng prefers European works. American theater, she feels, is crude, simplistic, and surface-level, while in China she can’t find good enough scripts or imaginative directors.

By the time Vertebra Theatre reached its eighth play, *Father's Bed*, some audience members felt aesthetic fatigue. *Father's Bed* seemed to return to the model of the earliest works, *The Open Couple* and *Lungs*, and some outside criticism seemed unable to escape the impression left by the first three plays. On this basis, people tried to sum up Vertebra Theatre’s style: solid text, delicate dialogue, attention to relationships between people, and minimalist yet highly symbolic stage design.

She flatly rejects labels like these. "Have you seen all eight plays? If you haven’t seen one of them, you can’t say that." She is even somewhat angry: "You haven’t felt it; it’s a crude, simplistic categorization. I have to say, you’re too rational. You’ve lost the ability to feel life."

Over three years, Vertebra Theatre has also cultivated a loyal audience with a more open mindset, one that has accepted Li Qianpeng’s view of theater. "In the audience group set up after a performance, some people ask, what is the theme of this play? What is it telling us? The creators in the group usually don’t need to respond, because other audience members will answer: don’t rush to identify a theme; you have to feel it, don’t rush to a conclusion."

Yet refusing ready-made rationality is not the same as refusing considered criticism. If not handled well, emphasizing feeling can sometimes sound like a way of evading criticism.

Li Qianpeng has never directly answered what kind of criticism is acceptable. She emphasizes that audiences are allowed not to like something, but denying a work’s existence is frightening. "Some viewers say they especially like *Poison* or *Father's Bed* because they like that style of veteran professional actors, and then they criticize *Lungs* for having no character development. But did we stage *Lungs* wrong? I don’t think so. Many other audiences love it. That’s the texture of contemporary theater."

Vertebra Theatre’s performances in Beijing often encounter more extreme reactions than in Shanghai, and these come not only from audiences but also from industry insiders. After *Father's Bed* was performed in Beijing, someone cursed the play out in the lobby, feeling that the play had too many lines and shouldn’t use various stage techniques; it should be a cold, aloof show where the actors simply speak the lines and that’s it.

Later, learning that this audience member was also a theater professional, Li Qianpeng called it a loss of composure. She challenged his view: "Then may I ask, is this a script reading? If you don’t like it, you can buy the rights and stage your own version; we don’t have an exclusive."

"We don’t always have to agree, but we can all exist and communicate with each other." Li Qianpeng explains her view from a position close to multiculturalism: "Some people feel that in their own world they can judge you, using paranoid and extreme language. That’s not a healthy state for society. It’s different from having personality. Respecting others and acknowledging their existence is different from having personality. No matter how much personality you have, you can’t disrespect others and their existence."

Li Qianpeng’s pickiness and stubbornness make it hard for her to accept others telling her what to do. For three years since Vertebra Theatre was founded, all funding has come from Li Qianpeng personally; it has not accepted any outside investment.

"Many investors say they’re not in a hurry and are willing to take the long view. But over that long period, they don’t understand theater, and you still have to make them understand what it is. Because they’ll definitely meddle, definitely point fingers at the investment and the product. That’s exhausting and diverts my energy."

"What’s scarier is another kind of idealistic investor. He always thinks he likes this. But he’s not really professional, and he doesn’t really understand it, yet he thinks he does. How scary is that? I don’t need to say more—a half-baked expert."

![Li Qianpeng (right) in Latvia](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904171114517MSgYKuaVHcmRTyO.jpg-w600)

Throughout those three years, Li Qianpeng constantly had the feeling that "we might fold." Actors, producers, stage managers—every link could fail to find the right person. Besides, funding was also a problem. Vertebra Theatre’s good fortune, however, was that its first play, *The Open Couple*, started turning a profit very early. The whole play also had a lighter atmosphere, and the theme of open relationships could attract many audiences.

But *The Open Couple*’s profitability didn’t mean it could cover all operating personnel and the production costs of new plays. What put Vertebra Theatre on track was the reputation built up by production after production. During the first run, like all domestic original plays, Vertebra Theatre performed almost exclusively in Beijing and Shanghai. But in the second and third years, more and more second- and third-tier cities began thinking of Vertebra Theatre when organizing theater or arts festivals, and this year *Poison* has tour dates in four or five such cities.

Starting in February, Vertebra Theatre has been busy preparing its first large-venue production, *Momo* 《默默》. The director, Gil Mehmert, is from Germany, and the lead is Wan Qian, who won the Golden Horse Award for Best Supporting Actress for *Paradise in Service*. The play will premiere at Shanghai’s Majestic Theatre from April 30 to May 1 and is currently in rehearsal—another new challenge for Vertebra Theatre.

![Rehearsal photo for *Momo*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904171111520nfhIJVr7aYB6Q5C.jpg-w600)

From 2016 until now, Li Qianpeng feels she has changed too. She says that while she has become more demanding about theater, she also admits that "now I compromise more than before. Personally, I’ve become more tolerant of many things, and my ways of handling things are more flexible." The most obvious sign is that when she encounters a bad play, she accepts that she can simply not talk about it: "At least I’m not telling lies."

Occasionally, Li Qianpeng also shows a tolerant attitude. While criticizing some audience members for being closed-minded, she says, "You still have to respect what people are ready to accept right now; you can’t ignore the audience’s level of engagement with theater."

She believes that audiences’ aesthetic levels are tied to the circumstances of their lives. "What you saw first, what you are watching now, what you will see—it’s all fate. Whatever comes into your life is your fate. If from the start you see a really good work, you might feel disappointed for a long time afterward, because nothing else measures up."
