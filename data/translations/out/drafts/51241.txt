"I just feel that a creator has to keep his life simple. The truth is, everyone's mental energy and attention are limited, just like a human life — you can only do your best to spend your energy on the things you want to spend it on."

Chu Teh-yung, a Taiwanese cartoonist.

On the mainland, many people first came to know him through a TV drama called *Pink Ladies*. Hailed as the pinnacle of mainland idol dramas, the show was adapted from Chu Teh-yung's four-panel comic strip *Astringent Girl* (《涩女郎》), serialized in 1993, and people can probably still recall the four women on screen — each with a distinct look and personality: the Marriage Maniac, the Charmer, the Tomboy, and Ha Mei.

Chu Teh-yung first became famous in Taiwan back in 1985. Fresh out of college, he was commissioned by the China Times, one of Taiwan's largest newspapers at the time. He handed in a set of *Double Firecrackers* (《双响炮》) to the paper and then rushed off to serve his military service on a remote, closed-off island. Six months later, his four-panel strips were a sensation across Taiwan, and Chu Teh-yung — a film-directing graduate returning from his service — became the cartoonist Chu Teh-yung. He went on to create *The Vinegar Tribe* (《醋溜族》) and *Astringent Girl* (《涩女郎》), becoming Taiwan's most popular cartoonist second only to Hayao Miyazaki.

![Chu Teh-yung's breakout work *Double Firecrackers* (《双响炮》)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319151330hY1GPgqyxctAVQDU.jpg-w600)

In 1999, Chu Teh-yung came to the mainland, and Sanlian Lifeweek began serializing his comics. Soon, *Double Firecrackers* and *Astringent Girl* had sold more than 10 million copies on the mainland. The following three or four years were the peak of mainland media rushing to run Chu Teh-yung columns: Sanlian Lifeweek, *Girlfriend* (《女友》), Chutian Metropolis Daily, Shanghai Weekly (《申江服务导报》), New Weekly, and more than ten other newspapers and magazines competed to publish his work. On the "Comic Writers Rich List" sub-list of the 2011 China Writers Rich List (6th edition), Chu Teh-yung became the "richest" name, with 61.9 million yuan in total royalties over ten years.

Chu Teh-yung's four-panel strips, along with his humor and his signature "Chu philosophy," shape how people see the world in a light, playful way. *Double Firecrackers* is about marriage, *Astringent Girl* about relations between men and women, *About the Thing Called Work* (《关于上班这件事》) about office workers, *Everything Is Happening* (《什么事都在发生》) about the problems of life, *Absolute Kid* (《绝对小孩》) a return to childhood, and *Everyone's Sick* (《大家都有病》) a critique of consumer society — his cartoons are always depicting or satirizing society.

![The TV drama *Pink Ladies*, adapted from *Astringent Girl* (《涩女郎》)](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319151456jygORnLiXa4PBrF7.jpg-w600)

Yet fame does not seem to have changed Chu Teh-yung very much; he has largely kept up a pace of one book every two years. In a 2011 [interview with Huaxi Metropolis Daily](http://news.163.com/11/1122/03/7JED1G4M00014AED.html), Chu Teh-yung said that when it came to adult humor comics he had already reached a level that satisfied him, and that he would cut back on his current comic output going forward; what he wanted to do next was turn comics into art. "In many European countries comics have already become works of art, and I hope to head down that road."

That held until 2013, when he released his latest work to date, *Everyone's Sick 2: Falling in Love with an Idiot* (《大家都有病2：跟笨蛋一起谈恋爱》). In the years since, Chu Teh-yung really has published no new serialized comics and has rarely appeared in the media. The inactive Chu Teh-yung gradually faded from public view.

During these years of "disappearing," Chu Teh-yung has occasionally staged exhibitions, but he spends most of his time at home with his wife and their cats, painting for a few hours each day and living the simple life he pursues. With the decline of print media, his work simply moved from newspaper columns to Facebook and Weibo, with a new strip posted every day.

This year, Japan's Silent Manga Audition world competition came to China — a major event for the country's comics scene. The judges are all industry heavyweights: Nobuhiko Horie, Tsukasa Hojo, Tetsuo Hara… and the list also included the name of Chu Teh-yung, who came this time to encourage young Chinese cartoonists to enter.

At the opening ceremony, Chu Teh-yung said: "After so many years in comics, there is one question people keep asking me: do Chinese people have creativity? And do Chinese people have a sense of humor? I think this competition gives us a chance to see whether Chinese people have sufficient creativity and humor."

In this exclusive interview with QDaily, Chu Teh-yung talked about what he has been thinking about lately, about how the decline of print media and the internet age have affected him, about his worries over excessive commercialization eroding creators, and about his plans for what comes next.

Chu Teh-yung says he may come to Shanghai again this May — and that time, it will be for *Absolute Kid 3*.

![A four-panel strip from Chu Teh-yung's comic *Absolute Kid* (《绝对小孩》). Source: Chu Teh-yung's Weibo account](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803191532000HcI1Y6oZAMznC97.jpg-w600)

**Q = QDaily**

**Z = Chu Teh-yung**

### Not Vanishing, Not Running Dry — Just Going Off to Live

**Q: What has been puzzling or occupying you lately?**

**Z:** Lately I've been thinking about how the rest of my life should unfold — deciding what kind of life I actually want to live.

I think life is easily led astray by many things. These days I spend a fair amount of time thinking about how much time to allocate to my work and how much to my life, and what actually makes a life worthwhile. By "worthwhile" I don't mean money or material things — I mean how I should choose.

**Q: Have you reached a conclusion?**

**Z:** Still thinking, but I don't think there will be any major changes.

The truth is, over all these years I haven't actually spent that much time on my career. I could have published twice as many books as I have, and I could even have adapted my own work — especially since IP adaptations have been so popular these past few years; a lot of people approached me, hoping I would take part myself, whether as a screenwriter or whatever. I trained in film directing, so I could actually have directed my own films, and in terms of wealth I would probably have a lot more. But I have never done any of that. Because my time is limited — if, on top of my comics and my life, I had to do other things as well, I don't think I could handle it. Even if all of it succeeded, I think that would still count as a failed life. Even with my newspaper strips, I have always drawn very few. I want to preserve my way of life as much as possible.

The truth is, I rarely go out. I haven't been to Shanghai in ten years. Most of the time I stay put, holed up at home, living the life I want to live — with my wife, with my cats. Because for me, that is my world, and it is enough.

![Taiwanese cartoonist Chu Teh-yung](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201803191528019nyFwrKdzbqeHu7l.jpg-w600)

**Q: So why did you agree to come out and serve as a judge for this comics competition this time?**

**Z:** My real hope is that more Chinese people will take part in competitions like this one.

When I was young, it was very hard to break through. There were only print media back then, and getting a newspaper or magazine to publish your work was extremely difficult. If your work wasn't good enough and you weren't famous, newspapers simply wouldn't use you. So in those days, every month I would go to bookstores and flip through magazines, and read the newspapers every day, looking for any comics competition — if there was one, I would hurry to enter it.

It seems that now, with the internet, there are endless platforms, and everyone seems to own their own media — anyone can run a WeChat account, anyone can run a Weibo account — so it looks like that problem (having no platform on which to publish) no longer exists. But in fact the situation is exactly the same as before: there are so many of them that you are just a single grain of sand, and no one can see you. So I think competitions of this kind really are a very good opportunity.

**Q: You also mentioned creativity at the competition's opening ceremony.**

**Z:** Right. More importantly, I think a comics competition like this can be used to get more Chinese people involved — to spur them to make their work because they want to enter a competition. I think creativity matters a great deal. Culture needs many people willing to become creators, because only when creators grow in number can a large body of work be created, and when that body of work passes through market selection and so on, some genuinely talented people may emerge. And once those talents emerge, they will, I believe, form the cultural strength of China in the future.

As for any other comics competition, I think everyone should enter as much as possible and seize these opportunities to break out on their own. It is a bit like looking for a job: if you really do your job well, you will naturally keep moving up in that company or organization.

### A Commercial Mechanism Is Burning Through Creators; Cartoonists Should Lead a Simple Life

**Q: How do you think comics are developing in China these days?**

**Z:** This is actually a rather absurd thing: I work in comics, yet I rarely read comics. So I don't really know how comics are developing on the Chinese mainland, and I'm not familiar with the new cartoonists who have emerged. What I do sense is a commercial mechanism. Many creators are easily sacrificed to platforms or to so-called investors.

**Q: What do you mean by "sacrificed"?**

**Z:** By "sacrificed" I mean this: say an investor today thinks you have potential, or you have just become a bit famous — he will absolutely never tell you to settle in and just draw what you want to draw, that as long as it is good enough, taking two or three years, even three or four, to put out a book is fine. I don't think anyone would do that. Most people will tell you: hurry up and publish, several books a year if possible, and then they will say, I'll let you turn this into a video game, I'll let you do all kinds of adaptations. That is strangling a creator by commercial means.

Some creators, given enough time, will keep getting better and better. They need others to give them that time to grow, they need room to breathe. But I think an excessively commercialized society won't give creators that kind of time. The approach it ends up taking is always to extract the maximum benefit from you, and it doesn't matter if you get sacrificed — as long as it gets enough benefit out of you. I think that is the biggest problem of an over-commercialized society: it burns a person out very quickly, and prematurely.

How to develop people is, I think, a very important question. The whole commercial process may develop some people, but at the same time it destroys many others. I find that genuinely regrettable. Commercialization is inevitable, but do the people who control these commercial operations ever have the thought that some things can wait?

![The four-panel comic *Everyone's Sick* (《大家都有病》). Source: Chu Teh-yung's Weibo account](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319152408zkgYxAPHeEfoTmcd.jpg-w600)

**Q: Why is it so hard to create interesting comics?**

**A:** Actually, this one is hard for me to answer, because for me drawing interesting comics isn't difficult. I usually spend a lot of time paying attention to this era, to the society I live in, to all the people I come into contact with — what feelings they give me — and in my work I draw everything I observe in daily life through humor. So creating is not difficult at all for me. In fact, for a long time I have been suppressing my creative drive; otherwise I would draw from morning to night. I have far too many things I want to draw — the subjects I could draw have piled up in my mind to at least five or six more books. If I drew them all, I would probably have no time left to live.

As for other people's situations, I can't answer that. I just feel that a creator must keep his life simple. When you're not famous, you should keep your life simple and channel as much of your energy as possible into your work. And once you are famous, you should keep your life even simpler — never spend too much time enjoying fame and fortune and then start living a life that disrupts your creative rhythm.

The truth is, everyone's mental energy and attention are limited, just like a human life — you can only do your best to spend your energy where you want to spend it.

### Print and the Internet Are the Same — What Matters Is Not Being Controlled by Superficial Forms

**Q: With print media in decline, your comics have moved to Facebook and Weibo. What impact has that had on you?**

**Z:** I think it's just a change of platform. It is a tool for a different platform that comes along with the times, and it is hard to say whether it is good or bad. I don't think any one of them should replace the others — they should all exist.

This is how things are for print media right now, but I think it's hard to say about the future. What I am doing myself is Facebook and Weibo. Some people say Weibo is done for, replaced by WeChat; others say Facebook is done for, replaced by Twitter and Instagram. Honestly, I think that is a commercial operation endlessly stimulating you, telling you this thing of yours is outdated and you need to use that new thing. About three years ago people told me I should set up WeChat and so on, and I couldn't be bothered. Because for me, I don't need to spend that much time on these things — if I spend a lot of time on them, my time for creating becomes less.

Back in the print-media days, I never drew many strips either — I always drew just one column, though various newspapers reprinted it. Now I update Facebook and Weibo, but for me it is still just one. I don't want to be controlled by any kind of form. Form is not something I care that much about.

And I think if I do Weibo well, it is enough to have just the group of people who like my Weibo — I don't need to attract more people. It's like food: if you like spicy food, eat spicy food; if you like sweet food, eat sweet food. I don't need to attract people of every taste. I think that approach (caring too much about form) goes against a creator's mentality — a creator should keep himself as simple as possible.

![Source: Chu Teh-yung's Weibo account](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319152850g7f2tDYwHe6czUJm.jpg-w600)

**Q: What changes do you see in young readers nowadays?**

**Z:** Maybe young people today aren't used to reading newspapers or magazines. But many things are hard to predict — just as, after e-books came out, physical bookstores began closing one after another, yet in many countries abroad physical bookstores are slowly making a comeback, because they provide a kind of occasion, a space that is completely different. I think human beings are like this: when something develops to an extreme, people will turn back to look for what was simplest and purest at the beginning.

When you read a newspaper or a magazine, there is a different sense of serenity. When you read on a phone or a computer, the very act is a visual stimulus. And when something emphasizes visual stimulation, other layers of it inevitably begin to weaken. As I often tell people: looking at a painting or a passage of text is completely different from watching a film or TV. When you watch TV or a movie, you have no control — your pace follows the pace it gives you. When I look at paintings, newspapers, magazines, and books, the pace is mine. Each has its pros and cons, and I don't think one should completely replace the other.

**Q: So it's about taking back control.**

**Z:** I think people today really need to control themselves and not be controlled by other things. Look at how everyone keeps swiping on their phones nonstop — I only use my phone when I need it. I started using Weibo around 2010, and even then I would post what I needed to post and look up what I needed to look up within about ten minutes a day, and then switch it off. For me, that saves a huge amount of "self time" — by which I mean time not controlled by other people.

I think people should have some control over, some mastery of, themselves — that is, I should decide what quality of life I want.

### Having Written About Love, Life, and Childhood, the Next Topic Is Imagination

**Q: Your earlier works took on many social topics — the new generation, women, children, consumer society. Is there a new topic you want to take on now?**

**Z:** Yes. In 2011 I drew *Everyone's Sick*, about modern people's lives, which included satirizing fashion. It seems no one has ever asked: what exactly is this thing called fashion? The entire fashion industry is actually controlled by a handful of fashion brands, and those brands are in turn controlled by a handful of designers. So think about it: across Asia, the people who might wear fashion brands probably number in the hundreds of millions, and they are controlled by just a few people — people who, at some meeting nobody knows about, decide what everyone will wear next year. And Asians dumbly put it on; wearing fashion is like wearing a uniform. When it comes to fashion, you have no autonomy, because your entire sense of beauty is Western.

Back in 2011 I was already reflecting on these things: we Chinese have started to have money, and apart from the benefits it brings, has that money also brought harms that may be double the benefits? Asians were done in by poverty once, and then done in again by affluence. I did a lot of satire on fashion, on mobile phones, and the like. Now it is 2018, and I don't think the situation has improved much.

![Chu Teh-yung's satire of fashion in his comics. Source: Chu Teh-yung's Weibo account](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20180319153228vOea8TqDs5PRQ4ho.jpg-w600)

What I hope to achieve with my comics is this: my views may not necessarily be right, but I look at this era through my own perspective — especially at Asians, or, narrowing the scope further, Chinese people, ethnic Chinese — what they have actually experienced in this era, and whether visible happiness or invisible happiness is the better one. I think this is something everyone needs to think about.

**Q: What will this year's forthcoming *Absolute Kid 3* mainly be about? After all, the last volume came out ten years ago.**

**Z:** Besides the conflict between the adult world and the child's world, what I actually want to talk about is imagination.

The economies of the whole world have fallen apart. The mainland's economy is still very good for now, but abroad, every country's economy is doing badly. So I think many existing ideas will begin to change, many professions will disappear, and the overall environment and the entire business model will inevitably go through enormous change. That is why imagination has become so, so important — with imagination, I think you can truly cope with the era to come. So what I want to draw in *Absolute Kid 3* is related to this.
