What is freestyle?

### 1

The first 8 Mile Underground battle was held on June 20, 2012, at a Xi'an nightclub called Guangquan.

The backdrop displayed their early signature logo: a black-and-white circle and five-pointed star, with the bold Chinese character for "street" in the center. The stage couldn't have been more makeshift—besides the propped-up turntables, there was barely enough room for one contestant on each side. The emcee shouted with effort and strain. The nightclub's hazy lights drifted in from behind, making it look like any ordinary club night. It wasn't until the contestants actually opened their mouths that the evening took on a hint of freestyle battle.

Seven years later, that first competition looks extremely crude. The person who handled the first 8 Mile Underground from start to finish was Nie Lei, a friend of Ye Nan's who also played hip-hop with him. Nie Lei gathered the hip-hop kids in Xi'an at the time and shot a promotional video that looked downright casual—the background music even drowned out some of the voices.

Nie Lei didn't promote it much, just posted information on Weibo and Renren and let word spread. On June 2, they sent out their first Weibo post. A day later, someone commented asking when and where the battle would be, and he only replied: mid-June, south Xi'an. Through word of mouth alone, roughly 100 people finally showed up at the door, each paying a 10-yuan ticket. Altogether it came to just enough to cover the venue fee of a little over 1,000 yuan.

The whole event had no schedule, no introductions, and no judges. Winners and losers were decided entirely by the crowd's cheers—whoever got the loudest response could stand till the end. Ye Nan thought the first 8 Mile Underground was "actually just a very small competition," and the original reason for holding it was simply that he and his friends had been caught up in work and life: "We had to find the music we loved; we couldn't let the fire in our hearts go out."

But only when placed in a longer time frame can people realize what that crude little competition meant. Among the fewer than 20 contestants was Ding Fei, who had already founded Red Flower Society and also helped Nie Lei organize 8 Mile Underground. The champion of that first edition was Bei Bei, also a Red Flower Society member.

### 2

Most people's current understanding of the freestyle battle that 8 Mile Underground represents is still stuck on Kris Wu constantly asking contestants on *The Rap of China*, "Can you freestyle?" But within hip-hop culture as a whole, freestyle carries enormous weight.

Born out of African American musical culture, hip-hop has from the start carried the functions of seeking freedom and resisting reality. The freestyle battle that emerged from this spirit—one-on-one improvised rap duels with aggressive, attacking lyrics—is the purest expression of that spirit.

Most Chinese rappers' awareness of freestyle comes from *8 Mile*, the semi-autobiographical film starring American rapper Eminem. The first time Xiao Qinglong watched the movie, he didn't even know what hip-hop music was, yet he was instantly hooked: "So that's how you can insult someone—so cool." Nie Lei and Ye Nan also watched the film and got the idea of holding a freestyle competition. "Why can't China have one? It definitely can." The name 8 Mile Underground, obviously, was borrowed from the film.

![*8 Mile*](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201904252333254Pt6NHFRb1Emy2Co.jpg-w600)

There were already similar competitions in China at the time. Dana Showtyme, who like Eminem came from Detroit, arrived in China in 1999 and walked into a nightclub flying the hiphop flag, only to see a Michael Jackson impersonation show. He wanted to bring the freestyle battle format to China, and two years later he held the first Iron Mic in Shanghai.

Although it remained a niche scene, the hip-hop circle was getting livelier. Around 2000, rap crews and labels began popping up in various places. In 2000, Yin Tsang was founded; among its members was Wang Bo, who would go on to win the first three Iron Mic championships. In 2002, Zhu Youren was founded in Shanghai; [rapper Lu1 once noted that Zhu Youren opened his eyes to rhyme schemes](http://www.qdaily.com/articles/45227.html).

Unlike American hip-hop culture, which spread early through mass media, freestyle became Chinese rappers' main channel for practice, exchange, learning, and creation.

"Back then, everyone had access to limited information, and ways to spread music were pretty restricted. There was no WeChat or Weibo yet; it was the BBS forum era. Not many people knew how to produce or record music. So it was freestyle." Xiao Laohu, a "senior" figure in the rap scene, also started out as a battle MC; he was a two-time champion of MC Battle: Dragon vs. Tiger.

"People connected through underground battles, close personal contact, small-circle sparring, and very real freestyle. It's not like today, when you write a song in your bedroom, add auto-tune or pick a hot track to make your own version, post it online, and shoot an MV, and maybe people know you. That's the current way. But in the past, you earned respect or got to know people through freestyle. Wherever you went, you battled, you rapped. That was about it."

And competitions gave rappers a further evaluation system, attracting them to join one after another and hone their craft. PG One took third place in a 2013 national battle competition. "I wasn't satisfied; I wanted to be number one and really do it right... When I practiced freestyle, I kept at it for half a year, training more than 10 hours a day. Anyone in that environment improves at freestyle. Even if you don't have talent, your brain accumulates a certain vocabulary, right?"

In 2015, Xiao Qinglong faced PG One in the 8 Mile Underground semifinals. The two went head-to-head, each trading improvised rebuttals based on what was happening on stage. In the end, Xiao Qinglong defeated PG One and became the champion.

### 3

According to Ye Nan, he ran 8 Mile Underground partly hoping to build a platform for promoting hip-hop from scratch, and partly hoping that freestyle battle would have a homegrown Chinese brand.

Growing up in a middle-class family, he fell in love with hip-hop music in middle and high school and began experimenting. To find beats and instrumentals, he would sit in internet cafés for entire afternoons. Later, he met a few local friends who also loved hip-hop through forum posts, and they formed a crew together.

Ye Nan's Luanzhan Men was one of Xi'an's earliest influential hip-hop labels. Pact, who later founded Nous Underground and appeared on *The Rap of China*, was also a member. In 2008, Luanzhan Men released its first album, *1506*, which included 18 complete tracks plus an intro and outro. The album notes read: "A new studio, a new starting point, from hobby to career, a new starting point, starting from zero."

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190425233707KnhT3CF8IHwfoQOl.JPG-w600)

However, rap at the time was still a niche existence, and rappers performing in bars and other venues had little income. One Luanzhan Men member's parent told their child that as long as they could earn 1,000 yuan a month, they wouldn't interfere with the hobby.

"Whether music was becoming niche or because of the national context, and the internet was still weak at the time, we didn't have our own promotional channels either. Many factors meant it was impossible to make it back then; no rapper was living well before 2010," Ye Nan said.

Against this backdrop, Ye Nan began thinking about corporate-style operations. In 2010 he founded OneTym Culture Communication Co., Ltd., covering artist packaging, music production, corporate planning, and film and television production. After the company made some money initially, it was immediately poured into the 8 Mile Underground events.

### 4

8 Mile Underground grew quickly.

"Before 8 Mile, Xi'an might have had three or four hiphop events a year. After 8 Mile, especially by 2014, Xi'an had three or four hiphop events a month," Ye Nan said.

As its influence grew, Ye Nan began receiving inquiries from hip-hop fans around the country: when would 8 Mile Underground come to their city? At first he hadn't planned to make 8 Mile Underground very big, but he started organizing events in different places. Later someone suggested bringing all the regional champions together to compete, and a nationwide contest was born.

Xiao Qinglong was the first champion after 8 Mile Underground expanded nationwide. By the time he competed, with contestants gathered from across the country, 8 Mile Underground already had authority comparable to Iron Mic.

Before competing in 8 Mile Underground, Xiao Qinglong made a lot of preparation. His first face-to-face battle was on the stage of Iron Mic Kunming in 2013. By then he had already been practicing freestyle battle for more than four years, but mainly through YY voice chat, with almost no in-person experience. Xiao Qinglong recalled that at his first offline battle, he was so nervous his legs shook and his movements were awkward; he was eliminated in the second round.

After that, Xiao Qinglong "went crazy entering competitions." The endless stream of competitions around the country gave him opportunities. He called that period "the golden age of Chinese battle"—before the 2014 Shanghai Iron Mic finals, he didn't even need to fly to competitions from Zhaotong, Yunnan, where he lived. He said he practiced freestyle two to three hours every day, sometimes battling people online for an hour at a stretch.

In early 2014, [Xiao Qinglong quit his job at the highway bureau](https://www.jiemian.com/article/1556633.html) and moved to Kunming. There he worked as a nightclub singer and a sales assistant, taking a few days off whenever there was a competition. By the time he competed in 8 Mile Underground in 2015, Xiao Qinglong was already a very mature battle MC. He excelled at counterattack, starting to flip his opponent's words the moment their round ended.

Standing on the 8 Mile Underground finals stage, Xiao Qinglong asked a staff member for a bottle of water, took a sip, and set it on the floor. He paced the stage with his head down, eyes not on his opponent, but listening and thinking about how to hit back.

The beat cut out. Xiao Qinglong took the mic. The instrumental for *California Love* came on, and just hearing that classic beat made the crowd cheer and wave to the rhythm. "I can use my dialect to throw some flow your way here, so you'll know my flow hits Yunnan rap heights." Xiao Qinglong opened his freestyle with two lines in Yunnan dialect, countering his opponent's claim that his freestyle had no flow. The crowd erupted in cheers even louder than before.

Whether it was the crowd's cheers during the battle or the final decision by volume, Xiao Qinglong got more sound than his opponent. And so Xiao Qinglong became the national champion of the second 8 Mile Underground competition.

"I was so excited—I actually did it. I'm the national champion." Four years later, recalling that freestyle battle, Xiao Qinglong said this. Although his tone was flat, the second half of his sentence gave away his excitement: "I feel that in my era, the national championship really meant something."

Xiao Qinglong is now signed to Banana Culture; just this March he released his first solo album, *He Cheng*.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190425233957vhW4ZO2ek5dQIM6n.JPG-w600)

"He came from the bottom, the hiphop dream, especially what he gained on the 8 Mile stage—the motivation and the biggest help it gave him at that stage—allowed him to today stand before the world as an artist," Ye Nan said when talking about 8 Mile Underground's influence on Xiao Qinglong.

Xiao Qinglong agrees that 8 Mile Underground brought him recognition, but his self-positioning is still the same as before: "rapper," not "artist." Even today, he says he still misses the combat feel of those battles: "When I got on stage, I wanted to slaughter everyone. Not very peace at all."

### 5

2017 was a turning point for 8 Mile Underground.

The competition had already emerged from the underground by then. It appeared in 22 cities including Taipei, with more than 1,000 registered contestants and joint sponsorship from more than 20 brands. Total live audiences surpassed 30,000. For a battle that started at 7 p.m., people were already lining up at 2 p.m. at Xi'an's Dahua 1935. The finals audience exceeded 2,000, Panda Live's simultaneous online viewers surpassed 1 million, and there were 60 security staff alone. The eventual champion, Bao Yin, won a 100,000-yuan prize.

In the process of expanding its territory, Ye Nan had a concern: if 8 Mile Underground events in other regions were hosted by local organizers, there might be rigging—Ye Nan saw referee influence as one reason Iron Mic's reputation and influence declined.

Unlike Iron Mic, 8 Mile Underground had no judges; the crowd's cheers were the sole criterion for victory. To make sure no local connections were involved, wherever 8 Mile Underground is held, the 8 Mile Underground team—including not just the organizer but also photographers, camera operators, and accommodation managers—goes there.

Another factor that changed 8 Mile Underground's circumstances was *The Rap of China*. The variety show, which dominated the summer, completely changed hip-hop music's underground status, bringing it into the mainstream along with terms like freestyle. Performance invitations and commercial contracts flooded in, bringing fame and fortune to rappers who had once been on the margins.

![](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190425234037YZoAeHB8jNGWwaDm.jpg-w600)

Xiao Qinglong also entered the mainstream spotlight through *TIME*, his collaboration with Hui Zi on the program. PG One was pushed onto a pedestal overnight. The changed environment also made freestyle battle events like 8 Mile Underground even hotter. When the 2017 8 Mile Underground was held, *The Rap of China* director Che Che and contestants MC Jin, Xiao Qinglong, Simba, and Zhuo Zhuo also expressed support for the event via video.

8 Mile Underground has now moved beyond the one-person-running-everything model. "We're now an organizing committee model, with ten people total," Ye Nan said. There are dedicated photographers, camera operators, people in charge of accommodation, booking flights and cars, "plus coordinators, stage managers."

The competition format has also changed. In addition to freestyle, a showcase round for produced tracks has been added. "We added this segment partly to see how the market responds and partly to see what kind of quality the rappers we encounter have."

### 6

"In real hiphop, only the MC battle survives. No matter how much money is thrown at you, however dope you are, one on one, face to face—if the MC battle is good, it's good; if it's not, it's not." [Pact said something along these lines on the Iron Mic stage](https://v.qq.com/detail/f/f79dyoyaj0k66wg.html), because freestyle is seen as a form that commerce can't manipulate. But more and more rappers are beginning to realize that freestyle, now that it has broken into the mainstream, is starting to change.

"I don't admit to being a rhyme-only MC; I think I'm a punchline MC," Xiao Qinglong said. In his view, many people today force rhymes for the sake of rhyming, making their lyrics formulaic and battles less free than before.

After *The Rap of China* aired, Xiao Laohu started shooting a series called [*Freestyle Is Dangerous*](http://www.sohu.com/a/195206397_500141). He wanted to use it as a freestyle experiment. The people appearing in the videos could be singers, producers, dancers, or even passers-by; Xiao Laohu himself appeared every time, needing to rap off the top of his head to the beat. The episodes varied—some gave prompt words, others used a Q&A format—but either way, it had to be freestyle, shot only once, no retakes.

"TV and media prefer polished, simple, symbolic things. But freestyle rejects all that—it taps your instinct while raising your technique, but most importantly, anytime, anywhere, only once, no repeats," he said.

Freestyle needs freedom, and that is precisely its greatest risk.

Xiao Qinglong and PG One's 2015 8 Mile Underground semifinal is still regarded by fans as a classic moment, but when people dug up the video, they angrily discovered that PG One had rapped a line, "I'll send you to meet Yao Beina." Offending the dead is taboo in traditional Chinese culture.

![8 Mile Underground necklace. Image from: 8 Mile Underground Weibo](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20190425234444Yci0ypWE7GRBxVQ6.jpg-w600)

That which traditional culture considers dirty in freestyle cannot be eradicated. In an 8 Mile Underground freestyle battle, two people attack each other. The attacks may include profanity and even personal insults, but improvising based on the atmosphere on the spot or throwing out a tit-for-tat punchline is what earns the most cheers.

On March 15, 2019, a few hours before the Chengdu stop of 8 Mile Underground, the organizers received a performance ban notice from the Ministry of Culture, even though before that they had already completed the six qualifications and five levels of approval required by the Ministry of Culture.

Three days later, [organizer Ye Nan announced via social media](https://weibo.com/5609390710/HlEQUibeO?from=page_1005051957553222_profile&wvr=6&mod=weibotime) that all 2018–19 season 8 Mile Underground competitions would be suspended.

Ye Nan said 8 Mile Underground would meet everyone again with new content and format. He said the new format had nothing to do with censorship pressure: "This is entirely because we know we have to make this thing more fun, more interesting, in line with both the audience and hiphop's own nature. We have to explore something new, which is innovation."

"In over 20 years of Chinese rap, which year has been easy?"
