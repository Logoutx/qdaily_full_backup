“When we seem to have nothing to do with mainstream values, are we in fact locked more firmly inside that system?”

A group of people are shooting a film in a gloomy old house. The female ghost who haunts the place takes her revenge, luring the protagonist with banknotes pouring forth toward the spot where he is about to be executed.

This scene from the film left a deep impression on Dai Jinhua. The film, called *The Lonely Spirit of the Black Building* (《黑楼孤魂》), was selected for an exhibition she recently co-curated — “Imagining Mainstream Values.”

“Within this spatial field, a historical moment appears. It is a moment of encounter, and also a moment of passing each other by.”

The exhibition officially opened on November 18 at the Inside-Out Art Museum in Beijing and runs until March of next year. The co-curator is Su Wei, a senior curator at the museum.

*The Lonely Spirit of the Black Building* is a genre film produced by Shenzhen Film Company in 1989, and the first horror film made in China more than a decade after the genre had been banned without any written decree.

The ghost in the film is a young girl who died unjustly during the Cultural Revolution, yet the means of seduction she employs is a flurry of crisp green U.S. dollars. On one side, the specter of history; on the other, capital and desire arriving from the outside world in the new era — the atmosphere of 1989 as a critical historical juncture was unexpectedly recorded in a somewhat clumsily made film, becoming an allegory in the process.

This is the first time Dai Jinhua has taken part in curating, and the exhibition dovetails almost seamlessly with her academic research on mass culture.

“Over these entire 40 years, China has been in a state of both vibrant vitality and lurking crisis, and the change has been so drastic that it's hard to say where the beginning is and where the end is. In the end, we chose the 1990s as a starting point,” she said in a lecture on opening day.

Dai Jinhua believes that amid rapid change, people find it hard to reach a consensus on “what mainstream values are.” The concept keeps shifting, and how each new “mainstream” comes into being is precisely what the exhibition wants to examine.

The collaboration between Su Wei and Dai Jinhua makes the whole presentation of the exhibition different from what one would expect to see in an art museum. On the one hand, it contains many elements of art, including works specially commissioned for this exhibition; on the other hand, it tries to conduct a profound discussion through a sorting-out of history. Beyond the exhibition itself, there are several lectures on “why mainstream values,” continuing through the end of December.

A clear timeline opens up the narrative across the museum's three floors, in seven chapters: “Prologue: The Lonely Spirit of the Black Building”; “The Other Shore: Small Theater, New Documentary, and Sixth Generation Cinema”; “Beside the Sunken Ship: Urban Cinema”; “Sex · Politics: Literature”; “The Split Surfaces: Debates in Intellectual History”; “Rearview Mirror: Legacy and Translation”; “Spotlight: Positions, Camps, and the Refinement of Questions,” and “Parallel Universes,” focusing on the cultural language and markers from the 1990s to the present.

Thirty artists (groups) and twenty-three works (groups) are placed together, but this is not entirely an art exhibition in the traditional sense; the forms throughout mix video, photography, oil painting, installation art, and a large quantity of archival documents.

The closer the time point to the present, the more diverse the forms become — for example, the third-floor gallery devoted to the topic of *Chinese Parents* uses three entire walls of commissioned art to reconstruct all the topics and discussions related to this realist game. At a set of school desks stacked with copies of *Five Years of Gaokao, Three Years of Simulation*, visitors can also try out the game on computers.

![Exhibition view of Chinese Parents. Source: Inside-Out Art Museum, Beijing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206190929RheCj0vc9XHPxuVD.jpg-w600)

The exhibition begins on the second floor.

“Decade” is one thread. Before reaching *The Lonely Spirit of the Black Building*, the right side of the first gallery concentrates images recorded by *Living Space*, a former sub-program of CCTV's *Oriental Horizon*, presented in photographs, while on the left wall plays *Mama* (1990), directed by Zhang Yuan — one of the earliest Chinese films shot in a documentary style.

Jiang Yue's *The Other Shore* and Wu Wenguang's *Bumming in Beijing* appear in the exhibition as representative works of the New Documentary Movement of the early 1990s. At the same time, they form a series of connections with the first program to appear, *Living Space*: Jiang Yue shot a series of short films for *The Other Shore* aimed at documenting ordinary people, and Lu Wangping, a producer of *Living Space*, served as cinematographer on *Bumming in Beijing*.

Although independently made, the three works share a common thread in their imagery: amid the rapid population mobility of the cities, the faces and living conditions of ordinary people were captured — something rarely seen in moving-image production at the time.

![Documentary The Other Shore / A Discussion on the Chinese Grammar of the Other Shore, Jiang Yue, 1995. Image source: Inside-Out Art Museum, Beijing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206212429lEJH8vCZqaLFcT4w.jpg-w600)

![Exhibition view. Source: Inside-Out Art Museum, Beijing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206212406qkabTeEJ3PfNmB7H.jpg-w600)

“Symbols,” or labels, are another thread of the exhibition.

In the chapter “The Split Surfaces,” the exhibition uses a large quantity of documents to show the topical debates that took place in the 1990s in academic journals led by the journal *Xueren* (《学人》). Among them, the debates between the “New Left” and the “liberal” camps in the mid-to-late 1990s are given prominent display.

The “Sex/Politics” section, meanwhile, excerpts a selection of representative cultural works, including Wang Xiaobo's *Golden Age*, Tie Ning's *The Rose Door* (《玫瑰门》), and Cui Zi'en's *Enter the Clowns* (《丑角登场》).

But beyond this, you can also see many artists' individual explorations, which leave viewers room for their own interpretation.

The Makeshift Theater workers' troupe, built by Zhao Chuan together with workers at Foxconn's Shenzhen plant, carried out joint creation and performances between 2016 and 2018. Besides performance videos and images, one wall of the exhibition displays the email exchanges from the troupe's workshops, revealing the living conditions of these workers as well.

In addition, *Father and Son*, a documentary by Wang Bing — the director of *West of the Tracks* (《铁西区》) — and a single frame of photography by Hao Jingban, frozen in the darkness on the night of the evictions, both reveal a close-up observation of, and humanistic care for, so-called socially disadvantaged groups.

![Film Father and Son, Wang Bing, 2014](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206192133gznJMfKvSyDI98lX.JPG-w600)

![Photograph Still Frame, Hao Jingban, 2018. Image source: Inside-Out Art Museum, Beijing.](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206194446lINcTE4VARnpri0a.jpg-w600)

The exhibition gives fairly clear markers for the cultural changes before the 21st century, but with the internet age, the sudden arrival of the vast amount of information it carries turned everything into a blurred mass, harder to distinguish.

Through commissioned works, the exhibition presents four projects. Among them, fan works culture and web series are both connected to so-called “subculture.”

The fan comics adapted from *Les Misérables* by fan creator Hu Wei, through a large number of original drawings plus the creator's own written reflections, explain some basic elements of fan works and danmei culture — such as character pairing (CP) and the concept of parallel universes (AU).

Hugo's French Revolution is transposed into the historical time and space of France's May '68. In her introduction, the author writes that she “tries to project the revolutionary theme of *Les Misérables* into contemporary political life.”

*Chinese Parents*, which appeared in 2018, is an RPG that has players raising the game's protagonist from birth all the way to the gaokao. The exhibition shows a survey about the game: a wall covered in bullet-screen-style discussions, from Tieba to Weibo, from sticker memes to lengthy commentary, mixed with references to social realities — such as the online group “Parents Are All Scourges,” which once appeared on the internet and was later shut down. The cultural phenomenon it reflects goes far beyond the design of the game itself.

Even a niche creative practice like danmei fan works, “circling the ground to amuse itself,” is endowed under the exhibition's theme with broader social significance and presented in a multifaceted way. This is not a scene one commonly finds in the white-box space of an art museum, but it injects a new reality-facing quality into the dialogue, with fewer platitudes.

The last work in the exhibition is a 1978 woodblock print, tucked in a corner of the gallery that is easily overlooked. A white egret looks this way and that against a dark background; it is called *No Way Home* (《不知归路》).

![Dai Jinhua at the exhibition](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206192259YmlZiB4y8eSocX0f.jpeg-w600)

Another noteworthy point is that the curatorial framework introduces a concept Dai Jinhua coined in her research on mass culture: “shared space.”

Dai Jinhua believes that in any space, if power is able to operate, it must mean that society and its members have reached a kind of tacit understanding and resonance within it. Looking back at Chinese society over the past 20-plus years, although mass culture has always been strongly influenced and controlled by state power, many new value judgments did not emerge from state power in the traditional sense; rather, they evolved out of marginal spaces and represent society's genuine will.

The seemingly disparate exhibits in the exhibition, each with its own theme and orientation, were all selected under this premise and all share certain characteristics as “shared space.”

But the form of an exhibition always has its limits. It is like a cultural-studies paper full of visual elements from which most of the text has been removed. As a viewer, from the exhibition alone it is hard to grasp the many thoughts hidden behind it, or the specific reasons many exhibits were chosen, without context.

We spoke with Dai Jinhua and Su Wei, hoping to provide a clearer frame of reference.

The following is compiled from a group interview by QDaily and several other media outlets, as well as the opening-day talk:

![Poster for “Imagining Mainstream Values.” Image source: Inside-Out Art Museum, Beijing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206173808mgkNctPA8CpYWbHw.jpg-w600)

**Dai = Dai Jinhua**

**Su = Su Wei**

**Q: From the 1990s to now, what changes have we seen in this so-called “shared space”? Has its scope expanded?**

**Dai:** When we say “shared space,” we are actually talking about gray zones. They often emerge amid fierce debate — for example, between the old and the new; or between those particularly obvious clichés and the popular language everyone bandies about on the street. It is actually the various cracks and fissures that appear in the process of change.

At moments of certain loss and chaos, you see new mainstreams growing out of this “shared space” — or sometimes it gets completely sealed off. So the points we chose this time look like a decade, but we are not actually focusing on the decade itself; we are focusing on a moment of fierce debate in Chinese society. Mainstream values are just a parameter, a coordinate, that helps us measure how social culture is formed and how its function operates.

**Su:** Professor Dai and I roughly reached a consensus on calling it the delayed space, or buffer space, of mainstream values — it is actually in there that things ferment again. What we can see clearly is that mass culture in China is a product of a power structure.

In the cases we just mentioned, this kind of digestive and delaying space exists. In today's culture, are the new things growing in these new spaces also carrying out a kind of imagining? Actually, while installing the exhibition, I carefully looked at the survey about *Chinese Parents*; I carefully read the very detailed discussions inside it. That cannot be divided by camps, cannot be divided by morality, and cannot be judged and identified by too many already-formed rules of reality. It actually requires us to regard it from the angle of “history in the making.”

![Exhibition view. Source: Inside-Out Art Museum, Beijing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/201812061946437yP9rfcpLlxFGwD0.jpg-w600)

**Q: A situation like the past few decades, where we may not be quite clear where mainstream values actually are — does this exist universally across cultures around the world, or do you think China has a certain uniqueness?**

**Dai:** I personally believe it is 100 percent Chinese characteristics. Because there is absolutely no precedent: a region, a country, experiencing such great development, such fast development, and such great social change in so short a time.

Throughout this, the state has always played the role of a ghost — sometimes a ghost, sometimes a flesh-and-blood presence. In the art world, in the 1980s the state's modernization demands and art's modernization demands were still aligned. Later, in the 1990s — actually the 1990s was an era of splitting and surfacing, when individuality began to awaken.

The individual and the individualistic of the 1980s were themselves collective nouns. Take individualism: it is a collective image, something that happened under the will of a nation waking up. In the 1990s, artistic creation began to become an individual affair, and this is an especially important thing. That is, it no longer revolves around a particular work, a certain philosophical question, or a certain social trend; the individual became the absolute foundation of creation.

**Su:** I think this point is especially important, because when we discuss this exhibition, when we discuss mainstream values, we inevitably talk about the state. Between the 1990s and today, the relationship between the individual and the ghost of the state has become dialogic, no longer that of an agent. It has even produced the possibility that I can, as a subject, choose to turn a blind eye to the spirit of the age, and to the direction the state demands. I keep a critical distance from you — this also began to appear in the 1990s.

**Q: Like the team behind *Living Space* you mentioned earlier — they did not belong to the mainstream themselves but gradually entered the mainstream's field of vision. Is that kind of transition still possible now?**

**Dai:** This is very hard to answer. Our current concrete state is, first, the space of possibility is shrinking — that is clear; second, new mechanisms have now appeared.

In roughly the past year or two, I spent some time and energy researching certain subcultural communities — for example, including the fujoshi community, or some danmei web novels, audio dramas, fan culture, comic conventions, doujinshi. I spent a bit of time and energy observing and understanding them, but the generation gap is right there; I will never claim to understand them best.

I did not expect it myself — it once again confirms my observation from the 1980s: you will observe, in the most marginal, most alternative, most offensive, or completely ignored cultural fields, a powerful construction and formation of the mainstream.

Danmei is such a subcultural, small-circle thing — how did it swell into such a large community of original creators and derivative merchandise, and then start spilling over through web series and audio dramas? I really don't exaggerate when I say that I think, relatively speaking, danmei stories will be more successful than romance stories, because this kind of story has instead become a kind of case of repairing intimate relationships. Look at how difficult, how accidental, yet how necessary and beautiful the relationships within are. I believe this kind of story's popularity is, in a sense, a fantasy manufactured in response to social scarcity. If it were more powerful, it could also become a kind of repair, something that reconnects social relationships. I use this example to say: this kind of extremely marginal or extremely niche thing may perhaps grow a certain mass character and social character.

And subcultures, compared with so-called mainstream or official culture, have more unscrupulous — or more productive — forms of expression: they will “instinctively” question certain value statements. I think this is what is interesting. It is entirely not the kind of official-versus-folk binary opposition we once imagined — entirely not that kind of state.

![Les Misérables fan comic, Hu Wei, 2016. Image source: Inside-Out Art Museum, Beijing](http://img.qdaily.com/uploads/20181206173952fgRFAZGPHdeQWcaU.jpg-w600)

**Q: In today's online environment, much of what seems to be mainstream information has actually been filtered. When what we see does not necessarily represent genuine mainstream values, how do we identify them?**

**Dai:** When it comes to these things, it is somewhat what this exhibition wants to shake up. I think our previous discussions of mainstream values focused too much on the level of power. Out of inertia we tend to feel that so-called mainstream values must be official values, or that official values must possess mainstream-ness, and so on.

Recognizing mainstream values is not a process of searching for and inducting patterns from cultural material; it is a process of constant reflective self-examination and constant questioning. And this process itself does not mean we convert to mainstream values — on the contrary, it means finding a relative position between it and me.

**Q: You mentioned in your earlier lecture that a person cannot survive entirely independent of mainstream values. Could you explain that further?**

**Dai:** Right. What we see now is actually unprecedented freedom — in a sense, I really do feel that individuals above the urban middle class possess unprecedented freedom, because we really can live independently without being lonely. But nobody has realized that when we can really possess this kind of self-contained, complete personal space, we are actually intensifying our socialization.

It starts from the most basic things — take the internet, then online shopping, then the global logistics system, then the stable order of society. It is not like a delivery man comes to the door and a killer walks in, right? You rely on the whole of this social system operating completely and smoothly. But people don't feel it; it is as if we believe this system is natural. So I say it is unprecedented: our government has public trust, our public is full of confidence in the system.

Although if you asked them about it, their expression might not be like this. But in fact, everyone believes it very much. For example, we all accepted online payment, mobile payment, without a second thought, never considering what a big deal this is. I saw an article in a German magazine with a passage saying: while the entire EU is still quarreling anxiously over whether to begin demonetization, China has already gone fully demonetized — yet nobody would ever think of the connection between paying by phone and demonetization.

**Q: That is to say, we individuals are very compliant with this overall social order?**

**Dai:** I think you can call it compliance, and you can call it delight. We don't actually feel anything oppressing us; we happily have new technological means serving us. Everyone simply accepts it as a service, a convenience, without feeling that this is a big systematic change.

How do we position ourselves in the face of mainstream values? When we seem to have nothing to do with mainstream values, are we locked more firmly inside that system by mainstream values? We can probably imagine the “shut-in” as the tree in Asimov's future world, with us as the beehive hanging from that tree.

In that sense, our relationship with the tree, and the relationship between our little cell and the other cells — this is, in a sense, something that the new global mainstream values try to make us ignore, yet cannot be ignored. There is probably no way to reflect this in the exhibition, but it is indeed one of our fundamental questions.

Young people in China once stood very high, and they saw quite a lot. Now, “the scholar needn't leave his door to know all that's happening under heaven” — yet everyone's attention and vision are instead cast very far, pushed far away from themselves. One does not actively sense the overall condition of society, or the shifts in social mainstream values; one's identification happens in a state of being swept along.
